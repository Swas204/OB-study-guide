Learning, Perception and Individual Decision-Making

The second individual-level unit, and it tells one continuous story: behaviour is acquired through learning, filtered through perception, and perception errors turn directly into decision errors. It closes on the two newest headings in the course, artificial intelligence and data in managerial decisions.

3.1Learning: Definition and Importance

Definition

Learning is any relatively permanent change in behaviour that occurs as a result of experience.

Robbins and Judge

Three elements in that definition, and each one excludes something:

  • Change: learning involves change, which may be for better or worse. Picking up a bad habit is also learning.
  • Relatively permanent: so temporary changes caused by fatigue, illness, alcohol or a one-off mood are not learning.
  • Through experience: so changes caused by physical maturation or injury are not learning. Experience may be direct or indirect, through observation or practice.
The point examiners test

Learning is inferred from behaviour, not from knowledge. An employee who has attended the safety training and can recite every rule, but still does not wear the helmet, has not learned. Knowing is not learning until behaviour changes. This distinction is the basis of every question about why training programmes fail.

Importance of learning in OB

01
Behaviour can be changed

If behaviour is learned, it can be unlearned and relearned. This is the entire premise of training, discipline and behaviour modification.

02
It explains individual differences

Two employees differ partly because of personality, and partly because of what each has been reinforced for in the past.

03
It is the basis of training and development

Every training design decision, spacing, feedback, practice, is a learning-principles decision.

04
It underpins socialisation and culture

New employees learn the norms of the organisation, which is how culture reproduces itself (Unit 5).

05
It explains habits, good and bad

Unsafe shortcuts, informal workarounds and corner-cutting are all learned behaviour that was reinforced by convenience.

06
It makes attitudes changeable

Since attitudes are learned, they can be modified, which links back to attitude change in Unit 2.

3.2Stimulus-Response Theory (Behaviourism)

The stimulus-response or behaviourist approach says learning is the formation of a connection between a stimulus and a response. It deliberately ignores what happens inside the mind, treating it as a black box, and studies only observable behaviour. It comes in two forms.

A. Classical conditioning

Ivan Pavlov, working with dogs. A neutral stimulus comes to produce a response it did not originally produce, by being repeatedly paired with a stimulus that does.

Example at work

A plant manager visits the shop floor only when a senior client or an inspection is expected, and each time the floor is scrubbed and machines are repainted. After two years, staff begin cleaning frantically the moment they hear the manager is coming, even for a routine visit.

The visit was the neutral stimulus. The inspection was the unconditioned stimulus. The cleaning is now a conditioned response to the visit itself. Note the managerial cost: he can no longer see the plant as it normally runs, because his arrival changes it.

The limitation to state

Classical conditioning explains reflexive and involuntary behaviour, the emotional and physiological reactions. It cannot explain the deliberate, voluntary behaviour that makes up most of work. A person's anxiety when the boss's name appears on the phone is classical conditioning. Their decision to answer it is not. For voluntary behaviour you need operant conditioning.

B. Operant conditioning

B. F. Skinner. Behaviour is a function of its consequences. People learn to behave in ways that get them something they want or let them avoid something they do not want. Voluntary behaviour is called operant because it operates on the environment to produce consequences.

The law of effect

Behaviour followed by a satisfying consequence is likely to be repeated. Behaviour followed by an unsatisfying consequence is less likely to be repeated.

Edward Thorndike
The mistake almost everyone makes

Negative reinforcement is not punishment. Negative reinforcement increases a behaviour by removing something unpleasant when it occurs. A manager who stops asking for daily updates once an employee starts submitting them on time has used negative reinforcement, and the on-time submission will continue. Getting this right in the exam is worth an easy mark, and getting it wrong is the most common error in the topic.

Schedules of reinforcement

ScheduleHow it worksEffect on behaviourExample
ContinuousReinforced every single timeFast learning, but rapid extinction once reinforcement stopsPraising a trainee for every correct entry in week one
Fixed intervalReinforced after a fixed time periodPerformance peaks just before the reinforcement and dips afterMonthly salary; the annual appraisal, which is why effort spikes in March
Variable intervalReinforced after varying time periodsSteady, consistent performance, because timing is unpredictableSurprise quality audits or unannounced site visits
Fixed ratioReinforced after a fixed number of responsesHigh and steady output, with a pause after each rewardPiece rate: ₹50 for every ten units
Variable ratioReinforced after a varying number of responsesHighest and most extinction-resistant. The behaviour persists for a long time without rewardCommission selling, where the number of calls before a sale is unpredictable. Also why gambling is so persistent
Exam tip

Variable schedules produce more persistent behaviour than fixed ones. The practical consequence is that unpredictable recognition sustains behaviour better than a predictable annual bonus, which mainly produces a spike in March. Say that and you have converted a list into an insight.

Organisational Behaviour Modification (OB Mod)

The applied version of operant conditioning, in five steps. Worth learning because it is a clean answer to "how would you change employee behaviour?"

  1. Identify the critical behaviours that make the biggest difference to performance.
  2. Measure the baseline frequency of those behaviours.
  3. Identify the existing contingencies: what currently reinforces the behaviour, including the wrong behaviour.
  4. Develop and apply an intervention strategy, changing the consequences.
  5. Evaluate performance improvement against the baseline.

3.3Cognitive Theory of Learning

The cognitive approach rejects the black-box assumption. It argues that learning involves internal mental processes: thinking, understanding, expectation, memory and insight. What is learned is not a stimulus-response bond but a cognitive map or an understanding of relationships.

BasisStimulus-Response (behaviourist)Cognitive
What is learnedA connection between stimulus and responseKnowledge, understanding, expectations, a mental map
Role of the mindIgnored as unobservableCentral to the explanation
Learner's rolePassive; shaped by consequencesActive; interprets, organises and makes meaning
MechanismReinforcementInsight, understanding, goal expectancy
Key namesPavlov, Thorndike, SkinnerEdward Tolman, Wolfgang Köhler, Jean Piaget
Best explainsHabits, reflexes, simple skill acquisition, disciplineProblem solving, understanding concepts, transfer to new situations

The contributing ideas

  • Tolman's cognitive maps and latent learning. Tolman's rats learned the layout of a maze even when unrewarded, and demonstrated that learning as soon as a reward was introduced. So learning can occur without any change in behaviour, which behaviourism cannot accommodate. He also introduced expectancy: learners form sign-significate relationships, a sense that this cue leads to that outcome.
  • Köhler's insight learning. His chimpanzees solved problems suddenly, by restructuring the situation mentally rather than by trial and error. The "aha" moment is a real phenomenon and not a gradual curve.
  • Information processing. Learning as encoding, storage and retrieval, with attention and working memory as limiting factors.
Example · why the distinction matters practically

Two ways to train a new accounts assistant on an expense policy.

Behaviourist: give her a checklist, correct her every time she errs, praise correct submissions. She becomes fast and accurate on the cases the checklist covers, and stops dead when a case appears that is not on it.

Cognitive: explain why the policy exists, what risk each rule prevents, and where the judgement lies. She is slower in week one, and in month three she handles a case nobody anticipated, correctly.

The behaviourist approach produces compliance. The cognitive approach produces transfer of learning, which is the ability to apply learning to a new situation. Routine work needs the first. Judgement work needs the second. Most training fails because it uses the first for a job that requires the second.

3.4Social Learning Theory

Definition

Social learning theory holds that people can learn through observation and direct experience, by watching what happens to other people and by being told about something, as well as by direct personal experience.

Albert Bandura. Later developed into Social Cognitive Theory

This is the most practically important learning theory for management, because it explains how most workplace behaviour is actually acquired: by watching. It is an extension of operant conditioning that accepts the existence of cognition, so it sits between the two previous theories.

The central mechanism is vicarious reinforcement: I do not need to be rewarded myself, I only need to see someone else rewarded.

Self-efficacy, Bandura's other major contribution, belongs here: the belief that one can perform a task. It is built through four sources, in descending order of power: enactive mastery (having done it before), vicarious modelling (seeing someone like you do it), verbal persuasion (being told you can), and arousal (an energised state). This explains why watching a peer succeed motivates far better than a manager's encouragement.

Example · the modelling a manager did not intend

A firm issues a policy that timesheets must be filled daily and accurately. The senior manager fills his own weekly, backdated and rounded, and says openly that it is "a formality for billing".

Every junior in that team has now learned the real policy, through all four processes: they attended to the most important available model, they remembered it, they can easily reproduce it, and it is reinforced because nothing happens to him. The written policy was never the teacher. He was.

This is the reason "tone at the top" is not a slogan. In social learning terms, senior behaviour is the highest-attention model in the organisation, so it overrides written rules automatically.

Managerial applications of social learning
  • Behaviour modelling in training: demonstrate, let the trainee practise, give feedback. More effective than lecturing.
  • Mentoring and buddy systems, which work because the model is similar and available.
  • Leaders as models, deliberately, on the behaviours that matter most, including ethics and safety.
  • Visible consequences. A rule enforced once, visibly, teaches more than the same rule circulated five times.
  • Choosing who to promote is a teaching act, because it tells everyone which behaviour gets rewarded.

3.5Principles of Learning

These are the conditions under which learning actually happens, and they are the design rules for any training programme.

PrincipleWhat it meansApplied
1. Motivation and readinessThe learner must want to learn and be ready for it. Without motive there is no attentionExplain what is in it for the trainee before the content, not after
2. ReinforcementBehaviour that is rewarded is repeated. The strongest single principleRecognise the new behaviour in the first week, when it is most fragile
3. Feedback / knowledge of resultsLearners must know how they are doing. Immediate, specific feedback beats delayed, general feedbackCorrect an error in the moment, not in the annual appraisal
4. Practice and repetitionSkills need doing, not hearing. Repetition builds retentionA hands-on session beats a presentation for any procedural skill
5. Spaced versus massed learningSpaced learning, distributed over time, produces better retention than massed learning, crammed into one block, though massed is faster in the short termFour one-hour sessions over four weeks beat one four-hour session. This also applies to your own exam revision
6. Whole versus part learningComplex tasks are better learned by breaking them into parts; simple and highly integrated tasks are better learned wholeTeach a long reconciliation in stages; teach a short entry as one flow
7. Active participationDoing produces better learning than passive receivingCase discussion and role play over lecture
8. Meaningfulness of materialContent connected to what the learner already knows and to their real work is retained far betterUse the firm's own examples, not generic ones
9. Transfer of learningLearning is only valuable if it moves to the actual job. Requires similarity between training and work conditions, and supervisor support afterwardsTrain on the software the employee will actually use; brief the supervisor to reinforce it

11. Primacy and recencyWhat comes first and last in a session is remembered bestPut the critical message at the start and repeat it at the close
The single point most worth making

Transfer of learning is where most corporate training fails, and the cause is usually the supervisor rather than the trainer. An employee returns from a two-day workshop to a manager who says "forget that, do it the way we always do". The training was effective and the learning was extinguished within a week. In reinforcement terms, the old behaviour is still the reinforced one.

3.6Shaping as a Managerial Tool

Definition

Shaping behaviour means systematically reinforcing each successive step that moves an individual closer to the desired response.

The logic: if you wait for the complete desired behaviour before reinforcing anything, you may wait forever, because the behaviour is too far from what the person currently does. So you reinforce successive approximations, and progressively raise the standard.

The four methods of shaping

These are the four operant consequences from 3.2, now used deliberately as a management tool.

01
Positive reinforcement

Follow the desired behaviour with something pleasant. The most effective method, and the most under-used.

02
Negative reinforcement

Withdraw something unpleasant when the desired behaviour occurs. Effective, but it teaches avoidance rather than commitment.

03
Punishment

Apply an unpleasant consequence to an undesired behaviour. Fast, but with side effects, see below.

04
Extinction

Withhold reinforcement so the behaviour fades. Useful for behaviour that is being accidentally rewarded by attention.

Why punishment is the weakest of the four
  • It suppresses behaviour only while the punisher is present, so it requires permanent surveillance.
  • It teaches what not to do without teaching what to do instead.
  • It produces fear, resentment and concealment. Punishing error reports does not reduce errors, it reduces reports, which is the psychological safety point from Unit 1 arriving here from another direction.
  • It generates negative emotional associations with the punisher and the workplace, which is classical conditioning at work.
  • It can inadvertently reinforce the behaviour if the attention is itself rewarding.

The rule for an answer: use positive reinforcement to build behaviour, extinction to remove accidentally rewarded behaviour, and reserve punishment for serious and clear violations where deterrence is the point.

Example · shaping a reluctant presenter

An analyst is technically excellent and refuses to present to clients. Telling her to "be more confident" achieves nothing, because the target behaviour is far outside her current range.

Shaping instead: week 1, she presents one slide in an internal team meeting, and is thanked specifically for the clarity of it. Week 3, she presents her whole section internally. Week 6, she answers one question in a client call. Week 10, she presents one section to the client with her manager present. Week 16, she runs the update alone.

Each step was reinforced, each was only slightly beyond the last, and her self-efficacy was built through enactive mastery, which is the strongest source. Note that nothing about her personality was changed.

3.7Learning for Green Behaviour

This heading asks you to apply the three learning theories to environmental behaviour. The exam value is that it lets you use everything above rather than recite new content.

Two extra concepts worth naming here
  • Habit formation. Most green behaviour is repeated, low-attention behaviour, so the goal is a habit rather than a decision. Habits form through consistent cue, action and reward, which is why changing the physical cue, such as bin placement, outperforms changing the attitude.
  • Feedback loops. Real-time consumption feedback, for example a visible energy meter or a weekly waste figure per team, is one of the most reliably effective environmental interventions there is, precisely because it supplies the missing learning principle: knowledge of results.

3.8Perception and the Factors Influencing It

Definition

Perception is a process by which individuals organise and interpret their sensory impressions in order to give meaning to their environment.

Robbins and Judge
The most important sentence in this unit

What we perceive can be substantially different from objective reality, and behaviour is based on perception, not on reality. People do not respond to the world. They respond to their interpretation of it. So a manager managing "the facts" is managing something no employee has access to.

A. Factors in the perceiver

  • Attitudes: two interviewers, one who believes women are unsuited to field sales and one who does not, will hear the same candidate differently.
  • Motives: unsatisfied needs stimulate and strongly influence perception. An insecure employee reads a neutral email from the boss as a threat.
  • Interests: we notice what concerns us. An auditor notices the missing approval; a designer notices the layout of the same page.
  • Experience: past experience can make us notice things others miss, and can also blind us. An experienced manager may stop seeing a problem because it has always been there.
  • Expectations: we see what we expect to see, which is the mechanism behind stereotyping and the self-fulfilling prophecy.
  • Mood: from Unit 2, mood colours interpretation. The same feedback lands differently on a good and a bad day.

B. Factors in the target

Targets are not perceived in isolation, so the relationship between a target and its background matters. Novelty, motion, sounds, size, background, proximity and similarity all affect what stands out. Because we tend to group nearby or similar things together, people are often judged by the group they are standing in rather than individually.

C. Factors in the situation

Time, work setting and social setting. The same person in a formal boardroom and at a team dinner is perceived quite differently, and the same suggestion made in a crisis and in a routine review is received quite differently.

Example · all three at once

A junior raises a data-quality concern in a Monday review.

Perceiver: the manager is under pressure on a deadline and in a poor mood, and expects juniors to raise minor issues. Target: the junior is soft-spoken, new, and has raised two issues previously that turned out to be small. Situation: it is the last five minutes of an overrunning meeting with a client call next.

The concern is dismissed. Nothing about its merit entered the decision. Three weeks later it surfaces as a restatement. Perception, not information, determined the outcome, and this is exactly the bridge to section 3.11.

3.9Attribution Theory

Definition

Attribution theory explains how we judge people differently depending on the meaning we attribute to a given behaviour. It attempts to determine whether an individual's behaviour is internally or externally caused.

Developed by Fritz Heider and formalised by Harold Kelley
  • Internally caused behaviour is believed to be under the personal control of the individual: ability, effort, attitude.
  • Externally caused behaviour is believed to result from outside causes: the situation, luck, other people, the system.

Kelley said we use three pieces of information to decide which it is.

Example · running the three tests

An employee arrives late to today's meeting. Is she unreliable, or was there a reason?

  • Distinctiveness: is she late for everything, or only for meetings? If she is punctual for everything else, that is high distinctiveness, so look external.
  • Consensus: was everyone else late too? If four of six people were late, that is high consensus, so look external, probably the traffic or the schedule.
  • Consistency: is she late every week? High consistency points internal.

Low distinctiveness, low consensus and high consistency together give a confident internal attribution. Any other combination should make a manager pause, and the practical value of the theory is exactly that pause.

The two errors that distort every attribution

When judging others
Fundamental attribution error
  • The tendency to underestimate situational influences and overestimate personal ones when judging others' behaviour.
  • A colleague misses a deadline, so we conclude he is careless, not that his data arrived two days late.
  • Consequence: managers over-diagnose attitude problems and under-diagnose broken processes, then "fix" the person.
When judging ourselves
Self-serving bias
  • The tendency to attribute our own successes to internal factors and our own failures to external factors.
  • "I closed that deal" but "the market killed that one."
  • Consequence: appraisal discussions where employee and manager sincerely disagree about the same result, and neither is lying.
Put the two together

I explain my failure by the situation and your failure by your character. You do exactly the same in reverse. Both of us are being honest. This single mechanism explains a very large share of workplace conflict, unfair appraisals, and the reason "performance conversations" go badly. It also connects to locus of control in Unit 2: an accurate attribution is healthier than either a habitually internal or habitually external one.

3.10Common Shortcuts in Judging People

Judging people is effortful, so we use shortcuts. They are efficient and frequently accurate, which is why we keep them, and they are the source of systematic error. This is a guaranteed question, so learn every one with an example.

ShortcutMeaningExample
Selective perceptionBecause we cannot take in everything, we selectively interpret what we see based on our own interests, background and attitudes. We see what we are looking forIn a review of a failed launch, marketing sees a product problem and product sees a marketing problem. Both attended the same review
Halo effectDrawing a general impression on the basis of a single favourable characteristicAn articulate candidate is assumed also to be analytical, organised and a good team player. Only one of those was observed
Horn effectThe reverse: one negative characteristic colours the whole impressionOne missed deadline in March, and every later contribution is read as unreliable
Contrast effectEvaluating someone by comparison with others recently encountered rather than against a standardAn average candidate interviewed after two poor ones is rated as strong. The order of interviews changed the outcome
ProjectionAttributing one's own characteristics and motives to other peopleAn ambitious manager assumes everyone wants a promotion, and is baffled when an employee declines one
StereotypingJudging someone on the basis of one's perception of the group to which they belong"Engineers cannot write", "she has small children so she will not travel"
Primacy / first impressionEarly information carries disproportionate weight, and later information is interpreted to fit itA judgement formed in the first four minutes of an interview that the remaining forty minutes only confirms
RecencyThe most recent behaviour dominates the judgementAn annual appraisal that mostly reflects the last six weeks
Similar-to-me effectRating more favourably those who resemble us in background, college, region or interestsHiring the candidate from your own university. Diversity dies here, quietly and with good intentions
Central tendencyRating everyone in the middle of the scale to avoid difficult conversationsAn appraisal sheet where the whole team scores 3 out of 5, which conveys no information at all
Illusory correlationPerceiving a relationship between two things where none existsBelieving mood depends on the weather, from Unit 2

Specific applications in organisations

01
Employment interview

Unstructured interviews are dominated by primacy, halo, contrast and similar-to-me, and different interviewers routinely reach different conclusions about the same candidate. Structured interviews exist to suppress exactly these shortcuts.

02
Performance expectations

The self-fulfilling prophecy or Pygmalion effect: people behave in ways consistent with how they are expected to behave. A manager who believes an employee is weak assigns easier work, gives less feedback and less exposure, and the employee duly develops less. The expectation created the evidence.

03
Performance evaluation

Appraisals depend heavily on the evaluator's perception, particularly where judgement rather than measurement is involved. Every shortcut above is an appraisal error waiting to happen.

How to answer "how can these errors be reduced?"
  • Structure the judgement: same questions, defined criteria, scoring against a standard rather than against other candidates.
  • Use multiple raters, since individual biases partly cancel out.
  • Keep records through the year to defeat recency.
  • Train evaluators to recognise the specific errors by name, which measurably reduces them.
  • Evaluate behaviour, not traits. "Submitted all twelve monthly reports on time" cannot be haloed; "is dependable" can.
  • Separate observation from evaluation in time, and require written justification for extreme or vague ratings.

3.11The Link Between Perception and Individual Decision-Making

This heading exists to make one argument, and it is the hinge of the whole unit.

The argument

Decisions are reactions to problems. A problem exists when there is a discrepancy between the current state and some desired state, which requires someone to perceive that discrepancy. Therefore every decision is built on perception, and inherits every perceptual error made along the way. One person's problem is another person's satisfactory state of affairs, and both are looking at the same data.

Example

Attrition in a department reaches 30 per cent. The HR head perceives a compensation problem, because that is the lever she owns and the exit data she reads is the salary comparison field. The department head perceives a hiring-quality problem, because his experience is that the last four joiners were weak. The finance head perceives no problem at all, because the wage bill has fallen.

Three decisions will be proposed, all defensible, and the actual cause, an abusive supervisor that nobody has been told about, appears in none of them, because nobody perceived it and therefore nobody is even choosing against it.

3.12Decision-Making in Organisations

A. The rational decision-making model

A rational decision is one that is consistent and value-maximising within specified constraints. The model has six steps.

B. Bounded rationality

Definition

Bounded rationality is the process of making decisions by constructing simplified models that extract the essential features of a problem without capturing all of its complexity.

Herbert Simon, who received the Nobel Prize in Economics for this idea

Because human information-processing capacity is limited, people cannot analyse all information about all alternatives. So they satisfice: they search for a solution that is satisfactory and sufficient, and take the first acceptable one rather than the optimal one.

Example

Looking for a job, a graduate does not evaluate every employer in India against weighted criteria. She applies to the ones she has heard of, in cities she knows, and accepts the first offer that clears her minimum bar on salary, location and role. That is satisficing, and it is not a failure of intelligence. It is the only available strategy given time, information and attention limits.

C. Intuitive decision-making

Definition

Intuitive decision-making is an unconscious process created out of distilled experience.

It operates outside conscious thought, relies on whole-pattern associations, is fast, and is affectively charged, meaning emotions are involved. The honest position: intuition is compressed expertise, so it is reliable where the person has had long experience in a domain with quick, clear feedback, for example a trader or an experienced surgeon. It is unreliable in novel or slow-feedback domains, where it is simply bias wearing the clothes of experience. The practical rule is to use intuition to generate hypotheses and analysis to test them, and never to rely on intuition alone for a decision that can be analysed.

D. Common biases and errors in decision-making

BiasMeaningExample
Overconfidence biasWe are more confident in our judgements than accuracy warrants. Confidence is highest where competence is lowestA first-time entrepreneur's revenue projection for year one
Anchoring biasFixating on initial information and failing to adjust adequately for later informationThe first salary figure named in a negotiation sets the range for everything that follows
Confirmation biasSeeking out information that reaffirms past choices and discounting information that contradicts themA manager convinced a hire is weak notices every error and reads every success as luck
Availability biasJudging on the information most readily available, typically the vivid and the recentOverestimating the risk of a plane crash after a news report, while ignoring road risk
Escalation of commitmentIncreasing commitment to a failing course of action because it is our decisionPouring another ₹2 crore into a project already ₹5 crore over budget, because abandoning it admits the first ₹5 crore was wasted. E. Individual differences and organisational constraints

Individual differences
Who is deciding
  • Personality: conscientiousness relates to escalation of commitment, since achievement-striving people dislike abandoning a goal. High self-esteem relates to self-serving bias.
  • Gender: research finds women are more likely to ruminate, that is to reflect at length on a decision after it is made, which deepens analysis and also increases regret and stress.
  • Mental ability: higher ability helps people avoid some biases, but does not protect against anchoring, overconfidence or escalation, which is a striking and examinable finding.
  • Cultural background: affects time horizon, tolerance of ambiguity, and whether decisions are expected to be individual or consultative.
Organisational constraints
Where they are deciding
  • Performance evaluation: managers decide in line with what they are measured on.
  • Reward systems: the reward system tells people which decisions are preferred, and it overrides the mission statement every time.
  • Formal regulations: rules limit and pre-shape the choice set.
  • System-imposed time constraints: almost all decisions have deadlines, which forces satisficing.
  • Historical precedents: decisions are not made in isolation; past commitments constrain present options, which is how escalation becomes institutional.

3.13Ethics in Decision-Making

The three ethical decision criteria

CriterionThe test it appliesStrengthWeakness
UtilitarianismDecisions are made solely on the basis of outcomes, seeking the greatest good for the greatest numberPromotes efficiency and productivity; consistent with profit maximisation, so it dominates business thinkingCan ignore the rights of a minority. A decision that benefits 90 employees and destroys 10 passes the test
RightsDecisions must respect and protect the basic rights of individuals: privacy, free speech, due processProtects individuals from harm; it is the basis of whistle-blower protectionCan obstruct high productivity and efficiency, and rights can conflict with one another
JusticeRules must be imposed and enforced fairly and impartially, so benefits and costs are distributed equitablyProtects the underrepresented and those with less powerCan encourage a sense of entitlement and reduce risk-taking and innovation
Example · the three criteria on one decision

A firm must cut costs by 15 per cent. It can lay off 40 people from one plant, or cut everyone's pay by 8 per cent.

  • Utilitarian: whichever produces the greater total good. Probably the layoff, since 800 people keep full pay and the firm stays viable.
  • Rights: were those 40 given notice, a fair process, a hearing, and the statutory entitlements? The process is as much the ethical question as the outcome.
  • Justice: are the 40 disproportionately older, or from one community, or those who once raised complaints? And is senior management taking a cut too?

The three criteria do not agree, which is precisely why this is an ethical dilemma and not an arithmetic problem. A good answer states that the criteria conflict, rather than pretending one settles it.

Behavioural ethics and moral development

Behavioural ethics is the study of how people actually behave when confronted with ethical dilemmas, as opposed to how they say they would. The consistent finding is a large gap between stated values and actual conduct, and the main explanations are already in your syllabus: cognitive dissonance and rationalisation (Unit 2), conformity and obedience (Unit 4), low psychological safety (Unit 1) and reward systems that pay for the outcome and not the method (above).

Moral intensity · why some issues get attention and others do not

Six factors make an issue feel ethically pressing: the magnitude of the consequences, the social consensus that it is wrong, the probability of harm, the temporal immediacy of it, the proximity of the victims, and the concentration of the effect.

This explains a great deal. Environmental harm scores low on almost every factor, since the consequences are distant in time, diffuse across many people, probabilistic, and the victims are not in the room. That is not indifference. It is low moral intensity, and it is why sustainability requires structural support rather than appeals to conscience, which is the point made in Unit 1 now given its mechanism.

Improving ethical decision-making

  • Codes of conduct that are specific about real situations, not aspirational language.
  • Ethical leadership and visible modelling, which is social learning from 3.4 and the single strongest lever.
  • Align reward systems with the method and not only the result, since measuring only outcomes licenses whatever produces them.
  • Protected channels for voice, and a whistle-blower mechanism that works (Unit 2 Part B).
  • Ethics training using real dilemmas from the firm's own operations.
  • Raise moral intensity deliberately: make distant consequences concrete and name the people affected.

3.14Artificial Intelligence in Managerial Decision-Making

Why this heading matters for your exam

Along with technostress and green OB, this is one of the newest headings in the course and is not covered by the prescribed textbooks. It is a likely source of a fresh question, and the framing below, augmentation versus automation, is the one that will read as informed rather than general.

The three ways AI enters a decision

What AI genuinely improves

  • Consistency. A model applies the same rule at 4 pm on Friday as at 10 am on Monday. Human decisions vary with mood, hunger and fatigue, an effect called noise, and this is AI's strongest and least-discussed advantage.
  • Scale and speed on decisions no human volume could cover.
  • Pattern detection in data too large or too high-dimensional for a person.
  • Reduction of some human biases, including recency, halo, contrast and similar-to-me, because the model does not see the candidate's face or remember last week.
  • Making criteria explicit. Building a model forces an organisation to state what it actually values, which is often the most useful part of the exercise.

The risks, and this is where the marks are

RiskWhat goes wrongExample
Algorithmic biasA model trained on historical decisions learns historical discrimination and applies it at scale, with the appearance of objectivityA recruitment model trained on ten years of hiring at a male-dominated firm learns to downgrade women's resumes. It was not programmed to discriminate; it learned what the firm rewarded
Opacity / black boxNobody can explain why a particular output was produced, so it cannot be justified to the person affected or to a regulatorA loan rejection with no explainable reason. Note this may breach the requirement to give reasons in many regulatory settings
Automation biasHumans over-trust the machine's output and stop exercising independent judgement, so the human in the loop becomes a rubber stampA credit officer approving a score she privately doubts, because overriding requires justification and agreeing does not
Accountability gapWhen the model is wrong, responsibility is diffused between vendor, data team, and manager, so nobody owns the outcome"The system decided" is not an answer available to a director under Indian company law
Data quality and proxiesModels optimise the measurable proxy, not the real goal, and inherit every defect in the dataUsing healthcare spending as a proxy for healthcare need, which understates need in groups that historically received less care
DeskillingIf the model makes the judgements, the humans stop developing judgement, so there is nobody competent to override it in five yearsAnalysts who can run the tool but cannot evaluate whether its answer is sensible
Narrowing of optionsThe model can only rank the alternatives it was given, so it cannot produce the option nobody thought ofConnects directly to 3.11: unseen alternatives are absent rather than rejected
False confidenceQuantified output feels more authoritative than a human estimate carrying the same uncertainty, so overconfidence bias is amplified rather than reducedA forecast with three decimal places, built on assumptions nobody re-examined
The point that separates a strong answer

AI does not remove bias from decisions. It relocates it, from many individual decision makers, where biases are inconsistent and partly cancel out, to a single model, where the bias is consistent, invisible and applied at scale. A biased interviewer harms the candidates she meets. A biased model harms every candidate, identically, forever, and produces an audit trail that looks objective.

Governing AI in decisions

  1. Match the mode to the decision. Automate the repetitive and reversible; augment the consequential.
  2. Keep a human in the loop with real authority, plus the time and information to use it. A human who cannot override is a formality.
  3. Demand explainability proportionate to the stakes, and the ability to give the affected person a reason.
  4. Audit outcomes for disparate impact across gender, age, caste, region and disability, regularly and not once at launch.
  5. Assign a named owner for every consequential model, so accountability does not diffuse.
  6. Provide appeal to a person, which addresses both the ethical and the technostress problem from Unit 2.
  7. Protect human judgement deliberately, by keeping people involved in enough real decisions to stay competent.
  8. Be transparent with employees about what is measured and how it is used.

3.15Data-Driven Decision-Making

Definition

Data-driven decision-making is the practice of basing decisions on the analysis of data rather than on intuition, precedent or hierarchy. In OB the closely related term is evidence-based management: basing managerial decisions on the best available scientific evidence.

The argument for it follows directly from 3.10 and 3.12. If human judgement is systematically distorted by selective perception, halo, recency, anchoring and overconfidence, then data is a discipline that constrains those errors. Robbins's case for evidence-based management is exactly this.

Applications in OB, usually called HR or people analytics

  • Selection: which selection criteria actually predict performance in this firm, rather than which ones sound sensible.
  • Attrition: identifying which factors precede resignation, and for whom.
  • Engagement: linking survey results to business outcomes such as customer satisfaction or safety incidents.
  • Diversity: finding at which stage of the funnel under-represented candidates are actually dropping out, which almost always contradicts the assumption.
  • Training: measuring transfer to the job rather than satisfaction with the session.
  • Pay equity: testing for unexplained gaps after controlling for role, level and tenure.

The pitfalls, which are as examinable as the benefits

PitfallMeaningExample
Correlation is not causationTwo things moving together does not mean one causes the otherEmployees who attend the leadership programme get promoted more. It may be that the firm selects likely promotees for the programme. Reversing the causation reverses the decision
Goodhart's law / metric fixationWhen a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure, because people optimise the metric rather than the goalMeasuring support agents on call handling time produces short calls and unresolved problems, which is exactly the Unit 2 Part C case
Survivorship biasAnalysing only the cases that remained visibleStudying the traits of your current high performers tells you nothing about the people who would have succeeded and were never hired
Garbage in, garbage outAnalysis inherits every defect in the underlying dataAppraisal data distorted by central tendency and halo cannot support a valid performance model, however sophisticated the model
What is measurable displaces what mattersQuantifiable variables crowd out important unquantifiable onesMentoring, trust and psychological safety are hard to count, so they fall out of the model and then out of the decision
Data theatreData collected and presented to justify a decision already taken, which is confirmation bias with a dashboardCommissioning an engagement survey and acting only on the findings that support the existing plan
Privacy and consentEmployee monitoring data raises rights questions under the justice and rights criteria in 3.13, and under India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023Keystroke and location monitoring used for appraisal without disclosure
The balanced conclusion

Data-driven decision-making is a corrective to perceptual and cognitive bias, not a replacement for judgement. It is strongest at diagnosis, telling you what is actually happening rather than what you assumed, and weakest at prescription, because choosing what to optimise is a values decision that no dataset can make. The mature position, and the one to write: data should decide what is true, and people should decide what is important.

Case study practice · Section C style

A mid-sized NBFC introduces an AI model to score personal loan applications, trained on eight years of its own approval and default history. Approvals become three times faster and the reported default rate falls.

Nine months in: a credit officer, Farida, notices that applications from two particular districts are rejected at an unusually high rate, including some she considers sound. She raises it with her manager, who replies that the model is "data-driven, so it has no bias", and points out that her own approval override rate is already the highest in the team, which is a metric on her scorecard. She stops overriding.

Separately, a customer complaint reaches the regulator asking for the reason for rejection. Nobody in the firm can produce one. The head of credit tells the board the model is "97 per cent accurate" and the matter is a one-off. Two senior officers have meanwhile asked to move to another function, saying they now "just press approve".

(a) Identify the learning, perception, decision-making and ethical concepts at work. (b) Why did the organisation fail to catch the problem? (c) What should it do?

Answer skeleton for the case

(a) The concepts. Algorithmic bias: the model trained on eight years of the firm's own decisions has learned its historical district-level discrimination and now applies it consistently and at scale, with the appearance of objectivity. Automation bias: officers over-trusting the output. Reinforcement and shaping, used destructively: Farida's override was punished through the scorecard metric while agreeing carried no cost, so the firm shaped exactly the behaviour it did not want, and her giving up is extinction of the one control that was working. Social learning: the two senior officers' exit and the manager's response teach everyone else that raising this is pointless. Confirmation bias and the appeal to "97 per cent accuracy", which is a base-rate problem, since a high overall accuracy is compatible with severe error concentrated in a small group. Opacity: the inability to state a reason. Deskilling: "we just press approve". Selective perception: only Farida perceived a problem, because only she saw individual files; the head of credit saw an aggregate metric and therefore perceived no problem at all, which is section 3.11 exactly. Ethics: fails the justice criterion, because costs fall on a district-defined group with less power, and fails the rights criterion, because the applicant cannot get reasons or due process. Accountability gap: nobody owns the model. Also low psychological safety from Unit 1, and the fundamental attribution error in reading Farida's overrides as a performance defect rather than a signal.

(b) Why it was missed. The firm monitored aggregate outcomes, so district-level disparate impact was invisible by construction. The falling default rate was read as validation, when rejecting sound applicants also lowers defaults, which is a survivorship problem: the firm can see the defaults of those it approved and never sees the performance of those it refused. The override metric converted the only human check into a career risk. No named owner, no explainability requirement and no post-launch audit. And the head of credit's "one-off" framing is hindsight and confirmation bias protecting a decision already made.

(c) What to do. Audit the model for disparate impact by district, gender, age and caste immediately, and repeat it on a schedule rather than once. Remove the override rate from individual scorecards and instead treat overrides as information, reviewing them for patterns, which restores the control and reinforces the right behaviour. Require explainability sufficient to give every rejected applicant a reason, which is also a regulatory necessity. Assign a named owner and a governance forum for the model, with the board informed of limitations rather than a single accuracy figure. Reinstate human authority with the time and information to use it, and rotate officers through enough real assessment work to prevent deskilling. Retrain or reweight the model on data corrected for historical bias, and consider withholding district as a feature along with its proxies. Finally, on the people side, reverse the message sent to Farida visibly, because until the person who raised it is seen to be vindicated, no one else will raise the next one.
