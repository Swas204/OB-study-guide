Learning, Perception and Individual Decision-Making

One story in four parts. Learning shapes how people behave, perception edits what they see, every perception error carries into the decisions they make, and machines now amplify all of it.

CO3 · L4 Analyse Depth follows class notes Gender Human Values Professional Ethics

3.1Learning: Definition and Importance

Definition

Learning is any relatively permanent change in behaviour that occurs as a result of experience.

Robbins and Judge

In OB, learning is about what people do at work, not what they study for an exam. Three words carry the weight of the definition, and each one rules something out.

  • Permanent: the change has to last. A salesperson who follows the new pitch once, because the manager is listening, and then slips back to the old habit has not learned. The word "relatively" is there because learned behaviour can still fade later.
  • Behaviour: the change has to show up in what the person actually does. Knowing a rule is not enough. It counts only when the person acts differently.
  • Experience: the change comes from practice, from watching others, or from the consequences of past actions. Reading a circular does not change behaviour on its own. Learning cannot be installed by a memo.

Why learning matters to a manager

Before anyone can be motivated or managed, they have to learn. Every skill, habit and bad reflex in a team was learned at some point. That is good news, because anything learned can be reshaped. Much of a manager's job is changing behaviour and making the change stick, which is exactly what learning means.

The next three sections explain how learning happens. The syllabus names three lenses, and each explains a different slice: learning from consequences, learning by thinking, and learning by watching others.

Exam tip

Learning is judged by behaviour, not knowledge. An employee who can recite every safety rule but still skips the helmet has not learned.

3.2Stimulus-Response Theory (Behaviourism)

The behaviourist view sees learning as a bond between a stimulus (something that happens) and a response (what the person does). Learning means that bond gets stronger. It comes in two forms.

Classical conditioning (Pavlov)

Dogs salivate automatically when they see food. That is a natural reflex. Pavlov rang a bell each time food was served. After many pairings, the bell alone made the dogs salivate. A neutral cue had become linked to an automatic reaction.

At work, classical conditioning explains involuntary reactions. Your stomach tightens when the notification tone for a harsh manager sounds. A new hire feels a jolt of anxiety in the room where they once had a brutal review. Nobody chooses these reactions. The cue triggers them.

Operant conditioning (Skinner)

Here behaviour is voluntary and shaped by its consequences. A person acts, notices what follows, and adjusts. Behaviour followed by a good outcome is repeated. Behaviour followed by a bad outcome fades. This is the stronger theory for management, because managers control so many consequences: praise, bonuses, assignments, warnings. In short, what gets rewarded gets repeated.

Common mistake

Classical conditioning is about an involuntary reaction set off by a cue that comes before it. Operant conditioning is about a chosen behaviour driven by the consequence that comes after it.

Four consequence tools

Operant conditioning gives a manager four tools. Two increase a behaviour and two decrease it. "Positive" means something is added and "negative" means something is taken away. They do not mean good and bad.

ToolWhat the manager doesEffectExample
Positive reinforcementAdds a pleasant consequenceBehaviour risesPraise a rep publicly right after a big sale
Negative reinforcementRemoves an unpleasant oneBehaviour risesStop the daily status emails once targets are hit
PunishmentAdds an unpleasant consequenceBehaviour fallsA formal warning for a safety breach
ExtinctionRemoves the reward that kept it goingBehaviour fadesStop laughing at disruptive jokes so they die out

Confusing these tools is how managers reward the very thing they want to stop. Laughing along at the disruptive jokes is positive reinforcement. Ignoring them is extinction.

Schedules of reinforcement

When you reward matters as much as whether you reward. Two questions decide the schedule. Is the reward tied to time (interval) or to output (ratio)? And is it fixed or variable (unpredictable)? That gives four schedules.

  • Fixed interval: a reward after a set period, like a monthly salary. It arrives on a known date whatever the effort, so effort tends to rise just before it and then drop.
  • Variable interval: a reward after unpredictable gaps, like surprise inspections or a manager who drops by at random. The reward could come at any time, so effort stays steady.
  • Fixed ratio: a reward after a set number of outputs, like piece-rate pay. More output brings more reward, so output is high, with a short pause after each reward.
  • Variable ratio: a reward after an unpredictable number of outputs, like sales commission. Any attempt could be the one that pays, so this gives the highest and most lasting effort.

This is why a slot machine is more addictive than a salary. A salary is a fixed interval. A slot machine is a variable ratio.

3.3Cognitive Theory of Learning

Behaviourism treats people as responding to cues and consequences. Tolman argued that learning is more than reflex. Between the stimulus and the response sits thinking: people make sense of a situation before they act.

  • Mental maps: people build a mental picture of how a place or a system works, such as which step leads to which and who handles what.
  • Expectancies: people form beliefs about what an action will lead to. "If I finish this certification, I will get the analytics project." They act on these expectations as well as on rewards they have already received.
  • Learning without immediate reward: Tolman's rats explored a maze with no food in it. Later, when food was placed at the end, they ran the maze as quickly as rats that had been rewarded all along. They had learned the layout earlier. It only showed once there was a reason to use it.

This is the key difference from operant conditioning. Operant learning follows a consequence. Cognitive learning can happen before any consequence, be stored, and be used later when it pays.

The managerial lever: explain the why, not just the what. People who understand how the system works, and what their effort leads to, can handle new situations without being told every step.

Example

A new analyst at an Infosys project watches how month-end reporting works long before she is asked to do it. When her lead goes on leave, she runs the process without training. She learned it earlier, with no reward, and used it once it became useful.

3.4Social Learning Theory

Bandura showed that we also learn by watching others and copying them. The learner does not need to be rewarded personally. Seeing what a model does, and what happens to them, is enough. We copy models we find credible or admired, such as a respected senior, a successful colleague or a leader.

The Bobo doll experiment: children watched an adult hit and kick an inflatable doll. Later, left alone with the doll, many copied the same aggression. Nobody had rewarded them. Watching was enough.

The four processes

Learning by watching works only if all four steps happen, in this order:

  1. Attention: the learner must notice the model. We notice people who are credible or admired. A new joiner copies whoever seems to succeed, good or bad.
  2. Retention: the learner must remember what they saw, so they can recall it when the situation comes up.
  3. Reproduction: the learner must be able to perform it. Watching a skilled negotiator does not help until the learner practises the skill.
  4. Motivation: the learner must want to do it. People copy behaviour they see rewarded and avoid behaviour they see punished.

The managerial lever: a leader is always modelling, whether they mean to or not. A leader's visible behaviour teaches more than any policy document. So behave the way you want copied, and put your best people where new joiners can watch them.

Exam tip

A complete manager uses all three theories: consequences (operant), understanding (cognitive) and models (social learning). Say this in any comparison answer.

3.5Principles of Learning

The three theories explain how learning happens. The principles turn them into a checklist for designing any training. Whatever the method, learning sticks better when these conditions are built in. Ignore them and even good content fails to land.

01
Motivation and readiness

The learner must want to learn and be prepared for it. Readiness means they already have the basics the new skill builds on. Advanced Excel training fails for someone who cannot yet write a simple formula.

02
Active practice and repetition

Behaviour changes through doing, not hearing. Repeating the action is what turns it into a habit.

03
Timely feedback and reinforcement

The sooner the consequence follows the action, the clearer the link. Feedback given a month later teaches very little.

04
Meaningful, whole-to-part material

Show the big picture first, then the detail. Details are remembered when the learner can see where they fit and why they matter to the job.

05
Transfer

Practise in conditions close to the real job so the skill carries over. A skill learned only in a classroom often never reaches the desk.

Example

A bank teaching staff a new loan system first shows the full customer journey (whole-to-part), lets them process dummy files on the live screen (practice and transfer), and corrects errors on the spot (timely feedback).

3.6Shaping as a Managerial Tool

Definition

Shaping means reinforcing successive approximations, rewarding each small step that moves closer to the target behaviour instead of waiting for the finished article.

Robbins and Judge

Complex behaviour rarely appears in finished form. If a manager waits to reward only the perfect performance, there is nothing to reward for weeks and the learner gives up. Shaping applies operant conditioning in steps. "Successive approximations" simply means each attempt that comes a little closer to the target. It is how you teach anything complex, from a new CRM habit to a full sales pitch.

  1. Start where they are. Reward the first rough attempt, not perfection. This gets the behaviour started.
  2. Raise the bar in stages. Each round, reward only the closer version. The old level no longer earns the reward, so the learner moves up.
  3. Reward often early, then thin out. Frequent reinforcement builds the habit. Spaced reinforcement makes it durable, so it survives without the manager.
  4. Never punish the attempt. Punishing an imperfect step kills the willingness to try the next one.
Example

A new sales rep is learning the full pitch. In week one the manager praises a polite, confident opening. In week two praise comes only if the rep also asks about the customer's needs. By week four it is reserved for the full pitch with a clear close.

3.7Learning for Green Behaviour

Learning also reaches beyond performance. Learning for green behaviour applies the same learning engine to sustainability. Green actions usually bring no immediate personal payoff, so they rarely become habits on their own. Modelling, reinforcement and shaping turn them into habits. This is where organisational behaviour meets human values.

MODEL
Make it visible

Social learning at work. Leaders and green champions who publicly practise sustainable habits teach the team far faster than a policy poster. If the department head prints every email, no poster will change anyone.

REINFORCE
Reward the act

Operant conditioning at work. Recognise and reward energy saving, waste cutting and car-pooling. What gets reinforced gets repeated, so green wins must be celebrated, not assumed.

SHAPE
Start small

Shaping at work. Begin with one easy habit, like a reusable bottle or a lights-off rule, then raise the bar in stages until sustainability is simply how the office runs.

Exam tip

Link each green move back to its theory: model is Bandura, reinforce is Skinner, shape is successive approximation. That link is what earns the marks.

3.8Perception and the Factors Influencing It

Definition

Perception is the process by which individuals organise and interpret their sensory impressions in order to give meaning to their environment.

Robbins and Judge

Perception has two steps. We take in what we see and hear, and then we organise and interpret it to make sense of it. The interpreting step is where people differ. Two people sit through the same meeting, or read the same email, and walk away with opposite stories. What we perceive can be very different from objective reality.

This matters because people act on their perception, not on the facts. An employee who believes promotions are unfair behaves as if they are, whatever the data says. So as far as behaviour is concerned, perception is reality, and a manager has to manage perceptions as well as facts.

The three factors that bend perception

When two people read the same thing differently, the cause usually sits in one of three places.

  • The perceiver (you): your own attitudes, motives, interests, past experience and expectations colour what you see. A manager who dislikes remote work reads a remote employee's silence as slacking. An accountant notices the cost of a plan where a marketer notices the brand. Someone let down by a colleague before expects it again. You do not see people as they are, you see them as you are.
  • The target (them): features of the person or thing being judged. Novelty, motion, sound and size grab attention, so the loud voice in a meeting is noticed and the quiet one is not. Proximity and similarity make us group things together, so people who sit together or look alike are wrongly assumed to think alike.
  • The situation (context): the timing and the work or social setting. The same behaviour reads differently in different contexts. A person can seem confident in a boardroom and arrogant at a funeral.
Inattentional blindness

In Daniel Simons' selective attention test, viewers busy counting passes in a ball game fail to see something impossible happening in plain sight. Attention is limited, so focusing hard on one thing blinds us to others. This is how attention works, not a personal failing. The lesson for a manager: whenever you feel sure you saw the whole picture, you probably did not. Treat your confident perception as a first draft and build in a second pair of eyes.

3.9Attribution Theory

When someone behaves in a certain way, we instantly build a story about why. Attribution theory says we decide whether the behaviour was internally caused, meaning under the person's own control (effort, ability, attitude), or externally caused, meaning forced by the situation (a broken system, an impossible deadline). That single judgement decides whether a manager coaches someone or fires them.

Kelley's three signals

We make the judgement using three signals, whether we know it or not. Take an employee who missed a deadline.

  • Distinctiveness: do they miss deadlines only on this task, or on everything? If it is only this task, something about the task is the likely cause, so it points external. If it happens on everything, it points to the person.
  • Consensus: did everyone with the same task also miss it? If everyone missed, the situation is the likely cause. If only this person missed, it points internal.
  • Consistency: do they miss this deadline every time, or was it a one-off? A repeated pattern points to the person. A rare slip points to something in the situation that day.
SignalHigh points toLow points to
DistinctivenessExternalInternal
ConsensusExternalInternal
ConsistencyInternalExternal

Two errors that break the reading

  • Fundamental attribution error: when judging others, we overweight their character and underweight their situation. We see the person clearly but not the pressures around them, so the late employee is "lazy", never mind the broken system.
  • Self-serving bias: for ourselves we flip it. Our wins are our talent, and our failures are bad luck or someone else's fault. It protects how we see ourselves.

Put together, the two errors make us harsh on others and easy on ourselves.

The fair-manager habit

Before you attribute a failure to the person, force yourself to argue the situation's case first. It is the single fastest way to become fairer than other managers. In answers, note that consistency is the odd one out: high consistency points internal, while high distinctiveness and high consensus point external.

3.10Common Shortcuts in Judging People

Reading people fully is slow, so the brain takes shortcuts. They save time and are sometimes right, which is why we keep using them. The danger is that each one quietly injects bias into hiring, reviews and promotions.

ShortcutWhat happensWorkplace example
Selective perceptionWe notice what matches our interests, background and expectations, and filter out the rest. We see what fits.Two managers watch the same employee and each sees only the evidence that confirms their earlier view.
Halo effectOne standout trait colours the whole impression. The reverse, the horn effect, lets one bad trait drag down everything else."He speaks so well in meetings, he must be our sharpest analyst." Nobody has checked his output.
Contrast effectWe judge a person against whoever came just before, not against a fixed standard.An average candidate shines after a weak one and sinks after a brilliant one. Interview order should never decide a hire, but it often does.
StereotypingWe judge someone by the group we place them in (gender, age, region, background) instead of as an individual. It is quick and often wrong.Assuming an older employee cannot learn new software, and passing them over for the project.
ProjectionWe assume others share our own motives, values and reactions. We mistake our own mirror for a window."I would never take leave for that, so she can't be committed." The workaholic manager assumes everyone wants to work weekends.
Common mistake

Do not mix up halo and contrast. Halo is one trait spreading over the same person. Contrast is a comparison between two different people.

These shortcuts do not stay inside our heads. They feed straight into the decisions we make, which is where the next section begins.

3.11The Link Between Perception and Individual Decision-Making

The key idea

Individual decision-making is a response to a perceived problem.

This sentence is the bridge between perception and decisions. Each part of it carries meaning.

  • A response: a decision is not made in a vacuum. Something prompts it. A manager decides because they believe something needs attention.
  • Perceived: the prompt is the problem as the decision-maker sees it, not the problem as it really is. If the manager does not notice the problem, no decision is made at all. If they read it wrongly, they decide about the wrong thing.

Why perception comes first

You cannot decide about a situation until you have first perceived and interpreted it. So the order is always the same: take in what is happening, make sense of it, then choose what to do. The decision is built on top of the interpretation.

How the distortions carry over

That order means every distortion from 3.8 to 3.10 rides straight into the choice. The perceiver's expectations decide which problems get noticed. Selective perception filters out the evidence that does not fit. The fundamental attribution error blames a person when the cause was the situation. The halo effect makes one strong trait look like overall merit. In each case the decision can be carried out perfectly and still be wrong, because the reading it rests on was wrong.

A decision is only ever as clean as the perception it was built on.

Example

A regional head sees falling sales in one branch and, through the fundamental attribution error, blames the branch manager. A new competitor opening nearby, the real cause, is never examined. Replacing the manager is a well-executed decision about the wrong problem, so sales keep falling.

Exam tip

When a case asks why a decision went wrong, check the perception before the choice. Name the factor or shortcut that distorted the reading, then show how it led to the decision.

3.12Decision-Making in Organisations

3.11 showed that a decision starts with how a problem is perceived. This section is about what happens next, when the manager actually chooses. Every decision starts as a gap between how things are and how you want them to be. Sales are at 80 and the target is 100. The team takes three months to onboard and you want it done in one. Closing that gap is the decision.

Organisations assume people then choose rationally. Reality is kinder to human limits. There are three modes running in every manager, and knowing which one you are in is half the discipline.

The three decision modes

ModeHow it worksWhen it winsHow it fails
Rational: the idealFull information, clear weighted criteria, every option scored, the best one wins.High-stakes calls when there is real time and data.In real life information is never complete and the clock never stops.
Bounded: the realistBuild a simplified model, scan a few options, pick the first one that is good enough.Most everyday decisions. It is fast and usually fine.You may stop searching before the best answer.
Intuitive: the expert reflexFast judgement distilled from deep experience, felt more than reasoned.Recurring, pattern-rich situations you have met many times.Novel or high-stakes calls, where it is just bias wearing a confident face.

The rational decision-making model

The rational model assumes a clear problem, full information and a single goal. It then runs six steps.

  1. Define the problem. State the gap clearly, because a wrongly defined problem leads to a well-made wrong decision.
  2. Identify the decision criteria. List what matters in the choice, such as cost, quality or speed.
  3. Weight the criteria. Not everything matters equally, so give each criterion a weight.
  4. Generate the alternatives. List the possible options before judging any of them.
  5. Rate each alternative against the criteria. Score every option on every criterion.
  6. Choose the highest-scoring option. The best total wins.

Nobody quite lives in this model, because it needs time and information that managers rarely have.

Bounded rationality and intuition

Herbert Simon showed that we rarely finish the rational process. Our time, information and mental capacity are limited, so we build simplified models, consider a few options and pick the first one that is good enough. Simon called this satisficing, as opposed to finding the perfect option. It is fast and usually fine, but it can stop the search before the best answer, so a manager should protect the decisions that truly matter by slowing down for them.

Under expertise and time pressure, we also lean on intuition. A senior credit officer who has seen thousands of loan files can sense a risky one in seconds. That works for familiar patterns. It fails on new kinds of problems, where the "gut feel" has nothing real to draw on. Neither satisficing nor intuition is lazy, but both are where bias sneaks in.

The decision biases to hunt

Robbins warns every manager to watch for these errors.

BiasWhat it isThe trap
AnchoringFixating on the first piece of information and adjusting too little from it.Fixating on the first salary number named in a negotiation.
ConfirmationSeeking information that supports what we already believe and ignoring what challenges it.Only reading reports that agree with your plan.
Escalation of commitmentStaying with a decision even when evidence shows it is failing, to justify what was already spent.Pouring more money into a failing project to justify the last spend.
AvailabilityJudging how likely something is by how easily it comes to mind.Fearing a rare risk because it was just in the news.
OverconfidenceBelieving our judgement is more accurate than it is.Being far more certain you are right than the facts allow.
HindsightBelieving, after the outcome is known, that we predicted it.Claiming you knew the outcome all along, after it happened.
Exam tip

In a case, first name the mode the manager used (rational, bounded or intuitive), then name the bias that crept in, and quote the line from the case that shows it. Then say which mode the decision really needed.

3.13Ethics in Decision-Making

A decision can be well made and still be wrong in a moral sense. So every serious call has to pass an ethical test as well as a rational one. Robbins names three ethical decision criteria that people use to make ethical choices. Each one protects something real, and each one has a blind spot.

CriterionThe ruleWhat it protectsIts blind spot
UtilitarianismChoose whatever delivers the greatest good for the greatest number.Efficiency and results.It can quietly sacrifice a minority for the majority.
RightsRespect the fundamental rights and liberties of individuals, such as privacy, free speech and due process.The individual, including whistle-blowers.It can block decisions that would help the many.
JusticeImpose and enforce rules fairly and impartially, so benefits and costs are distributed equitably.The underrepresented.It can reward mediocrity by ignoring performance.
  • Utilitarianism is how most business decisions are justified, because it asks which option produces the most benefit overall. Closing one loss-making plant to save the company may be the greatest good for the greatest number. The workers at that plant are the minority who pay for it.
  • Rights puts the individual first. A person's privacy, their freedom to speak up and their right to a fair hearing cannot be traded away just because the majority would gain. This is the criterion that protects a whistle-blower who reports wrongdoing. Its weakness is that one person's right can stop a change that would help everyone else.
  • Justice asks whether rules are applied evenly and whether benefits and costs are shared fairly. It protects people who have less power or voice. Pushed too far, equal treatment ignores who actually performed better, which can reward mediocrity.
The cross-cutting point

Real ethical maturity is holding all three in tension, not picking the one that lets you do what you already wanted. Human values and professional ethics live in that discomfort.

Example

A hospital chain plans to share patient data with an insurer to cut costs for most patients. Utilitarianism says yes, because most people pay less. Rights says no, because patients did not consent and their privacy is at stake. Justice asks whether poorer patients would end up with worse cover. A mature manager weighs all three before deciding.

3.14Artificial Intelligence in Managerial Decision-Making

The newest member of every management team is an algorithm. This section is about deciding with machines: what to hand them, what to keep, and how to stay ethical when the recommendation comes from a black box, a system whose reasoning nobody can see.

AI in managerial decision-making is powerful precisely where humans are weak, because it is fast, tireless and consistent with large amounts of data. It is dangerous precisely where humans matter most, in calls about fairness, dignity and values.

AI does not remove bias

AI learns from the data we feed it. If that data carries our past biases, the AI learns them too, and then applies them at scale and invisibly. A biased manager affects a few decisions. A biased model affects every decision it touches. His algorithmic ethics check shows three ways this goes wrong.

  • Historical bias in the data. A hiring AI trained on ten years of past hires keeps rejecting women for engineering roles. It is not being objective. It learned that past hires were mostly men and copied that pattern.
  • Abdication. A manager approves every loan the model flags green without ever asking how it decides. That is not efficient delegation. No human is accountable and nobody can explain the decisions.
  • Aggregate accuracy hiding unfairness. A model is 95 percent accurate overall but wrong far more often for one regional group. The overall figure looks good, but it hides unfairness to a subgroup.

The manager's four rules for deciding with machines

  1. Augment, do not abdicate. The algorithm advises and the accountable human decides. Never hand a one-way-door, people-affecting call, such as a dismissal or a loan rejection, to a black box.
  2. Interrogate the data. Biased training data produces biased decisions at scale. Ask what history the model learned from before you trust it.
  3. Demand explainability. If no one can explain why the model chose, you cannot defend the decision, ethically or legally.
  4. Keep the human in the loop for judgement, ethics and edge cases. Give AI the pattern-matching and keep the values, fairness and dignity calls for people.

Who owns the decision

Deciding with machines means sorting each decision to the right owner. Some go to the algorithm, such as routine, high-volume pattern-matching like flagging duplicate invoices. Some stay with humans, such as calls about people's jobs, dignity or fairness. The rest run as a hybrid, where the model recommends and a person reviews and decides.

The synthesis of this unit

Learning shapes behaviour, perception filters reality, biases distort judgement, and machines now amplify all of it. The leader who understands this chain does more than make better decisions. They build systems and cultures that make everyone's decisions better.

Exam tip

For any AI case, name the risk first (historical bias, abdication or hidden subgroup unfairness), then apply the matching rule. Historical bias calls for interrogating the data. Abdication calls for augmenting, explainability and a human in the loop.

3.15Data-Driven Decision-Making

Robbins calls disciplined, evidence-first decision-making evidence-based management: base your calls on the best available data, not on gut, tradition or the loudest voice in the room. Data-driven decision-making and artificial intelligence are the modern engines of exactly that.

Used well, they debias us, because data can correct the perception errors and biases covered earlier in this unit. Used badly, they scale our worst biases to millions of decisions in seconds. So the manager's new skill is not coding. It is knowing where a human must stay in the loop.

The data pipeline

A data-driven decision is only as good as the pipeline behind it. Each stage demands a discipline, and each has a failure that hides there.

StageWhat it involvesThe failure that hides there
1 · Frame (question)Turn the business problem into a clear question the data can answer.A badly framed question, so even perfect data answers the wrong thing.
2 · Gather (data)Collect data that is relevant, accurate and covers everyone the decision affects.Incomplete or unrepresentative data, which builds bias in from the start.
3 · Analyse (insight)Find the pattern in the data and work out what it means.Reading too much into a pattern, such as treating two things that move together as cause and effect.
4 · Decide and review (act)Act on the insight, then check whether the decision worked.Never reviewing the outcome, so mistakes repeat.
Example

A retail chain asks, "Which stores should get extra staff during Diwali?" (frame). It pulls three years of footfall and sales by hour (gather), finds that evening peaks drive most sales (analyse), adds evening shifts in the busiest stores, and compares results with the previous year (decide and review).

Exam tip

Data-driven does not mean data-only. Write that evidence-based management uses data to correct gut feel and bias, while the human checks the frame, the data quality and the final call.

Case · The quiet analyst

Kavya, an analyst at a Bengaluru fintech, is quiet in meetings. Three colleagues call her "not a team player". Her new manager, Arjun, came from a sales team where loud people were the stars. In appraisals Arjun rates her below Rohit, who presents confidently, though Kavya's reports have fewer errors. Arjun notes she missed one deadline, the same week the whole team missed it after a server failure. HR now wants a fair review process and a plan to help new joiners pick up good habits faster.

Questions: Identify the perception errors in Arjun's judgement. Suggest how learning theory can improve onboarding.

Answer outline
  • Perceiver factor: Arjun's sales background shapes what he values (3.8).
  • Halo effect for Rohit and stereotyping of quiet people as disengaged (3.10).
  • Missed deadline: high consensus points external, so blaming Kavya is the fundamental attribution error (3.9).
  • The low rating is a decision built on distorted perception, so it is unfair however carefully it was written up (3.11).
  • Onboarding: assign strong models (social learning), shape new skills step by step, reward often early then thin out (3.4, 3.6, 3.2).
