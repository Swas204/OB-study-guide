Learning, Perception and Individual Decision-Making

One story in three parts. Learning shapes how people behave, perception edits what they see, and every perception error carries straight into the decisions they make.

CO3 · L4 Analyse Depth follows class notes Gender Human Values Professional Ethics

3.1Learning: Definition and Importance

Definition

Learning is any relatively permanent change in behaviour that occurs as a result of experience.

Robbins and Judge

Three words carry the weight of this definition:

  • Permanent: a one-off fluke does not count. The change has to last.
  • Behaviour: it has to show up in what a person actually does, not just in what they say they know.
  • Experience: it is earned through practice, observation or consequence. It cannot be installed by a memo.

Why it matters to a manager: before anyone can be motivated or managed, they have to learn. Every skill, habit and bad reflex in a team was learned, so every one of them can be reshaped. If you can change behaviour and make it stick, you have made someone learn, and that is the quiet core of management.

Exam tip

Learning is judged by behaviour, not knowledge. An employee who can recite every safety rule but still skips the helmet has not learned.

3.2Stimulus-Response Theory (Behaviourism)

The behaviourist view: learning is a bond between a stimulus and a response. It comes in two forms.

Classical conditioning
Pavlov · involuntary
  • A neutral cue is paired with an automatic reaction until the cue alone triggers it.
  • Pavlov paired a bell with food until the bell alone made the dog salivate.
  • The root of workplace associations, like dread at a certain ringtone.
Operant conditioning
Skinner · voluntary
  • Behaviour is shaped by its consequences.
  • Behaviour followed by a good outcome repeats. Behaviour followed by a bad one fades.
  • Stronger for management: what gets rewarded gets repeated.

Four consequence tools

Operant conditioning gives managers four tools. Two increase a behaviour and two decrease it. Positive adds something, negative removes something.

ToolWhat the manager doesEffectExample
Positive reinforcementAdds a pleasant consequenceBehaviour risesPraise a rep publicly right after a big sale
Negative reinforcementRemoves an unpleasant oneBehaviour risesStop the daily status emails once targets are hit
PunishmentAdds an unpleasant consequenceBehaviour fallsA formal warning for a safety breach
ExtinctionRemoves the rewardBehaviour fadesStop laughing at disruptive jokes so they die out

Schedules of reinforcement

When you reward matters as much as whether you reward. This is why a slot machine is more addictive than a salary.

  • Fixed interval: reward after a set time, like a monthly salary. Effort rises just before payday, then drops.
  • Variable interval: reward after unpredictable time gaps, like surprise inspections. Steady, consistent effort.
  • Fixed ratio: reward after a set number of outputs, like piece-rate pay. High output, with a pause after each reward.
  • Variable ratio: reward after an unpredictable number of outputs, like sales commissions. The highest and most lasting response.
Common mistake

Negative reinforcement is not punishment. It increases behaviour by removing something unpleasant. Classical is involuntary and triggered by a paired cue. Operant is voluntary and driven by consequences.

3.3Cognitive Theory of Learning

Associated with Edward Tolman. Learning is not just reflex. Thinking sits between the stimulus and the response.

  • Cognitive maps: people build mental maps of how things work.
  • Expectancies: they form expectations about what an action will lead to.
  • Learning without reward: people can learn without any immediate reward, then use the knowledge later when it pays. Tolman's rats learned a maze even with no reward, then used the map when it mattered.

The managerial lever: explain the why, not just the what. People who understand the system can learn now and act later.

Example

A new analyst at an Infosys project watches how month-end reporting works long before she is asked to do it. When her lead goes on leave, she runs the process without training. She learned it earlier, with no reward, and used it once it became useful.

3.4Social Learning Theory

Associated with Albert Bandura. We learn by watching others and copying models we find credible or admired.

The Bobo doll experiment: children copied the aggression they simply watched an adult perform on an inflatable doll. Nobody rewarded them. Watching was enough.

AAttention: we must notice the model
RRetention: we must remember what we saw
RReproduction: we must be able to perform it
MMotivation: we must want to do it

The managerial lever: a leader is always modelling, whether they mean to or not. A leader's visible behaviour teaches more than any policy document. So behave the way you want copied, and put your best people where new joiners can watch them.

Exam tip

A complete manager uses all three theories: consequences (operant), understanding (cognitive) and models (social learning). Say this in any comparison answer.

3.5Principles of Learning

Whatever the method, learning sticks better when a few conditions are built in. Ignore these and even good content fails to land.

01
Motivation and readiness

The learner must want it and be prepared for it.

02
Active practice and repetition

Behaviour changes through doing, not hearing.

03
Timely feedback and reinforcement

The sooner the consequence, the tighter the lesson.

04
Meaningful, whole-to-part material

Context first, then detail.

05
Transfer

Practise in conditions close to the real job so the skill carries over.

Example

A bank teaching staff a new loan system first shows the full customer journey (whole-to-part), lets them process dummy files on the live screen (practice and transfer), and corrects errors on the spot (timely feedback).

3.6Shaping as a Managerial Tool

Definition

Shaping means reinforcing successive approximations, rewarding each small step that moves closer to the target behaviour instead of waiting for the finished article.

Robbins and Judge

It is how you teach anything complex, from a new CRM habit to a full sales pitch. Four steps:

  1. Start where they are. Reward the first rough attempt, not perfection.
  2. Raise the bar in stages. Each round, reward only the closer version.
  3. Reward often early, then thin out. Frequent reinforcement builds the habit. Spaced reinforcement makes it durable.
  4. Never punish the attempt. Punishing an imperfect step kills the willingness to try the next one.
Why it beats a training manual

People do not leap to competence. Shaping turns an overwhelming skill into a ladder of small, winnable steps, and each rung earns a reward that pulls them up.

3.7Learning for Green Behaviour

Learning also reaches beyond performance. Learning for green behaviour applies the same engine to sustainability, turning eco-friendly actions into habits through modelling, reinforcement and shaping. This is where organisational behaviour meets human values.

MODEL
Make it visible

Leaders and green champions who publicly practise sustainable habits teach the team through social learning far faster than a policy poster.

REINFORCE
Reward the act

Recognise and reward energy saving, waste cutting and car-pooling. Green wins must be celebrated, not assumed.

SHAPE
Start small

Begin with one easy habit, like a reusable bottle or a lights-off rule, then raise the bar in stages until sustainability is simply how the office runs.

Exam tip

Link each green move back to its theory: model is Bandura, reinforce is Skinner, shape is successive approximation. That link is what earns the marks.

3.8Perception and the Factors Influencing It

Definition

Perception is the process by which individuals organise and interpret their sensory impressions in order to give meaning to their environment.

Robbins and Judge

What we perceive can be substantially different from objective reality. Two people see the same meeting or the same email and walk away with opposite stories. Since people act on their perception, not on the facts, perception is reality as far as behaviour is concerned.

The three factors that bend perception

  • The perceiver: your own attitudes, motives, interests, past experience and expectations. You do not see people as they are, you see them as you are.
  • The target: features of the person or thing judged, such as novelty, motion, sound, size, proximity and similarity. Loud or unusual targets grab attention, and we wrongly group similar things together.
  • The situation: the context, timing, and work or social setting. The same person reads as confident in a boardroom and arrogant at a funeral.
Inattentional blindness

In Daniel Simons' selective attention test, people focused on one task fail to see something obvious happening in plain sight. This is how attention works, not a personal failing. The lesson: treat your own confident perception as a first draft, and build in a second pair of eyes.

3.9Attribution Theory

When someone behaves, we instantly build a story about why. Attribution theory says we decide whether a behaviour was internally caused (under the person's own control) or externally caused (forced by the situation). That single judgement decides whether a manager coaches someone or fires them.

Kelley's three signals

SignalQuestionHigh points toLow points to
DistinctivenessDoes it happen only on this task, or on everything?ExternalInternal
ConsensusDid everyone in the same situation do it, or just them?ExternalInternal
ConsistencyDoes it happen every time, or was it a one-off?InternalExternal

Two errors that break the reading

  • Fundamental attribution error: when judging others, we overweight their character and underweight their situation. The late employee is lazy, never mind the broken system.
  • Self-serving bias: for ourselves we flip it. Our wins are our talent, our failures are bad luck or someone else's fault.
The fair-manager habit

Before you blame the person, argue the situation's case first. Note that consistency is the odd one out: high consistency points internal, while high distinctiveness and high consensus point external.

3.10Common Shortcuts in Judging People

Reading people fully is slow, so the brain takes shortcuts. Each one saves time and quietly injects bias into hiring, reviews and promotions.

ShortcutWhat happensWorkplace example
Selective perceptionWe notice what matches our interests, background and expectations, and filter out the rest.Two managers watch the same employee and each sees only the evidence that confirms their prior view.
Halo effectOne standout trait colours the whole impression. The reverse, the horn effect, does the same damage in the negative direction."He speaks so well in meetings, he must be our sharpest analyst." Nobody has checked his output.
Contrast effectWe judge a person against whoever came just before, not against a fixed standard.An average candidate shines after a weak one and sinks after a brilliant one.
StereotypingWe judge someone by the group we assign them to (gender, age, region, background) rather than as an individual.Unfair bias in hiring and promotion decisions.
ProjectionWe assume others share our own motives, values and reactions."I would never take leave for that, so she can't be committed."
Common mistake

Do not mix up halo and contrast. Halo is one trait spreading over the same person. Contrast is a comparison between two different people.

3.11The Link Between Perception and Individual Decision-Making

Individual decision-making is a response to a perceived problem. A problem is a gap between the current state and a desired state. You cannot decide about a situation until you have first perceived and interpreted it.

Perception enters the decision at every stage:

  • Whether a problem is noticed at all.
  • Which information is treated as relevant.
  • Which options are considered, and how they are judged.

So every distortion from 3.8 to 3.10 rides straight into the choice. A decision is only as clean as the perception it was built on.

Example

A regional head sees falling sales in one branch and, through the fundamental attribution error, blames the branch manager. A new competitor opening nearby, the real cause, is never examined, so the decision (replacing the manager) does not fix the problem.

3.12Decision-Making in Organisations

The rational model

The ideal decision-maker follows six steps: define the problem, identify the decision criteria, weight the criteria, generate alternatives, rate each alternative, and choose the best one. It assumes complete information and unlimited time.

How decisions are actually made

  • Bounded rationality: people work with simplified versions of complex problems, because time, information and mental capacity are limited.
  • Satisficing: they accept the first solution that is good enough, rather than the best one.
  • Intuition: a fast, non-conscious judgement built on experience. Useful for experts, risky for novices.

Common decision biases

  • Overconfidence: being more certain we are right than events later prove.
  • Anchoring: fixing on the first number or piece of information and adjusting too little.
  • Confirmation bias: seeking information that supports what we already believe.
  • Availability bias: judging by what comes easily to mind, such as recent or vivid events.
  • Escalation of commitment: sticking with a failing decision because of what has already been invested.
Exam tip

In a case, name the bias and then the fix: a devil's advocate for confirmation bias, a fresh estimate for anchoring, a pre-set exit point for escalation of commitment.

3.13Ethics in Decision-Making

Every decision has an ethical side. Three criteria are used to judge whether a decision is ethical:

CriterionQuestion it asksWeakness
UtilitarianDoes it give the greatest good for the greatest number?Can ignore the rights of a minority
RightsDoes it respect basic rights such as privacy, free speech and due process?Can slow decisions down
JusticeAre rules applied fairly and impartially, with fair sharing of costs and benefits?Can discourage risk-taking and innovation

Business decisions lean on the utilitarian criterion because it fits efficiency and profit. A fuller ethical decision checks all three. Perception biases from this unit, such as stereotyping and self-serving bias, can quietly make an unfair decision feel justified.

Example

Closing a loss-making plant may pass the utilitarian test for shareholders. The rights and justice tests then ask whether workers were told honestly and in time, and whether severance was fair.

3.14Artificial Intelligence in Managerial Decision-Making

AI systems analyse large amounts of data to predict, recommend or automate decisions. They are now used in hiring, credit approval, demand forecasting and performance tracking.

What AI adds
Uses
  • Speed and scale on routine decisions.
  • Pattern-finding in data no human could scan.
  • Consistency, since it does not tire or have moods.
Where it fails
Limits
  • Bias in the training data is repeated at scale.
  • Many systems cannot explain how they reached an answer.
  • No judgement on ethics, context or new situations.

Human-in-the-loop: AI should support the decision, and a person should stay accountable for it. Managers must also guard against over-trusting the machine's output simply because it looks objective.

Example

An AI screening tool trained on ten years of past hires keeps rejecting women for technical roles, because past hires were mostly men. Stereotyping from 3.10 has been automated.

3.15Data-Driven Decision-Making

Data-driven decision-making means basing decisions on evidence from data rather than on intuition or opinion alone. It is a direct correction for the perception shortcuts in this unit.

Steps

  1. Define the question clearly.
  2. Collect relevant, reliable data.
  3. Analyse it to find patterns.
  4. Interpret the results in context.
  5. Decide, act, and track the outcome.

Pitfalls

  • Correlation is not causation: two figures moving together does not mean one causes the other.
  • Poor data quality: wrong or incomplete data gives confident wrong answers.
  • Measuring only what is easy: numbers can push out judgement on things that are hard to count, like morale.
Exam tip

The best answers say data and judgement work together. Data checks intuition, and judgement checks whether the data asks the right question.

Case · The quiet analyst

Kavya, an analyst at a Bengaluru fintech, is quiet in meetings. Three colleagues call her "not a team player". Her new manager, Arjun, came from a sales team where loud people were the stars. In appraisals Arjun rates her below Rohit, who presents confidently, though Kavya's reports have fewer errors. Arjun notes she missed one deadline, the same week the whole team missed it after a server failure. HR now wants a fair review process and a plan to help new joiners pick up good habits faster.

Questions: Identify the perception errors in Arjun's judgement. Suggest how learning theory can improve onboarding.

Answer outline
  • Perceiver factor: Arjun's sales background shapes what he values (3.8).
  • Halo effect for Rohit and stereotyping of quiet people as disengaged (3.10).
  • Missed deadline: high consensus points external, so blaming Kavya is the fundamental attribution error (3.9).
  • Fix the review: judge output against fixed criteria, use data on errors and delivery (3.15).
  • Onboarding: assign strong models (social learning), shape new skills step by step, reward often early then thin out (3.4, 3.6, 3.2).
