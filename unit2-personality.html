2.1Individual Behaviour and the Factors Influencing It

Individual behaviour is the way a single person acts and reacts at work. It covers what a person does, says, decides and avoids.

The foundational idea of this unit

B = f (P, E)
Behaviour is a function of the Person and the Environment.

Kurt Lewin

This one line settles a common exam trap. Behaviour is never caused by personality alone, and never by the situation alone. It is caused by the interaction of the two.

Example

A normally quiet accounts assistant becomes loud and assertive when the audit team questions her work. Her personality did not change in that hour. The situation activated a part of it. If you judged her only from the quiet weeks, you would predict her behaviour wrongly.

B. Psychological factors

These are internal and invisible, and they are the actual subject matter of Units 2 and 3: personality (2.2 onward), values and attitudes (Part B), emotions and moods, motivation (Part C), and perception and learning (Unit 3).

C. Environmental factors outside the organisation

  • Economic conditions: employment levels, wage rates, inflation. In a recession people tolerate worse conditions, so behaviour looks loyal when it is only trapped.
  • Political and legal environment: labour law, minimum wage, working-hours rules.
  • Social and cultural environment: family expectations, community norms, what counts locally as a respectable job.
  • Technological environment: which skills stay valuable, and the fear that they will not.

D. Organisational system factors

These are the ones a manager can actually change, which is why OB concentrates on them: physical facilities and layout, structure and span of control, rules and procedures, leadership style, the reward system (what gets measured and paid for is the loudest message any organisation sends), organisational culture, and the work group and its norms.

E. Socio-cultural factors

Culture, language, region, community expectation and social class. In a country as varied as India these matter a great deal for how instructions are received, how disagreement is expressed, and how hierarchy is treated.

The MARS model

McShane and Von Glinow, one of your essential readings, summarise individual behaviour with MARS. Its value is diagnostic.

MMotivation
"Do I want to?"
AAbility
"Can I?"
RRole perception
"Do I know what is expected?"
SSituational factors
"Does the situation permit it?"
Example · why MARS matters more than it looks

A branch employee is not cross-selling insurance. Most managers assume motivation. Run MARS instead:

  • Motivation? He is paid an incentive, so probably fine.
  • Ability? He has had no product training. Found one.
  • Role perception? He thought cross-selling was the relationship manager's job. Found a second.
  • Situation? The software takes twenty minutes per proposal. Found a third.

Three of the four causes had nothing to do with willingness. Shouting at him would have fixed nothing, which is the whole point of the model.

Types of individual work behaviour

  1. Task performance: doing the core job.
  2. Organisational Citizenship Behaviour: helping beyond the job description.
  3. Counterproductive Work Behaviour: theft, sabotage, absenteeism, harassment, time-wasting.
  4. Joining and staying: attraction and retention.
  5. Maintaining work attendance: avoiding absence and presenteeism.

2.2Personality: Meaning

Definition

Personality is the sum total of ways in which an individual reacts to and interacts with others.

After Gordon Allport, as used by Robbins and Judge

The breakdown that earns marks: sum total, so it is a whole pattern and not one trait; reacts and interacts, so it is visible only in relation to a world and other people; and relatively enduring, so it is consistent enough to be measured and to predict behaviour.

Heredity. Twin studies show a substantial genetic component in traits such as extraversion and neuroticism. Physical stature, temperament and energy level have a hereditary base.

Environment. Culture, family, social class, schooling and group memberships. Two siblings raised in the same house still differ, because birth order, friends and experiences differ.

Situation. Personality is stable, but the situation decides which traits get expressed.

Situation strength · a concept worth naming

In a strong situation such as a court hearing or an army parade, rules are clear and personality barely shows. In a weak situation such as an unstructured brainstorming session, personality dominates behaviour.

Example: you cannot tell an extravert from an introvert by watching a silent examination hall. Put the same students in a free-form group discussion and the difference appears in thirty seconds. Same people, different situation strength.

The related idea is trait activation theory: particular situations activate particular traits. A crisis activates emotional stability. A sales floor activates extraversion.

Measuring personality: three methods

MethodHow it worksLimitation
Self-report surveysThe person rates statements about themselves. Most common, for example a Big Five inventoryFaking good, especially in selection; self-insight is limited
Observer-ratings surveysA colleague or supervisor rates the personRater bias, limited exposure to the person
Projective measuresAmbiguous stimuli interpreted by the person: Rorschach inkblot test, Thematic Apperception TestPoor reliability and validity for work selection
Exam tip

Observer ratings predict job success better than self-reports do. Most students assume the opposite, so stating it correctly, with the reason (people are poor judges of their own behaviour and have an incentive to present well), is a mark-scoring sentence.

Personality traits are enduring characteristics that describe an individual's behaviour. Allport's early work catalogued around 18,000 trait words, and Cattell's 16 Personality Factor model reduced them to sixteen source traits. Modern OB uses the MBTI for discussion and the Big Five for anything serious.

2.3Type A and Type B Personality

Developed by the cardiologists Meyer Friedman and Ray Rosenman, who noticed that the chairs in their waiting room wore out only at the front edge, because their heart patients sat on the edge of the seat. Include that detail; it makes the answer memorable.

FeatureType A personalityType B personality
Core driveChronic time urgency and competitivenessRelaxed, easy-going
PaceAlways moving, walks and eats rapidlyUnhurried
Attitude to timeImpatient with the rate at which events happenComfortable waiting
MultitaskingStrives to do two or more things at onceDoes one thing at a time
LeisureCannot cope with leisure; feels guilty restingCan relax without guilt
Measures success byNumbers: how much, how many, how fastBroader, less quantified terms
CompetitionCompetes with others and with self, even in triviaPlays for enjoyment, not to win
HostilityOften free-floating hostility and irritabilityEven-tempered
SpeechExplosive, emphatic, interrupts, finishes others' sentencesMeasured
Health riskHigher risk of coronary heart diseaseLower
Work strengthFast, high volume, thrives on deadlinesBetter on complex judgement and patience-heavy tasks
Example

Two audit managers on the same engagement. Ravi (Type A) clears three client visits in a day and finishes fieldwork two days early, but misses a disclosure issue because he skimmed the notes and interrupted the client mid-explanation. Anil (Type B) finishes on the deadline, sits with the client's finance head for an hour, and catches an undisclosed related-party transaction.

Neither is superior. Type A delivers under time pressure, Type B delivers under complexity. The managerial insight is job fit: put Type A on deadline-driven high-volume work and Type B on judgement-heavy, relationship-heavy work.

The refinement that lifts a ten-mark answer

Later research found that the hostility and anger component, not the time urgency or the ambition, is the part that actually predicts heart disease. So "Type A" as a single construct is now considered too broad. Mentioning this shows you have read beyond the definition.

Also worth naming: Type D personality (distressed), characterised by negative affectivity plus social inhibition. Such a person experiences worry and gloom but does not express it, which is associated with poor cardiac outcomes and low help-seeking at work.

2.4The Myers-Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI)

The most widely used personality instrument in the world, built on the work of Carl Jung and developed by Katharine Cook Briggs and Isabel Briggs Myers. It is a 100-question self-report instrument that classifies people on four dichotomies, producing 16 types.

One letter from each pair gives a four-letter type. The sixteen are ISTJ, ISFJ, INFJ, INTJ, ISTP, ISFP, INFP, INTP, ESTP, ESFP, ENFP, ENTP, ESTJ, ESFJ, ENFJ and ENTJ.

The four types Robbins describes, which are the ones to quote

INTJ
The Visionary

Original minds with great drive for their own ideas. Sceptical, critical, independent, determined, often stubborn.

ESTJ
The Organiser

Practical, realistic, factual, decisive. A natural head for business or mechanics, good at organising and running activities.

ENTP
The Conceptualiser

Quick, ingenious, good at many things, resourceful on challenging problems, but may neglect routine assignments.

ISTJ
The Doer

Serious, quiet, dependable, practical, orderly and logical. Earns success through concentration and thoroughness.

Example · the four letters in a real meeting

Four students plan the college fest. Priya (ENFP) generates fifteen theme ideas in ten minutes, gets everyone excited, and resists finalising anything. Arun (ISTJ) asks for the budget sheet, the venue permission status and last year's actual expenses. Nisha (ENTJ) assigns owners and deadlines within twenty minutes and pushes for a decision today. Sameer (ISFP) notices the volunteer team is exhausted and quietly rearranges the duty roster.

All four are necessary. Left to Priya alone nothing happens; left to Arun alone nothing new happens. That is the practical use of the MBTI: it builds tolerance for how differently colleagues work.

Criticisms · a ten-mark answer must include these
  1. It forces either-or categories. Most traits are normally distributed, so someone scoring just above the midpoint on Thinking is labelled "T" and someone just below is "F", even though they are nearly identical.
  2. Poor test-retest reliability. A large share of people get a different type on retesting weeks later.
  3. Weak predictive validity. There is little evidence that type predicts job performance, so it must not be used for selection.
  4. No unfavourable outcome. Every type is described positively, which makes it popular but unfalsifiable and flattering.
  5. Barnum effect. The descriptions are broad enough that almost anyone recognises themselves in them.
The balanced conclusion to write

MBTI is useful as a self-awareness and team-discussion tool, because it gives people a shared, non-judgemental vocabulary for their differences. It is not acceptable as a hiring or promotion instrument, for which the Big Five is the evidence-based alternative.

One example per trait
  • Conscientiousness: two interns are told to prepare a reconciliation by Friday. One starts Monday with a checklist, the other starts Thursday night. Nothing about their ability differs.
  • Emotional stability: the server crashes an hour before a client presentation. One team member starts troubleshooting; another begins assigning blame.
  • Extraversion: at a networking event one person has spoken to nine people, another to one person for forty minutes. The second is not worse at networking; depth is also useful.
  • Openness: when the firm shifts to a new ERP, one employee is exploring its features on day two while another is still printing the old paper form.
  • Agreeableness: in an appraisal one manager can say "this is below standard" clearly. A highly agreeable manager says "overall it is fine", and the employee never improves.
The agreeableness paradox · the best point in this topic

High agreeableness makes you a better colleague but often a less successful careerist, because agreeable people ask for less, avoid confrontation and are reluctant to claim credit. The practical implication is not to become disagreeable. It is to learn two specific skills that do not come naturally to agreeable people: negotiating for oneself, and giving hard feedback.

Other OB-relevant personality attributes

Beyond the traits the syllabus names, Robbins lists these, and they are useful in a "personality traits relevant to OB" question:

  • Self-monitoring: the ability to adjust behaviour to situational factors. High self-monitors read a room and adapt, get better appraisals and emerge as leaders, but may be seen as inconsistent.
  • Risk-taking propensity: willingness to take chances, and it should be matched to the job. A high risk-taker suits entrepreneurship and trading; a low risk-taker suits audit, compliance and safety.
  • Proactive personality: identifies opportunities, shows initiative, takes action and persists until change occurs.

2.6Locus of Control

Definition

Locus of control is the degree to which people believe they are masters of their own fate.

Julian Rotter
CharacteristicInternalsExternals
Explanation for failure"I did not prepare enough""The paper was unfair", "the examiner was biased"
Job satisfactionHigherLower
AbsenteeismLowerHigher
TurnoverWill quit a bad job, believing they can change their situationStay in bad jobs, not believing quitting would help
Health behaviourMore likely to act on medical adviceLess likely, since outcomes feel out of their hands
Motivation and effortHigher, because effort is seen to matterLower, effort seen as pointless
Response to supervisionResent close supervision; perform better with autonomyMore comfortable with structure and direction
Information seekingActively seek information to improvePassive
Suitable jobsComplex, professional, managerial, sales, entrepreneurial work requiring initiativeHighly structured, routine, closely supervised work
Main riskOver-confidence; self-blame for things genuinely outside their controlLearned helplessness: stop trying even when they could succeed
Example

Two salespeople both miss their quarterly target by 20 per cent. Ramesh (internal): "My pipeline was thin because I stopped prospecting in month two. I am blocking two hours daily for calls from Monday." He changes behaviour. Suresh (external): "The market is down, the pricing is uncompetitive and the leads HR gave us were rubbish." Some of that may even be true, but nothing in his behaviour changes.

Next quarter Ramesh improves and Suresh does not. That is why locus of control predicts performance.

The qualification worth adding

An internal locus is generally more adaptive, but not universally. In situations that genuinely are outside one's control, a mass layoff or a natural disaster, internals can suffer more because they wrongly blame themselves. The healthiest position is accurate attribution, which connects directly to attribution theory in Unit 3.

Managerial application

For internals, give autonomy, goal-setting freedom and performance-linked rewards, and avoid micromanagement. For externals, provide clear structure, frequent feedback and visible cause-and-effect links between effort and reward, so the belief that effort matters can be learned rather than assumed.

2.7Core Self-Evaluation (CSE)

Definition

Core self-evaluation is the bottom-line conclusion individuals hold about their own capabilities, competence and worth as a person.

People with positive CSE like themselves, see themselves as capable and effective, and believe they control their environment. People with negative CSE dislike themselves, doubt their capability, and see themselves as powerless.

A common five-mark question

Distinguish self-esteem from self-efficacy. Self-esteem is about worth: "I am a good person." Self-efficacy is about capability on a specific task: "I can pass this exam." You can hold high self-esteem and low self-efficacy at once, for instance a confident person who genuinely cannot swim.

OutcomeEffect of positive CSE
Job performanceHigher, because they set more ambitious goals, commit to them and persist longer
Job satisfactionHigher. They see more challenge and meaning in the same job, so two people in identical roles report different satisfaction
Goal commitmentStronger
PersistenceThey keep going after setbacks instead of concluding they are incapable
Career successHigher salary and faster advancement over a career
Customer serviceBetter, because they do not take rudeness personally
Example

Two management trainees get the same stretch assignment: build a competitor analysis in five days with no template.

Positive CSE: "This is a chance to show what I can do. I will figure out the structure." Sets an ambitious scope, asks for help without embarrassment, delivers something imperfect but useful.

Negative CSE: "They gave this to me to expose me. I will probably get it wrong." Sets a minimal scope, does not ask questions for fear of looking incapable, delivers late and thin.

The task was identical. The self-evaluation produced the difference in both effort and outcome.

The downside

Very high CSE can become overconfidence and narcissism. Such people over-commit, ignore contrary feedback and blame the environment when a goal fails. So the relationship between CSE and performance is positive but not unlimited. The phrase to use: CSE helps most when it is accurate and hurts when it is inflated.

2.8Machiavellianism

Definition

Machiavellianism is the degree to which an individual is pragmatic, maintains emotional distance, and believes that ends can justify means.

Named after Niccolò Machiavelli, whose 16th-century book The Prince advised rulers on acquiring and holding power
CharacteristicWhat it looks like at work
Pragmatic and manipulativeTreats people as instruments for achieving objectives
Emotionally detachedDoes not let sentiment interfere with a decision, including firing a friend
Ends justify meansWill mislead, flatter or withhold information if it works
Strategic and patientPlans, builds alliances, plays long games
Cynical about human natureAssumes everyone else is equally self-interested
Low conventional moralityTreats rules as constraints to be managed, not obligations
Genuinely effectiveWins more and persuades more in certain situations, which is the uncomfortable finding
Example

Two managers want the same scarce budget allocation. Anita (low Mach) writes a well-argued proposal on merit and submits it. Vikram (high Mach) first finds out informally which director has the final say, learns that the director is worried about a compliance risk, reframes his own project as a compliance solution, casually mentions to a third party that Anita's project has "timeline concerns" so the remark reaches the director without attribution, and gets the budget.

Vikram's project may even be the worse one. He is simply better at getting it funded.

Is high Machiavellianism good or bad for the organisation?

The case for
Where it can help
  • Negotiation and bargaining roles, where strategic information control is normal
  • Commission-based selling
  • Turnaround and restructuring, which needs someone who can take unpopular decisions without paralysis
The case against
Where it damages
  • Erodes trust, the currency of every long-term relationship
  • Associated with counterproductive work behaviour, bullying and unethical decisions
  • Corrodes teamwork, because hoarding information is rational for a high Mach
  • Ethical exposure: most frauds involve someone who sincerely believed the end justified the means
The judgement to write

High Machs can produce short-term wins in unstructured, face-to-face, high-stakes situations. Over the long run the organisation pays in trust, ethics and turnover. The managerial response is therefore structural rather than personal: clear rules, documented decisions, segregation of duties, multiple approvals and transparent criteria. Each of those removes one of the three conditions in which Machiavellianism thrives.

2.9Narcissism

Definition

Narcissism describes a person who has a grandiose sense of self-importance, requires excessive admiration, has a sense of entitlement, and lacks empathy.

From the Greek myth of Narcissus, who fell in love with his own reflection
CharacteristicBehaviour at work
GrandiosityOverstates own abilities and achievements; talks about own contribution constantly
Need for admirationFishes for praise and reacts badly to its absence
Sense of entitlementExpects promotions, exceptions and special treatment as a right
Lack of empathyGenuinely does not register the effect of their behaviour on others
ExploitativeUses others' work and takes the credit
ArroganceDismisses those considered beneath them
Fragile self-imageAggressive or vindictive when criticised, because the grandiosity is defensive
Charm at firstInterviews and first impressions are excellent, which is exactly why they get hired and promoted
Example

A newly hired department head presents his first 100-day plan. He is articulate and confident, and describes his previous turnaround in detail using "I" throughout. Senior management is impressed.

Six months later: two capable team members have resigned, both saying their work was presented as his; he has cancelled the weekly review because "the team keeps raising negatives"; he has committed to a launch date he was warned twice was impossible, because backing down would look weak; and he responded to the first honest feedback in his 360-degree review by trying to identify who wrote it.

Nothing about his ability changed between month one and month six. Only the length of observation changed.

The Dark Triad

Machiavellianism and narcissism are two of three socially undesirable traits studied together.

An emerging extension adds sadism, making the Dark Tetrad.

What a manager should actually do about dark-triad traits

  1. Screen properly. Interviews reward exactly the surface charm these traits provide, so rely on structured interviews, work samples, reference checks focused on how the person treats others, and observer ratings rather than self-report.
  2. Use 360-degree feedback. Subordinates and peers see these traits long before the boss does.
  3. Build structural controls. Multiple approvals, documented decisions and segregation of duties limit the damage any one person can do.
  4. Measure behaviour, not only results. If the appraisal counts only numbers, exploitative behaviour is rewarded by design.
  5. Protect psychological safety. Low-safety environments are where these traits go undetected longest, because nobody tells the boss.

Case study practice · Section C style

A fast-growing logistics start-up promotes Karthik to head the Bengaluru hub on the strength of a brilliant interview and one visible success. Within eight months hub throughput has risen 18 per cent, and attrition among hub supervisors has risen from 9 per cent to 41 per cent. Karthik works fourteen-hour days, walks and talks fast, interrupts constantly, and has cancelled the weekly floor meeting because "it was full of excuses". He describes the throughput gain as "what I built here". Two supervisors resigned citing that their process redesign was presented to the CEO as his. A third says she raised a vehicle safety issue twice and was told not to be negative. In his own appraisal Karthik rates himself 5 out of 5 and attributes the attrition to "the quality of people HR hires".

(a) Analyse Karthik's personality using the concepts in this unit. (b) Why did the organisation not see this coming? (c) What should the company do now?

Answer skeleton for the case

(a) The diagnosis. Strong Type A markers: time urgency, rapid speech, interrupting, fourteen-hour days. Narcissism: grandiosity ("what I built here"), taking credit for subordinates' work, self-rating of 5 out of 5, punishing feedback by cancelling the meeting and labelling a safety report as negativity. External locus of control for failure and internal for success, which is the self-serving bias from Unit 3: the throughput gain is his, the attrition is HR's fault. Low agreeableness. Possibly inflated rather than accurate core self-evaluation. The safety issue that was suppressed shows destroyed psychological safety from Unit 1.

(b) Why it was missed. The selection decision rested on an interview and one visible result, which are precisely the two signals narcissism inflates. Charm is front-loaded, so short observation windows favour it. The appraisal measured only throughput, so exploitative behaviour was rewarded and the 41 per cent attrition never entered his score. No 360-degree feedback, so the people who could see the behaviour had no channel that reached above him.

(c) Recommendations. Immediately act on the suppressed safety report, since that is a live risk and not a personnel matter. Introduce 360-degree feedback and put retention and safety into his KRAs alongside throughput, so the scorecard stops rewarding the behaviour. Coaching with a specific, behavioural brief rather than a personality label, because narcissistic traits respond poorly to being named. Job-fit reasoning: his Type A profile suits a deadline-driven individual contributor or crisis role better than a people-leadership role. For future hiring, use structured interviews, work samples and reference checks that ask how the candidate treats juniors.
