Foundations in Group Behaviour and Team Dynamics

A group is more than a crowd, and a team is more than a group. This unit moves from how groups form and behave to how real teams are built.

CO4 · L4 Analyse Anchor text: Aswathappa Depth follows class notes Skill Development

4.1Defining and Classifying Groups

Definition

A group is two or more people who interact, depend on each other, see themselves as members, and come together for a shared objective.

The four criteria are what separate a group from a crowd:

  • Interaction: members communicate and respond to one another.
  • Interdependence: they rely on each other to get things done.
  • Psychological awareness: each is aware of the others as members.
  • Shared objective: they come together for a common goal.

A crowd at a bus stop fails all four. A project team that plans, argues and delivers together meets all four.

Example

A WhatsApp group that only shares the odd job lead fails interdependence and shared objective. It is a network, not a group in the OB sense.

Types of groups

  • Formal groups are created by the organisation. Command groups are a manager and direct reports. Task groups are people pulled together for a specific job.
  • Informal groups emerge naturally. Interest groups form around a shared cause. Friendship groups form around shared identity or liking.

Why people join groups

Security, status, self-esteem, affiliation, power and goal achievement.

4.2Tuckman's Model of Group Development

Bruce Tuckman described five stages that most groups pass through.

  • Forming: polite, uncertain, testing the waters. Who is here and why.
  • Storming: conflict over roles, control and direction. The hardest stage.
  • Norming: shared norms and cohesion settle in. The group gels.
  • Performing: energy goes into the task. The group works.
  • Adjourning: the temporary group wraps up and disbands.
Exam tip · adjourning is not the same for every group

A project task force truly adjourns, so the manager should recognise the work, debrief lessons and help members move on. A permanent department rarely disbands, so instead of closure the manager manages handover and renewal.

4.3Factors Influencing Group Behaviour

Three sets of factors shape how a group behaves.

  • External conditions: the organisation's strategy, authority structures, rules, resources, reward systems, culture and physical setting.
  • Group member resources: the abilities and personalities members bring.
  • Group structure: roles, norms, status, size and cohesiveness. These are the group properties in 4.4.
Example

Two sales teams have equally able people. One works under a reward system that pays only individual commission, the other shares a team bonus. The second team helps each other far more. The external condition, not the people, made the difference.

4.4Group Properties

Five structural properties decide whether a group helps or hurts performance: roles, norms, status, size and cohesiveness.

Property 1 · Roles

A role is the set of behaviours expected of someone in a given position. Five ideas to know:

  • Role identity: the attitudes and behaviours that go with a role.
  • Role perception: your own view of how you should act in the role.
  • Role expectations: how others believe you should act.
  • Psychological contract: the unwritten agreement on what employer and employee expect from each other.
  • Role conflict: when meeting one role's demands makes it hard to meet another's.

Group roles fall into three families. Task roles get the work done ("Let me summarise what we have decided"). Maintenance roles keep relationships healthy (encouraging, easing tension). Self-oriented roles serve the individual at the group's cost (blocking, seeking attention).

Property 2 · Norms

Norms are shared standards of acceptable behaviour. Performance norms are the most powerful, because they set how hard members work and what output is normal.

Conformity is the pressure that pulls members toward the group standard. Solomon Asch showed it with a simple line-matching test: many people gave an answer they knew was wrong because everyone else in the room gave it.

Property 3 · Status

Status is a rank given to a member by the group. It can be formal (title, designation) or informal (experience, skill, personality). When a person's status does not match what the group thinks they deserve, the mismatch causes friction.

Property 4 · Size

Bigger groups are better for fact-finding, because they bring more information. Smaller groups are better for action, because they decide and act faster.

Larger groups also invite social loafing, where people put in less effort in a group than alone. This is the Ringelmann effect: in his rope-pulling tests, total effort rose as people were added, but each person pulled less.

Property 5 · Cohesiveness

Cohesiveness is how strongly members are attracted to the group and want to stay in it. Its effect on output depends on the group's performance norms.

Common mistake

Cohesiveness alone does not raise output. A close-knit group with low performance norms sticks together to work less, which is why productivity drops.

4.5Group Decision-Making: Groupthink and Group Shift

Groups versus individuals

Strengths of group decisions
  • More complete information
  • More viewpoints
  • Higher acceptance
  • More legitimacy
Weaknesses of group decisions
  • Slower
  • Conformity pressure
  • Domination by a few
  • Unclear responsibility

Groupthink

Groupthink happens when the urge for agreement kills honest evaluation. Its symptoms:

  • Illusion of invulnerability: "We are a smart team, nothing can go wrong."
  • Rationalising warnings: bad data is explained away.
  • Pressure on dissenters: anyone raising problems is called negative.
  • Self-censorship: members keep their doubts to themselves.
  • Illusion of unanimity: silence is read as agreement.
  • Mindguards: members who shield the group from bad news.

Two simple remedies: assign someone to argue the opposite case (a devil's advocate), and invite an outside expert to review the plan before the group commits.

Group shift and polarisation

Discussion pushes members toward a more extreme version of their starting view. Often this is a risky shift: the group backs a bolder choice than any member would alone, because responsibility feels shared.

Example

A firm wants sales forecasts from experts in five cities who should not influence each other. Delphi fits: each sends answers, sees the summary, and revises over a few rounds.

4.7Transactional Analysis

Transactional Analysis (TA) was developed by Eric Berne. It explains how people communicate by looking at the ego state each person speaks from.

Three ego states

  • Parent: taught behaviour. Judging or caring. "You should always respect your seniors."
  • Adult: rational, fact-based, here and now. "Let us check the numbers first."
  • Child: felt behaviour. Emotional, spontaneous or rebellious. "This is so unfair, I am not doing it."

Three types of transactions

  • Complementary: the reply comes from the ego state the sender expected. Communication is smooth.
  • Crossed: the reply comes from an unexpected ego state. Communication breaks down and conflict starts.
  • Ulterior: a hidden message sits under the surface message.

Life positions

People hold one of four basic positions about themselves and others: I'm OK, You're OK · I'm OK, You're not OK · I'm not OK, You're OK · I'm not OK, You're not OK. The healthiest is "I'm OK, You're OK".

4.8The Johari Window

Developed by Joseph Luft and Harrington Ingham. It has four panes, formed by what you know about yourself and what others know about you.

  • Open: known to you and to others.
  • Blind: others see it, you do not.
  • Hidden: you know it, others do not.
  • Unknown: neither side sees it yet.
Key takeaway

Feedback shrinks the blind pane. Self-disclosure shrinks the hidden pane. Both grow the open pane, which is where trust lives.

4.9Types of Teams

A team is a group whose members work intensely together on a common goal, with mutual accountability and complementary skills, so their combined output is greater than the sum of individual inputs. A group shares information. A team produces joint work.

The common types are problem-solving teams, self-managed teams, cross-functional teams and virtual teams. The syllabus names three:

  • Green teams: employee teams set up to cut the organisation's environmental impact, for example energy use, waste or travel.
  • Virtual teams: members in different locations who work together mainly through technology.
  • Cross-functional sustainability teams: members from different departments, such as operations, finance, HR and procurement, working on a sustainability goal that no single department can deliver alone.
Example

A company aiming to cut plastic packaging forms a cross-functional team: procurement finds new suppliers, operations tests the packing line, finance checks the cost, and marketing explains the change to customers.

4.10Creating Effective Teams

Team effectiveness depends on three sets of factors.

  • Context: adequate resources, clear leadership and structure, a climate of trust, and a reward system that recognises team contribution.
  • Composition: members with the right mix of abilities and personalities, clearly allocated roles, and a team size kept small enough to act.
  • Process: a common purpose, specific team goals, belief in the team's ability to succeed, and conflict kept to ideas rather than personalities.
Exam tip

Structure a 10-mark answer under these three headings. Link composition back to roles and size in 4.4, and process back to groupthink in 4.5.

4.11Teams and Collaborations

Collaboration is people or teams working jointly toward a shared outcome, often across department or company boundaries. It works when there is:

  • A shared goal that everyone understands.
  • Trust and open communication.
  • Clear roles, so effort is not duplicated.
  • Shared credit, so no one holds back.

Collaboration has a cost. Too many meetings and requests slow work down, so managers should collaborate where the task needs it, not by default.

4.12Technology-Enabled Collaboration

Teams now work together through tools such as video calls, chat platforms, shared documents and project management software.

Benefits
  • Members can work from anywhere
  • Wider talent pool
  • Faster sharing of information
  • A written record of decisions
Risks
  • Messages are misread without tone or body language
  • Trust builds more slowly
  • Remote members can be left out
  • Constant notifications cause overload
Key takeaway

Technology makes collaboration possible across distance, but the group basics still apply. Clear goals, clear roles and trust matter more, not less, when people rarely meet.

Revision and case practice

Five-mark questions

  1. Define a group. Explain the four criteria that make a collection of people a group.
  2. Distinguish between formal and informal groups with examples of each type.
  3. Why do people join groups?
  4. Explain role identity, role perception, role expectations, the psychological contract and role conflict.
  5. How does group size affect performance? Explain social loafing.
  6. List the symptoms of groupthink.
  7. Explain the four panes of the Johari Window.
  8. What are green teams, virtual teams and cross-functional sustainability teams?

Ten-mark questions

  1. Explain Tuckman's five stages of group development. How should a manager handle adjourning in a task force compared with a permanent department?
  2. Discuss the five group properties. Using the cohesiveness and performance norms matrix, explain why a cohesive group is not always a productive one.
  3. Compare group and individual decision-making. Explain groupthink and group shift, and suggest how to prevent groupthink.
  4. Explain the four techniques of group decision-making and the situation each one suits.
  5. Explain Transactional Analysis: ego states, types of transactions and life positions.
Case · The launch that nobody questioned

A six-member product team at a consumer goods firm has worked together for three years and is very close. The leader is popular and wants to launch a new snack before Diwali. A market survey warns of weak demand. One member says the numbers are probably wrong, and the group agrees. Priya raises the issue twice and is told she is "being negative". Another member had doubts but stayed quiet because no one else spoke. The team approves a launch bigger than any of them would have backed alone.

Question: Diagnose what went wrong and recommend changes.

Answer outline
  • Name it as groupthink and match the symptoms: rationalising warnings, pressure on dissenters, self-censorship, illusion of unanimity.
  • The bigger-than-individual launch is a risky shift, because responsibility felt shared.
  • High cohesiveness made conformity stronger.
  • Remedies: a devil's advocate, an outside expert review, and NGT so every member writes a view before discussion.
  • Conclude: the team's closeness is an asset only if its norms reward honest evaluation.
