Foundations in Group Behaviour and Team Dynamics

The move up to level two. Everything so far has been about one person. This unit explains what changes when people are put together: behaviour that no individual would choose becomes normal, decisions get worse in predictable ways, and the manager's lever shifts from the person to the structure around them.

4.1Defining and Classifying Groups

Definition

A group is defined as two or more individuals, interacting and interdependent, who have come together to achieve particular objectives.

Robbins and Judge

Three conditions in that definition, and all three are needed. People in a lift are not a group, because they are not interdependent and share no objective. Sixty students in a lecture hall are barely a group. Five students assigned one project are.

Formal and informal groups

Created by the organisation
Formal groups
  • Defined by the organisation's structure, with designated work assignments and tasks.
  • Behaviour is stipulated by and directed towards organisational goals.
  • Command group: individuals who report directly to a given manager. Determined by the organisation chart.
  • Task group: people working together to complete a job task. May cross command relationships.
Created by the members
Informal groups
  • Neither formally structured nor organisationally determined. They form naturally in response to the need for social contact.
  • Powerful, because they satisfy needs the formal structure ignores.
  • Interest group: people who affiliate to attain a specific objective they share, for example pressing for a change in leave policy.
  • Friendship group: formed because members share one or more common characteristics, such as age, region, language or support for a cricket team.
Exam tip

Add this line and it lifts the answer: informal groups are not a problem to be eliminated, they are a channel to be understood. They carry the real information flow, they set the actual performance norms, and they decide whether a change succeeds. A manager who tries to abolish informal groups loses the only reliable view of what employees actually think.

Why people join groups

Six reasons, which map neatly onto Maslow from Unit 2:

  • Security: reduced insecurity of standing alone. Strength in numbers.
  • Status: membership of a group others regard highly.
  • Self-esteem: a feeling of worth from belonging.
  • Affiliation: the social need for interaction, which connects to McClelland's nAff.
  • Power: what cannot be achieved alone often becomes possible collectively. The basis of unions.
  • Goal achievement: some tasks simply require more than one person's talent, knowledge or hands.

Group versus team

BasisWork groupWork team
GoalShare informationCollective performance
SynergyNeutral, and sometimes negativePositive
AccountabilityIndividualIndividual and mutual

The point most students miss

Teams are not automatically better than groups. Teamwork takes time, has coordination costs, and creates the conditions for social loafing and groupthink. For many tasks, individuals working separately and pooling their output is faster and produces better results. Calling something a team does not make it one, and using a team where a group would do is a genuine and common management error. Saying this shows judgement rather than enthusiasm.

4.2Tuckman's Model of Group Development

Bruce Tuckman's five-stage model is the most quoted framework in this unit. Learn the stages, what happens in each, and what the leader should do in each.

Example

A five-person CIA-III project group. Week one everyone agrees to "divide it equally" and nobody says what they actually want to do, which is forming politeness. Week two there is a real argument about whether to do primary research, and two members stop replying on the group chat: storming. Week three they settle that one person will own the data, two the writing, one the deck, one the presentation, and agree Sunday evening check-ins: norming. Weeks four and five the work actually gets done: performing. After the viva they realise they worked well together and are mildly sorry it is over: adjourning.

Most student groups fail because they try to skip from forming straight to performing, and the unresolved storming arrives on the last night.

Criticisms, and the alternative model
  • Not always sequential. Groups can be at two stages at once, and can regress. Adding a member sends a group back to forming.
  • Conflict is not confined to storming. It recurs throughout, and some conflict at the performing stage actually improves output.
  • It ignores organisational context, treating the group as if it existed in isolation from resources, structure and reward systems.
  • It does not describe temporary groups with deadlines, which is most project work.

For that last case, use the punctuated equilibrium model: temporary groups with deadlines set their direction in the first meeting, then enter a long period of inertia in which very little happens, hit a transition at roughly the midpoint of the allotted time which triggers major change, then settle into a second phase of inertia, and finish with a burst of accelerated activity in the final meeting. The midpoint is the key finding: groups become aware that time is limited only when half of it is gone, and that awareness, not the calendar, is what produces change. Anyone who has done a group assignment recognises this immediately.

4.3Factors Influencing Group Behaviour

This heading asks for the full model of group behaviour, so structure it in five layers rather than giving a loose list.

The synergy equation, worth quoting

Actual group performance = potential performance + process gains − process losses.

Process gains come from sharing information, catching each other's errors and building on ideas. Process losses come from coordination effort, social loafing, conformity pressure and conflict. A group underperforms not because the members are weak but because the losses exceed the gains, and almost every intervention in this unit is aimed at one side of that equation.

4.4Group Properties

Five properties shape group behaviour, and this section is the analytical core of the unit. Any case question about a dysfunctional team is answered by running these five.

Property 1 · Roles

Definition

A role is a set of expected behaviour patterns attributed to someone occupying a given position in a social unit.

  • Role perception: an individual's view of how they are supposed to act in a given situation, acquired from the stimuli around them.
  • Role expectations: how others believe a person should act in a given situation. In the workplace these are captured by the psychological contract, the unwritten agreement of mutual expectations between employee and employer.
  • Role conflict: a situation in which an individual faces divergent role expectations, so complying with one makes it difficult to comply with another.
  • Role ambiguity and role overload, from the stress section in Unit 2, belong here too.
Example · role conflict

A team leader promoted from within the team is now expected by management to enforce attendance strictly, and expected by her former peers to cover for them as she always did. Both expectations are legitimate. She cannot satisfy both, and whichever she chooses she will be judged badly by one side. That is role conflict, and it is a structural problem, not a failure of her character.

The power of roles · Zimbardo's prison experiment

Philip Zimbardo randomly assigned ordinary student volunteers to be prisoners or guards in a mock prison at Stanford. Within days the guards had become abusive and the prisoners passive and distressed, and the study had to be stopped early. The individuals had not changed. Only the role they were assigned had.

The methodology has since been seriously criticised, including the demand characteristics and the coaching of guards, so treat the specific findings with care. The broader point survives and is the examinable one: situational roles can override individual disposition, which is the fundamental attribution error from Unit 3 demonstrated at group level.

Property 2 · Norms

Definition

Norms are acceptable standards of behaviour within a group that are shared by the group's members. They tell members what they ought and ought not to do under certain circumstances.

Four common classes of norm:

01
Performance norms

How hard to work, what level of output is right, how quickly to respond. The most powerful class, because it directly sets productivity, and it is set by the group rather than by the manager.

02
Appearance norms

Dress, when to look busy, when it is acceptable to leave. Includes the unwritten rule about whether leaving at 6 pm is acceptable.

03
Social arrangement norms

Who eats with whom, who is included, how much socialising is expected.

04
Resource allocation norms

How work, equipment, good assignments and credit are distributed.

Conformity: the Asch studies

Solomon Asch asked subjects to match the length of a line against three comparison lines, a task so easy that error was almost impossible alone. When seated with confederates who all gave an obviously wrong answer aloud first, around a third of subjects' answers conformed to the wrong group answer. They could see the right answer. They gave the wrong one.

What to take from Asch

People conform to the norms of the groups they belong to, called reference groups, out of a desire for acceptance. Note the two mechanisms, because naming both is a mark-scoring distinction: normative influence is conforming to be accepted, so the person does not really believe it, while informational influence is conforming because you assume the group knows something you do not, and the person does come to believe it.

This is also the mechanism behind employee silence in Unit 1 and the failure to report misconduct in Unit 2. Asch's subjects were not weak people, and neither are the employees who stay quiet.

Deviant workplace behaviour, also called counterproductive or antisocial behaviour, is voluntary behaviour that violates significant organisational norms and threatens the organisation or its members. The important finding: it is much more likely in groups where it is normative. An individual who would never pilfer alone does so where the group treats it as a perk. Norms cut both ways.

Property 3 · Status

Definition

Status is a socially defined position or rank given to groups or group members by others.

Status characteristics theory says status derives from three sources:

  1. The power a person wields over others, since those who control the group's resources and outcomes are perceived as high status.
  2. A person's ability to contribute to the group's goals, so those whose contribution is critical have high status.
  3. An individual's personal characteristics, such as appearance, intelligence, confidence or, in many settings, age, caste, institution attended or accent.
Effect of statusWhat happens
Status and normsHigh-status members have more freedom to deviate from norms, and are given more latitude when they do. They also resist conformity pressure better
Status and group interactionHigh-status members speak more, criticise more, and are interrupted less. This lowers the group's creativity, because the best information is often held by low-status members who never get the floor
Status inequityPerceived inequity in status produces resentment and corrective behaviour. Equity theory applies to status just as it does to pay
Status and stigmatisationAssociation with a low-status member lowers one's own perceived status, which is a driver of exclusion
Example

In a project review, the partner speaks for eleven of the first fifteen minutes. The article assistant who has actually read every ledger says nothing. When finally asked, she says "no issues, sir." Three weeks later the issue surfaces.

Nobody behaved badly here, which is the point. Status differential produced an information loss, and that is why speaking last is a technique and not a courtesy. This is psychological safety from Unit 1 with its mechanism now visible.

The key concept · social loafing

Social loafing is the tendency for individuals to expend less effort when working collectively than when working individually.

Demonstrated by Max Ringelmann, whose rope-pulling experiments found that as group size increased, the total output rose but the output per person fell

Two explanations, and both are worth naming:

  • Dispersion of responsibility: when individual contribution cannot be identified, individual accountability disappears.
  • Equity-based: members believe others are not pulling their weight, so they reduce their own effort to restore what they see as fairness. This is the more common cause in student groups, and it is self-reinforcing.

Property 5 · Cohesiveness

Definition

Cohesiveness is the degree to which group members are attracted to each other and are motivated to stay in the group.

The finding students get wrong · cohesiveness does not by itself improve productivity

The relationship between cohesiveness and productivity is moderated by the group's performance norms. A cohesive group with low performance norms is the worst of the four possibilities, because the cohesion is now enforcing low output. Never write "cohesive groups are more productive" without this qualification.

Example · the worst quadrant in practice

A billing team of eight has worked together for six years. They are genuinely close, cover each other's leave, and eat lunch together every day. They also have an unwritten norm that roughly forty files a day is a fair output, and a new joinee who cleared sixty in her first week was told, pleasantly, not to "spoil the rate".

She now clears forty. Management's error would be to read this as a motivation problem in her. It is a norm problem in the group, and cohesiveness is what gives the norm its force. Coaching her will fail; the intervention has to be aimed at the norm.

How to increase cohesiveness

Make the group smaller, encourage agreement on goals, increase the time members spend together, increase the group's status and the perceived difficulty of joining it, stimulate competition with other groups, give rewards to the group rather than to individuals, and physically isolate the group. Note that several of these also increase the risk of groupthink, which is the next section, and that trade-off is worth stating.

Diversity and faultlines

From Unit 1: diversity harms cohesion and increases conflict in the short run, and improves decision quality on complex tasks in the longer run, provided the group survives the early friction. The specific risk is a faultline, a perceived division that splits a group into subgroups along more than one characteristic at once, for example the three older men from head office against the two younger women from the branch. Faultlines are far more damaging than diversity along a single dimension.

4.5Group Decision-Making: Groupthink and Group Shift

Strengths and weaknesses of group decisions

Strengths
Why use a group
  • More complete information and knowledge. Several people bring more than one.
  • Increased diversity of views, so more alternatives are considered.
  • Increased quality of the decision, on complex problems.
  • Increased acceptance of the solution, which is often the real reason to use a group. People who participated in a decision support its implementation. This is the strongest argument, and it belongs in Unit 5 on change too.
Weaknesses
Why not to
  • Time consuming. Groups are slower.
  • Conformity pressures, which suppress dissent.
  • Domination by a few members, usually the highest status rather than the best informed.
  • Ambiguous responsibility. In an individual decision, accountability is clear. In a group decision, it is shared and therefore nobody's.

Effectiveness versus efficiency. Group decisions are generally more accurate than the average individual in the group, though usually less accurate than the best individual. They are more creative and better accepted. They are almost always less efficient, since they consume far more total working hours. So the choice is not about quality alone; it is a trade-off against time.

A · Groupthink

Definition

Groupthink is a phenomenon in which the norm for consensus overrides the realistic appraisal of alternative courses of action.

Irving Janis

Janis studied major policy fiascos and found a common pattern: cohesive, insulated groups under pressure, led by a directive leader, suppressed doubt in favour of unanimity, and made decisions that in hindsight nobody could defend.

Example

A senior team decides to launch a product in a new state. The regional head privately thinks the distribution is not ready, but the CEO has already described the launch as "the obvious next step" and the last two people who raised concerns in that forum were told they were being negative. He says nothing. Two others also say nothing, each assuming from the silence that they are the only doubter.

The launch fails. Afterwards, three people say they had reservations. Note that a majority privately doubted the decision and it passed unanimously. That is groupthink precisely: symptoms 5, 6 and 7 operating together.

Preventing groupthink
  • Leader speaks last and withholds their own preference at the start, to avoid anchoring the group.
  • Appoint a devil's advocate, formally, so challenging is a role rather than an act of disloyalty.
  • Use impartial leadership and encourage active disagreement as the group's norm.
  • Monitor group size. Above about ten, self-expression falls and cohesion pressure rises.
  • Bring in outside experts and expose the decision to people with no stake in it.
  • Break into subgroups that work independently, then compare conclusions.
  • Hold a second-chance meeting after the decision has been provisionally taken.
  • Build psychological safety, which is the underlying condition for all of the above.

B · Group shift and groupolarisation

Definition

Group shift is a change between a group's decision and the individual decision that members within the group would make. The discussion tends to exaggerate the initial position of the members, so the group's decision is more extreme than the average of the individuals' initial positions.

The shift is usually towards greater risk, which is why it is often called the risky shift, though the direction actually follows the group's pre-existing leaning: a conservative group becomes more conservative, and a risk-inclined group becomes more risk-inclined. The general term is group polarisation.

Why it happens: diffusion of responsibility, since no individual carries the blame; discussion builds familiarity and mutual reinforcement, so members grow bolder; and members compete to be seen as holding the group's valued position, which pushes the average outward.

Example

Six managers are each asked privately what proportion of the marketing budget should go to an unproven digital channel. The individual answers average 18 per cent. After a forty-minute discussion in which two enthusiasts spoke most, the group commits 40 per cent.

Not one person in the room had originally proposed 40 per cent. If it fails, no individual owns the number, which is exactly the condition that allowed it.

Distinguishing the two, which is often asked

Groupthink is about the suppression of dissent in favour of consensus, and it produces a poorly examined decision. Group shift is about the amplification of the group's existing tendency, and it produces a more extreme decision. A decision can suffer from both at once, and typically does.

The finding worth stating

Brainstorming is the most famous technique and one of the least effective. Individuals working alone and then pooling their ideas, which is what a nominal group does, consistently generate more ideas and more original ones than the same people brainstorming together. The reason is production blocking plus residual evaluation apprehension. Knowing this, rather than just listing brainstorming as a solution, is what marks out a strong answer.

4.7Transactional Analysis

Developed by the psychiatrist Eric Berne, and popularised by Thomas Harris in I'm OK, You're OK. It is a model for understanding interpersonal communication, and in OB it is used for improving interactions between managers and subordinates.

The three ego states

Definition

An ego state is a consistent pattern of feeling, thinking and behaviour. Berne identified three: Parent, Adult and Child. Everyone has all three and switches between them.

Types of transaction

Life positions

Berne argued that people settle into one of four basic positions about themselves and others, formed early and carried into adult relationships.

Strokes and games

  • Strokes are units of recognition, the acknowledgement one person gives another. Positive strokes are praise and recognition; negative strokes are criticism. Berne's important claim is that people need strokes, and will provoke negative strokes rather than receive none at all. This explains the employee who behaves badly for attention, and it explains why being ignored is worse than being criticised. Strokes can also be unconditional, about the person, or conditional, about the behaviour, and conditional positive strokes are what good feedback consists of.
  • Games are repetitive, ulterior transaction sequences with a predictable payoff, and they always end with someone feeling bad. Common workplace games include "Yes, but", "Why don't you? Yes, but", "Now I've got you", "Kick me" and "Harried". Recognising a game is what allows a manager to refuse to play it.
  • Life script is the unconscious life plan formed in childhood that a person continues to act out.
Applications of TA in OB
  • Improving manager-subordinate communication. The practical rule is to aim for Adult-to-Adult transactions, and to recognise when you have slipped into Critical Parent, which reliably provokes Adapted Child in the other person.
  • Feedback and appraisal: giving conditional positive strokes about behaviour rather than unconditional judgements about the person.
  • Handling difficult conversations: refusing to complete a crossed transaction, by answering the Child provocation from Adult rather than escalating to Parent.
  • Conflict resolution and negotiation, which is Unit 5.
  • Customer service training, where the skill is staying in Adult while the customer is in Child.

Criticism to include: TA is intuitively appealing and widely used in training, but it has limited empirical support, its terminology is imprecise, and it can become a labelling exercise where colleagues are dismissed as "being Child" rather than listened to.

4.8The Johari Window

Developed by Joseph Luft and Harrington Ingham, and the name is a contraction of their first names, Joe and Harry. It is a model of self-awareness and interpersonal disclosure, built on two axes: what is known to self, and what is known to others.

Example · a team leader's window

Aditi has just taken over a team of six.

  • Open: everyone knows she is technically strong and works long hours.
  • Blind: the team finds her rapid-fire questioning intimidating and has stopped volunteering problems. She believes she is being thorough. She has no idea.
  • Hidden: she is anxious about managing people for the first time and has not told anyone.
  • Unknown: nobody, including her, knows how she will handle a serious client escalation.

Her blind spot is currently costing the team its voice, and only feedback can close it. Her hidden pane is costing her support she could have, and only disclosure can close it. A single honest conversation in each direction changes the whole window, which is why the model is used in team-building.

Applications and the caution

Uses: self-awareness development, team building at the norming stage, coaching, 360-degree feedback design, and improving trust in new teams.

The caution to state: the model assumes disclosure is safe, and it is not always. In a low-trust or politically charged workplace, enlarging the hidden pane exposes the employee to real risk, and a manager who runs a disclosure exercise without first establishing psychological safety is asking people to take a risk the environment does not support. Disclosure should be reciprocal and gradual, and the senior person should go first.

Their distinctive challenge is that coordination has to happen between teams, and that is where such systems usually fail.

The three types the syllabus names specifically

Green teams

Definition

A green team is a group of employees, usually cross-functional and often voluntary, formed to identify, promote and implement environmental improvements in the organisation's operations.

Typical activities: waste and energy audits, running awareness campaigns, collecting and screening employee suggestions, tracking consumption data, and championing green practices within their own departments.

Why they work, in OB terms: they convert environmental behaviour from an individual decision into a group norm, they supply the peer modelling that section 3.7 showed is the most powerful learning route for green behaviour, and they create feedback loops by reporting consumption data back to the teams that generated it.

Why green teams usually fail
  • No authority and no budget, so they become a problem-solving team whose recommendations are ignored. This is the single most common cause.
  • No time allocated, so membership becomes unpaid extra work on top of a full role, and the committed people burn out first.
  • No senior sponsor, so the team cannot cross departmental boundaries.
  • Volunteers only, which means the team contains the already-converted and never reaches the departments that matter most.
  • No measurement, so nobody can show that anything changed, and the team cannot justify its own existence at the next budget round.

Cross-functional sustainability teams

A green team with real teeth. These are permanent, formally mandated bodies drawing members from operations, procurement, finance, HR, legal and compliance, with a budget, a senior sponsor and reporting obligations.

Why the cross-functional form is necessary rather than merely desirable: almost no sustainability problem sits inside one function. Cutting packaging waste needs procurement to change specifications, operations to change the line, finance to accept a higher unit cost, marketing to handle the appearance change, and legal to check labelling compliance. A single-function team can identify the problem and cannot solve it. In India this structure is increasingly driven by BRSR reporting obligations, which require data that no single department holds.

Their characteristic difficulty is the one noted above: members carry functional loyalties and are appraised by their own department heads, so the sustainability goal competes with the goal they are actually paid on. The fix is structural, namely putting the sustainability target into each member's own KRA.

4.10Creating Effective Teams

The standard model has three components, and this is the framework to reproduce for any "how do you build an effective team" question.

Belbin's team roles, a useful addition

Meredith Belbin found that effective teams need a balance of nine behavioural roles rather than nine similar high performers. Naming a few shows reading beyond the prescribed text.

OrientationRolesContribution
Action orientedShaper, Implementer, Completer-FinisherDrives the team on, turns ideas into action, and makes sure nothing is left undone
People orientedCoordinator, Teamworker, Resource InvestigatorOrganises and delegates, keeps the team together, and brings in outside contacts and options
Thought orientedPlant, Monitor-Evaluator, SpecialistGenerates creative ideas, judges options dispassionately, and supplies deep expertise

The practical point: a team of nine Plants generates brilliant ideas and finishes nothing. A team of nine Implementers executes the wrong plan flawlessly.

Turning individuals into team players

  • Selection: hire for team skills, not only technical ability, and be honest with candidates who genuinely prefer solo work.
  • Training: in problem solving, communication, negotiation, conflict management and coaching.
  • Rewards: restructure so cooperation is rewarded. This is the lever that actually changes behaviour, and it is the one organisations most often leave untouched while exhorting people to collaborate.
The cultural qualification

Team-based working was developed largely in individualist, low power distance settings. In a high power distance culture, self-managed teams struggle because members wait for direction and are reluctant to challenge a senior member, and in a strongly collectivist setting, individual accountability mechanisms such as peer evaluation can feel like betrayal. India scores high on power distance and leans collectivist, so team designs imported without adaptation predictably underperform. That observation earns marks in a CO4 question.

4.11Teams and Collaborations

Collaboration is not a synonym for teamwork. The distinction is worth making precisely, because the syllabus treats it as a separate heading.

TermWhat it involvesExample
CoordinationAligning separate work so the pieces fit. Each party keeps its own goal and its own workAudit and tax agreeing a timetable so they do not both visit the client in the same week
CooperationHelping one another with work that remains separately ownedOne department lending a resource to another during a peak
CollaborationShared goal, shared ownership of the outcome, joint work on the same problem, and mutual accountability. The parties produce something neither could produce aloneProcurement, operations and finance jointly redesigning packaging to cut waste and cost

Conditions that make collaboration work

  • A genuinely shared goal, not two goals with a shared meeting.
  • Aligned incentives. If each party is appraised only on its own function's metric, collaboration is an act of personal sacrifice, and asking people to be selfless is a poor substitute for fixing the scorecard.
  • Clear decision rights. Most collaboration stalls not from ill will but because nobody knows who decides when the parties disagree.
  • Trust and psychological safety, which allow parties to admit constraints and errors.
  • Boundary spanners: individuals who have credibility in both groups and translate between them. Naming this role is a good detail.
  • A shared mental model of the problem, from 4.10.
  • Executive sponsorship with authority to resolve cross-functional deadlock.
Collaborative overload · the honest counterpoint

Time spent in collaborative activity, meetings, calls and messages, has risen enormously, and research finds a small proportion of employees, typically the most competent and helpful, absorb a disproportionate share of collaborative requests. The consequences are real: their own deliverables slip, they work longer to compensate, and they are at high risk of burnout from Unit 2.

So more collaboration is not automatically better. The managerial task is to be selective about where collaboration is genuinely required, to protect the people who are over-consulted, and to notice that the person everyone asks for help is the person whose own work is quietly failing.

The most useful distinction to make

Synchronous versus asynchronous is the decision that matters most in practice. Synchronous work builds relationships, resolves ambiguity fast and is necessary for negotiation, conflict and sensitive feedback. Asynchronous work respects time zones and deep work, produces a written record, and lets thoughtful people contribute properly rather than rewarding whoever speaks fastest.

Most dysfunctional virtual teams have the balance backwards: they hold synchronous meetings for status updates, which should be written, and handle disagreements over message threads, which should be a call. Saying this converts a tool list into an argument.

Benefits

  • Access to talent irrespective of location, which is also a diversity gain.
  • Cost reduction in travel and real estate.
  • Speed and round-the-clock working across time zones.
  • A written record, which improves accountability and organisational memory.
  • Levelling effects. Written and anonymous channels reduce the status effects from 4.4, so quieter and more junior members contribute more. This is a real and underrated benefit.
  • Inclusion for employees with disabilities, caregiving responsibilities or mobility constraints.
  • Reduced environmental impact from avoided travel and office energy, which links this heading to the green theme.

Challenges

ChallengeWhy it happensWhat to do
Trust is harder to buildTrust normally develops through informal, unplanned contact, which virtual work eliminates. Virtual teams rely instead on swift trust, based on role and reputation, which is fragileFront-load one in-person or high-contact session at the forming stage; deliberately schedule informal time; be scrupulous about keeping small commitments, since reliability substitutes for familiarity
Loss of non-verbal cuesTone, hesitation and body language carry much of the meaning, and text removes almost all of it. Ambiguous messages are read negatively by defaultEstablish explicit norms for digital body language: response-time expectations, when to use which channel, cameras on or off, and over-communicating intent in writing
MisattributionA silent colleague is assumed to be uninterested rather than in a different time zone or a bad network. This is the fundamental attribution error from Unit 3, amplified by the absence of situational informationMake context visible: shared calendars, status, and stated working hours
Weaker social rapport and isolationLess spontaneous interaction; new joiners in particular have no informal networkStructured onboarding buddies, regular non-work contact, deliberate inclusion of remote members in decisions
Proximity biasIn hybrid settings, people physically present get more informal contact, more visibility and more opportunity, so remote members are systematically disadvantaged regardless of outputRun hybrid meetings as if everyone is remote; measure output rather than presence; audit who is actually getting promoted
Coordination and monitoring costProgress is not observable, so it must be reportedMake work visible on a shared board rather than increasing surveillance, which is the technostress trap from Unit 2
Meeting fatigueContinuous video calls with no transitions, plus the cognitive load of watching oneself and reading many faces at onceShorter default meeting lengths, cameras optional, buffers between calls, and moving status updates to writing

Example

A product team of nine is split between Bengaluru and Pune. All decisions are taken in a 9:30 am daily call. Two Pune members, who have a longer commute and school drop-offs, join late and stop contributing. Over three months the informal view in Bengaluru becomes that "Pune is not engaged", and the next two promotions go to Bengaluru members.

Diagnose it: proximity bias and a synchronous-only design, producing a faultline along location, reinforced by misattribution of a scheduling constraint as an attitude, ending in a real inequity. The fix is not a team-building event. It is to move the decision record to writing so contribution is possible asynchronously, rotate the meeting time so the burden alternates, and audit the promotion pattern.

Case study practice · Section C style

A manufacturer sets up a twelve-member "Sustainability Council" to hit a target of cutting packaging waste 25 per cent in one year. Members are volunteers, drawn mostly from HR and CSR, plus one junior from operations. It is chaired by the Managing Director, who opens each monthly meeting by stating his own view of the best option. Membership is unpaid and on top of normal duties. The council has no budget and can only make recommendations to the functional heads.

Ten months on: waste is down 3 per cent. Four of the twelve have stopped attending, and the junior operations member has not spoken in the last five meetings. In the second month the council unanimously endorsed the MD's preferred option, a switch to a new material, over the alternative of redesigning the carton, although two members have since said privately they always thought the redesign was better. Procurement declined to change the supplier specification, citing cost. Two of the most active members have taken extended sick leave. A senior manager told the CSR head, "that council is a CV-building exercise".

(a) Diagnose the council using the group and team concepts in this unit. (b) Why did the decision in month two go the way it did? (c) Redesign the council.

Answer skeleton for the case

(a) Diagnosis. Size: twelve exceeds the effective range of five to nine, and above about ten self-expression falls and loafing rises. Social loafing: four have stopped attending, contributions are not identifiable, membership is voluntary and unrewarded, so there is no individual accountability, which is the textbook condition. Composition: this is not genuinely cross-functional. It is an HR and CSR group with one token operations member, so it lacks the functional authority to change anything, which is why procurement could simply refuse. Status: a single junior among senior members, chaired by the MD, produces exactly the predicted outcome, that the lowest-status member with the most relevant shop-floor knowledge has fallen silent. Type of team: it is a problem-solving team with recommendation rights only, presented as if it were a cross-functional sustainability team, so its recommendations die at the functional heads. Context, in team-effectiveness terms: no adequate resources (no budget, no time allocated), no leadership structure with decision rights, and the reward system does not recognise participation at all. Collaborative overload and burnout: the two most active members on extended sick leave is the classic pattern, that the willing absorb the load until they break. Norms and legitimacy: "a CV-building exercise" tells you the organisational norm now treats the council as decorative, which is the green-team failure mode.

(b) The month-two decision. This is groupthink, and the mechanism is identifiable. The MD, a directive leader, states his preference first, which anchors the group (Unit 3) and creates direct pressure on dissenters. High status differential plus low psychological safety produces self-censorship, symptom 6. Because nobody objects aloud, each doubter assumes the others agree, which is the illusion of unanimity, symptom 7. The two members who privately preferred the carton redesign are the proof: a unanimous decision that a substantial minority did not actually support. There is also a plausible group shift element, in that the group committed to a more sweeping option, a full material change, than individuals would have chosen alone, with diffused responsibility making that comfortable.

(c) Redesign. Structure: cut to seven members, drawn deliberately from operations, procurement, finance, R&D, quality, HR and CSR, so every function whose agreement is required is inside the room rather than outside it. Give it a budget and decision rights within defined limits, so it stops being a recommending body. Appoint a senior sponsor who is not the chair. Process: the MD should not chair and should not speak first; adopt a rule that the most senior person speaks last, appoint a rotating devil's advocate, use the nominal group technique for option generation so members write and rank independently before discussion, and hold a second-chance meeting before committing. Accountability: allocate named owners and deadlines for each action, so contribution is identifiable and loafing has nowhere to hide. Put the packaging target into the KRA of each member and of their functional head, which is the only thing that resolves the functional-loyalty problem and is why procurement refused. Resources: allocate formal time, and treat council work as part of the role rather than additional to it, which is also the burnout fix. Measurement and feedback: publish monthly waste data by line and by team, supplying the feedback loop that makes the behaviour learnable (Unit 3). Voice: protect the operations member's contribution explicitly, since that person holds the information the council most needs, and consider written pre-reads so contribution does not depend on being willing to speak up in front of the MD.
