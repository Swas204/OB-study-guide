Foundations in Group Behaviour and Team Dynamics

A group is more than a crowd, and a team is more than a group. This unit moves from how groups form and behave to how real teams are built.

CO4 · L4 Analyse Anchor text: Aswathappa Depth follows class notes Skill Development

4.1Defining and Classifying Groups

Definition

A group is two or more people who interact, depend on each other, see themselves as members, and come together for a shared objective.

In OB the word "group" has a narrow meaning. People in the same place are not a group just because they are together. Four criteria must all be present:

  • Interaction: members talk to and respond to one another, in person or online. Without interaction, people are only working side by side.
  • Interdependence: each member's work depends on what the others do. If one person could finish the job without anyone else, there is no group task.
  • Psychological awareness: members see themselves as part of the group and know who else belongs. They start to think in terms of "we".
  • Shared objective: they are together for a common goal. The goal is what gives the interaction a purpose.

A crowd at a bus stop fails all four. A project team that plans, argues and delivers together meets all four.

Example

A WhatsApp group that only shares the odd job lead has interaction, and members know they are in it. But nobody depends on anyone else and there is no common goal. It fails interdependence and shared objective, so it is a network, not a group in the OB sense.

Types of groups

Formal groups are created by the organisation to get its work done. Management decides who belongs and what the group must achieve.

  • Command group: a manager and the people who report directly to them, as shown on the organisation chart. A branch manager and the branch staff form a command group.
  • Task group: people pulled together for a specific job, often from different departments. A committee set up to plan the annual audit is a task group, and it ends when the job is done.

Informal groups are not designed by management. They emerge naturally from people's own needs.

  • Interest group: people who join together for a shared cause, such as employees pressing for flexible working hours.
  • Friendship group: people who come together because they like each other or share an identity, such as colleagues from the same hometown who eat lunch together.

Why people join groups

  • Security: standing alone feels risky. A group gives members strength and makes them less anxious, for example during change.
  • Status: belonging to a group that others rate highly brings recognition.
  • Self-esteem: being accepted by a group makes people feel worthwhile.
  • Affiliation: people need social contact, and groups meet that need.
  • Power: what one person cannot get alone, a group often can. Numbers bring influence.
  • Goal achievement: some tasks need more skills or hands than one person has.

Once a group forms, it does not work well from the first day. Tuckman's model explains how it develops.

4.2Tuckman's Model of Group Development

Tuckman described five stages that most groups pass through. Each stage has its own mood, and the manager's job changes as the group moves on.

  • Forming: members are polite, cautious and unsure. They are testing the waters: who is here, why, and what behaviour is acceptable. They look to the leader for direction. The manager should set clear goals, introduce members and explain who does what.
  • Storming: once the politeness wears off, members clash over roles, control and direction. Who leads? Whose way of working wins? This is the hardest stage, because conflict can split the group. The manager should bring disagreements into the open, settle roles and keep attention on the goal.
  • Norming: roles are settled and members agree on how they will work. Shared norms form and members grow close, so cohesion settles in. The group gels. The manager should reinforce the useful norms and start to step back.
  • Performing: the group has accepted its structure, so energy moves away from sorting out relationships and into the task. The group works. The manager should delegate, provide resources and recognise results.
  • Adjourning: a temporary group finishes its task and disbands. Attention shifts to wrapping up. Some members feel proud of what they did, others feel a sense of loss.
Exam tip · adjourning is not the same for every group

A project task force truly adjourns, so the manager should recognise the work, debrief lessons and help members move on. A permanent department rarely disbands, so instead of closure the manager manages handover and renewal.

4.3Factors Influencing Group Behaviour

Two groups with similar tasks can behave very differently. Three sets of factors explain why.

  • External conditions: every group sits inside a larger organisation, and the organisation's conditions shape it.
    • The strategy decides what the group is expected to achieve.
    • Authority structures decide who reports to whom and who has the final say.
    • Rules set what members may and may not do.
    • Resources such as money, time and equipment limit what the group can attempt.
    • The reward system decides which behaviour pays.
    • Culture sets the unwritten standards everyone is expected to follow.
    • The physical setting, such as office layout, affects how easily members can meet and talk.
  • Group member resources: what each person brings. Members' abilities set the limit on what the group can do. Their personalities affect how well they get on, for example sociable members interact more freely.
  • Group structure: the internal shape of the group. It is made up of roles, norms, status, size and cohesiveness, which are the group properties in 4.4.
Example

Two sales teams have equally able people. One works under a reward system that pays only individual commission, the other shares a team bonus. The second team helps each other far more. The external condition, not the people, made the difference.

4.4Group Properties

Group structure is made of five properties: roles, norms, status, size and cohesiveness. Each one shapes how members behave, and together they decide whether a group helps or hurts performance.

Property 1 · Roles

A role is the set of behaviours expected of someone in a given position. Everyone plays several roles, and the group expects certain behaviour from each one. Five ideas build on each other:

  • Role identity: the attitudes and behaviours that go with a role. People adopt a new identity quickly when their role changes. A worker promoted to supervisor soon starts to see things from management's side.
  • Role perception: your own view of how you are supposed to act in the role. It comes from watching others, training and experience.
  • Role expectations: how others believe you should act. A manager is expected to be fair, a trainee is expected to learn.
  • Psychological contract: the unwritten agreement on what employer and employee expect from each other. The employee expects fair pay and treatment, the employer expects effort and loyalty. When either side breaks it, trust and performance fall.
  • Role conflict: when meeting the demands of one role makes it hard to meet another. A sales officer told to push targets by the manager and to give honest advice by customers faces role conflict.

Roles in a group also fall into three families. Task roles help the group reach its goal, such as giving information or summarising ("Let me summarise what we have decided"). Maintenance roles keep relationships healthy, such as encouraging others or easing tension. Self-oriented roles serve the individual at the group's cost, such as blocking ideas or seeking attention. A healthy group needs both task and maintenance roles, and the manager should check self-oriented ones.

Property 2 · Norms

Norms are shared standards of acceptable behaviour. They tell members what they should and should not do. Most are unwritten, and the group enforces them through approval or disapproval.

Performance norms are the most powerful. They tell members how hard to work, how much to produce and what quality is good enough. This is why a group of able people can still perform badly if its norm is "do not work too hard".

Norms work through conformity: members adjust their behaviour to match the group standard because they want to be accepted. Asch showed how strong this pull is. People were asked to match the length of lines. When others in the room confidently gave a wrong answer, many went along with it against the evidence of their own eyes.

For a manager, the lesson is to shape the group's performance norms as well as individual effort.

Property 3 · Status

Status is a rank or level of prestige the group gives a member. It comes from two places:

  • Formal status comes from the organisation, through title, designation and position.
  • Informal status comes from qualities the group itself values, such as experience, skill or personality.

Status matters because higher-status members usually have more say in the group. Problems start with a status mismatch: when a person's formal rank does not match what the group thinks they deserve, members see it as unfair and friction follows. A newly hired senior who knows less than the experienced junior they now direct is a common case.

Property 4 · Size

Size is a trade-off.

  • Bigger groups are better for fact-finding. More members bring more information, ideas and skills, which helps when the job is gathering facts or diagnosing a problem.
  • Smaller groups are better for action. With fewer people to coordinate, they decide and act faster.

Larger groups also invite social loafing: people put in less effort in a group than they would alone. Individual effort is harder to see, and responsibility feels shared, so each person eases off. This is the Ringelmann effect. In rope-pulling tests, total pull rose as people were added, but each person pulled less.

So a manager should keep action groups small and make each member's contribution visible.

Property 5 · Cohesiveness

Cohesiveness is how strongly members are attracted to the group and want to stay in it. A cohesive group sticks together. But cohesion does not decide which way the group pushes. It only makes members follow the group's norms more closely, so its effect on output depends on the performance norms.

  • High cohesion, high norms: members are close and committed to a high standard, so they push each other. Productivity rises strongly.
  • High cohesion, low norms: members are close, but the standard is to do little. They hold each other to that low standard, so productivity drops.
  • Low cohesion, high norms: the standard is high, but members are not strongly bound to the group. Some follow it, others do not, so there is a slight rise at best.
  • Low cohesion, low norms: there is no strong pull in either direction, so there is little effect.
Common mistake

Writing that cohesiveness raises output. It raises output only when performance norms are high. A manager should set high performance norms first, then build cohesion.

4.5Group Decision-Making: Groupthink and Group Shift

Groups versus individuals

Groups make many decisions in organisations. Compared with one person deciding alone, they have clear strengths and clear weaknesses.

Strengths of group decisions
  • More complete information: each member brings knowledge the others lack.
  • More viewpoints: more alternatives get considered.
  • Higher acceptance: people who took part support the decision.
  • More legitimacy: a group decision is seen as fairer than one person's call.
Weaknesses of group decisions
  • Slower: meeting and agreeing takes time.
  • Conformity pressure: members go along to fit in.
  • Domination by a few: loud or senior members steer the result.
  • Unclear responsibility: no one person is accountable.

Two of these weaknesses, conformity and shared responsibility, explain the two classic failures of group decisions: groupthink and group shift.

Groupthink

Groupthink happens when the urge for agreement kills honest evaluation. Members care more about staying united than about testing the decision, so weak ideas pass unchallenged. Its symptoms:

  • Illusion of invulnerability: the group feels it cannot fail, so it takes risks it should not. "We are a smart team, nothing can go wrong."
  • Rationalising warnings: information that challenges the plan is explained away instead of examined.
  • Pressure on dissenters: anyone raising problems is pushed to fall in line or labelled negative.
  • Self-censorship: members with doubts keep quiet and play down their own concerns.
  • Illusion of unanimity: because nobody objects, the group assumes everyone agrees. Silence is read as a yes.
  • Mindguards: members who shield the group or its leader from bad news that might upset the agreement.

Two simple remedies work because they make disagreement safe. Assign someone to argue the opposite case, a devil's advocate, so objections are raised without anyone being seen as disloyal. Invite an outside expert to review the plan, because an outsider is not bound by the group's wish to agree.

Group shift and polarisation

Group shift means that discussion pushes members toward a more extreme version of the view they started with. If members leaned slightly cautious, the group ends up more cautious. If they leaned slightly bold, it ends up bolder.

The shift is often a risky shift: the group backs a bolder choice than any member would alone. The reason is that responsibility feels shared. If the decision fails, no single person carries the blame, so each feels freer to accept the risk.

Exam tip · do not mix the two up

Groupthink is about agreement suppressing honest evaluation. Group shift is about the group's final position becoming more extreme than members' starting views. A question on the risky shift needs the "shared responsibility" reason.

4.6Techniques in Group Decision-Making

To keep the strengths of group decisions while limiting the weaknesses in 4.5, groups can use four techniques. Each one solves a different problem.

  • Interacting groups: the normal face-to-face meeting where members discuss freely. It is fast and natural, but it is the most exposed to conformity and to domination by a few.
  • Brainstorming: the aim is to generate as many ideas as possible. Members call out any idea, no criticism is allowed, unusual ideas are welcome, and quantity comes first. Judging the ideas is left for later, so fear of being judged does not hold people back.
  • Nominal Group Technique (NGT): members meet but first write their ideas down silently and on their own. Each then presents one idea at a time, the ideas are discussed only to clarify them, and every member ranks them privately. The idea with the highest total ranking is chosen. Because everyone writes before anyone speaks, it curbs domination.
  • Delphi technique: experts never meet. They answer a questionnaire, the answers are summarised and sent back, and they revise their views over several rounds. It suits members who are remote or should stay anonymous, though it is slow.
Example

A firm wants sales forecasts from experts in five cities who should not influence each other. Delphi fits: each sends answers, sees the summary, and revises over a few rounds.

4.7Transactional Analysis

Transactional Analysis (TA) was developed by Eric Berne. It studies transactions, the basic exchanges between two people: one person says something, the other replies. TA explains why some exchanges go smoothly and others go wrong by looking at the ego state each person speaks from.

Three ego states

An ego state is a consistent pattern of feeling, thinking and behaving. Everyone has all three and moves between them.

  • Parent: taught behaviour, copied from parents and authority figures. It can be judging (setting rules, criticising) or caring (protecting, helping). "You should always respect your seniors."
  • Adult: rational and fact-based, dealing with the here and now. It gathers information, weighs it and decides calmly. "Let us check the numbers first."
  • Child: felt behaviour, driven by emotion. It can be spontaneous (curious, playful) or rebellious. "This is so unfair, I am not doing it."

Three types of transactions

  • Complementary: the reply comes from the ego state the sender expected. "What time is the meeting?" "At three." Adult speaks to Adult and gets an Adult reply, so communication is smooth.
  • Crossed: the reply comes from an unexpected ego state. "What time is the meeting?" "Why do you always ask me? Check your mail." An Adult question gets a scolding Parent reply, so communication breaks down and conflict starts.
  • Ulterior: a hidden message sits under the surface one. "The report is due today, isn't it?" said in a tone that means "you are late again". The real message is the hidden one.

Life positions

People also hold a basic position about themselves and others. Taking "OK" and "not OK" for self and others gives four positions: I'm OK, You're OK · I'm OK, You're not OK · I'm not OK, You're OK · I'm not OK, You're not OK. The healthiest is "I'm OK, You're OK", because the person respects both themselves and others and can work through problems with them.

Key takeaway

For a manager, TA means speaking from the Adult state, aiming for complementary transactions, spotting crossed ones before they turn into conflict, and treating people from the "I'm OK, You're OK" position.

4.8The Johari Window

The Johari Window was developed by Joseph Luft and Harrington Ingham. It is a self-awareness tool. It asks two questions: what do I know about myself, and what do others know about me? Crossing the two answers gives four panes.

  • Open: known to you and to others, such as your skills and the views you share openly. Communication is easiest here.
  • Blind: others see it, you do not, such as a habit of interrupting or sounding curt in emails. You can only learn about it from other people.
  • Hidden: you know it, others do not, such as a worry, a past mistake or a private opinion you keep to yourself.
  • Unknown: neither you nor others see it yet, such as an ability that has never been tested. It may come to light in a new situation.

How the panes change

The panes are not fixed. Feedback from others tells you what they see, so the blind pane shrinks. Self-disclosure means sharing what you know about yourself, so the hidden pane shrinks. Both grow the open pane, and the open pane is where trust lives. A team where members give honest feedback and feel safe sharing works with fewer misunderstandings.

Example

A new team lead does not realise she cuts people off in meetings (blind). After a colleague mentions it, she changes. She also tells the team she is still learning the client's systems (hidden). Both moves enlarge her open pane, and the team trusts her more.

4.9Types of Teams

Everything so far has been about groups. A team is a group with positive synergy and shared accountability. Synergy means the combined result is different from what the members would produce separately. In a team it is positive, so the whole is more than the sum of its parts. Every team is a group, but not every group is a team.

Group or team?

BasisWork groupWork team
Main activityShares information so each member can do their own jobWorks jointly on a collective output
SynergyNeutral or negativePositive
AccountabilityIndividualIndividual and mutual
SkillsRandomComplementary
OutputThe sum of individual contributionsGreater than the sum of the parts

In a work group, members meet to share information, but each is judged only on their own work. They have random skills, whatever each person happens to bring, so the output is just the sum of what each person does. Sometimes it is less, because of the coordination cost (neutral or negative synergy). In a work team, each member is answerable for their own part and also for the team's result. The team has complementary skills, chosen to fit together, so it produces something no member could alone.

The newer kinds of teams

  • Green teams: cross-functional groups that drive the sustainability agenda day to day. Organisations need them for five reasons:
    • They cut waste and energy cost, which saves money.
    • They help meet tightening ESG and regulatory demands, as investors and regulators ask for proof of action.
    • They protect brand reputation, because customers notice whether a firm acts on its green claims.
    • They engage employees around purpose, giving people a cause beyond their daily tasks.
    • They turn a vague green vision into concrete shop-floor action, because the people who do the work decide the changes.
  • Virtual teams: members work across locations and time zones, held together by technology rather than a shared room. Without daily face-to-face contact, trust and clear goals matter even more. Members cannot see each other working, so they have to trust that the work is happening. Goals must be clear, because a quick chat to fix confusion is not always possible.
  • Cross-functional sustainability teams: people from different departments who own an ESG goal together. They blend finance, operations and marketing to make sustainability real. Finance builds the cost case, operations changes the process, and marketing tells customers about it. A sustainability goal usually fails when one department owns it alone, because no single department controls all three.
Example

A textile mill sets up a green team of six workers and supervisors from dyeing, maintenance and stores. Within three months they fix steam leaks and reuse rinse water, cutting energy and water bills. The plant head's vague target of "going green" has become a list of concrete shop-floor actions.

Exam tip

If a question asks whether a set of people is a group or a team, test the case against the five bases in the table. Synergy and accountability are the deciding points.

4.10Creating Effective Teams

Calling people a team does not make them one. Effectiveness depends on three sets of factors: the context the team works in, its composition, and its process.

Context: the support around the team

  • Adequate resources: time, money, tools and information. A team without resources cannot deliver however talented it is.
  • Trusted leadership: a leader the team trusts to set direction, settle disputes and represent it fairly.
  • A climate of trust: members trust each other enough to share ideas, admit mistakes and depend on one another.
  • Rewards that recognise the team: if only individual effort is rewarded, members will compete rather than cooperate. Part of the reward must depend on the team's result.

Composition: who is in the team

  • Abilities: the knowledge and skills the task actually needs, so the team is not missing a key capability.
  • Personalities: traits that suit working with others, such as being dependable and cooperative.
  • Roles: someone to drive the task and someone to keep relationships working, so all the roles the team needs are covered.
  • Diversity: different backgrounds bring different views and better ideas, as long as members can work through their differences.
  • A workable size: small enough for everyone to take part and stay visible, large enough to have the skills needed.

Process: how the team works

  • A common purpose: a shared sense of why the team exists, which gives direction.
  • Specific goals: clear, measurable targets that turn the purpose into action.
  • Team confidence: the team's belief that it can succeed. Early wins build it.
  • Managed conflict: disagreement about the task improves ideas, but personal clashes must be handled before they damage trust.
  • No free riders: each member's contribution stays visible, so nobody coasts on others' work. This is the social loafing problem from 4.4.
Exam tip

Organise any "build an effective team" answer under the three headings context, composition and process. Then apply two or three points from each to the case.

4.11Teams and Collaborations

Modern work relies on collaboration: people across teams and departments working together. The key tension here is collaboration versus accountability.

  • The benefit of broad collaboration: when many people contribute, ideas spread and more people feel ownership of the work.
  • The risk: when everyone owns everything, it becomes unclear who is actually responsible. Each person assumes someone else will handle a task, so it slips.
  • The answer: effective teams hold both. They collaborate widely, but on top of clear individual and mutual accountability. Each task has a named owner, and the team is still answerable for the joint result.
Example

An Indian company keeps small teams with named role owners inside its larger collaborative network. People share ideas freely across the network, but every task belongs to a named person in a small team. So no task falls into the gap between many willing hands.

This links back to 4.9. Mutual accountability is one of the features that turns a group into a team. Collaboration without it slides back towards a group where nobody owns the outcome.

4.12Technology-Enabled Collaboration

Shared documents, boards, video and chat let a team work as one across distance. This is what makes virtual teams possible. But the tool must fit the task. This is called task-technology fit: the right tool depends on what kind of work is being done.

BasisSynchronous toolsAsynchronous tools
ExamplesVideo, callsDocs, email, boards
NatureRich and real-timeLean and time-shifted
Best forComplex, creative or emotional work: brainstorming, conflict, sensitive feedbackRoutine updates, documentation, teams split across time zones
  • Synchronous tools are rich because they carry tone, facial expression and instant back-and-forth. That makes them right for work where people need to react to each other, such as brainstorming, settling a conflict or giving difficult feedback. Sending sensitive feedback by email can easily be misread.
  • Asynchronous tools are lean because they carry mainly words, and time-shifted because people respond when they are free. That suits routine updates and documentation, and teams across time zones, where a live meeting would mean someone working at midnight.

Tools remove friction, but they do not replace the human basics: clear roles, real trust and a shared goal. Technology carries the team, it does not build it. A team without trust or clear roles will struggle on the best software.

Exam tip

In a virtual-team case, match each activity to synchronous or asynchronous tools and say why. Then add that the tools only work if trust, clear goals and clear roles are already in place.

Revision and case practice

Five-mark questions

  1. Define a group. Explain the four criteria that make a collection of people a group.
  2. Distinguish between formal and informal groups with examples of each type.
  3. Why do people join groups?
  4. Explain role identity, role perception, role expectations, the psychological contract and role conflict.
  5. How does group size affect performance? Explain social loafing.
  6. List the symptoms of groupthink.
  7. Explain the four panes of the Johari Window.
  8. Distinguish between a work group and a work team.
  9. Why do organisations need green teams?

Ten-mark questions

  1. Explain Tuckman's five stages of group development. How should a manager handle adjourning in a task force compared with a permanent department?
  2. Discuss the five group properties. Using the cohesiveness and performance norms matrix, explain why a cohesive group is not always a productive one.
  3. Compare group and individual decision-making. Explain groupthink and group shift, and suggest how to prevent groupthink.
  4. Explain the four techniques of group decision-making and the situation each one suits.
  5. Explain Transactional Analysis: ego states, types of transactions and life positions.
  6. Explain how context, composition and process make a team effective. How should a virtual team choose between synchronous and asynchronous tools?
Case · The launch that nobody questioned

A six-member product team at a consumer goods firm has worked together for three years and is very close. The leader is popular and wants to launch a new snack before Diwali. A market survey warns of weak demand. One member says the numbers are probably wrong, and the group agrees. Priya raises the issue twice and is told she is "being negative". Another member had doubts but stayed quiet because no one else spoke. The team approves a launch bigger than any of them would have backed alone.

Question: Diagnose what went wrong and recommend changes.

Answer outline
  • Name it as groupthink and match the symptoms: rationalising warnings, pressure on dissenters, self-censorship, illusion of unanimity.
  • The bigger-than-individual launch is a risky shift, because responsibility felt shared.
  • High cohesiveness made conformity stronger.
  • Remedies: a devil's advocate, an outside expert review, and NGT so every member writes a view before discussion.
  • Conclude: the team's closeness is an asset only if its norms reward honest evaluation.
