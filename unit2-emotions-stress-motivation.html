2.19Emotions and Moods

Start with the umbrella term. Affect is a broad range of feelings that people experience, and it comes in two forms.

BasisEmotionsMoods
IntensityIntenseLess intense
CauseCaused by a specific eventCause is often general and unclear
DurationVery brief: seconds or minutesLonger lasting: hours or days
SpecificitySpecific and numerous, many discrete emotionsGeneral: two main dimensions, positive affect and negative affect
Facial expressionUsually accompanied by distinct facial expressionsNot usually indicated by a distinct expression
NatureAction oriented, prompts a responseCognitive in nature, colours thinking
ExampleAnger at a colleague who took credit for your work in a meetingFeeling generally low all week without being able to say exactly why
Positivity offset

At zero input, when nothing in particular is happening, most people experience a mildly positive mood. This is called the positivity offset, and it is why a normal day feels slightly good rather than neutral. It matters practically, because it means a workplace where the default mood is flat or negative is not normal, it is a signal.

Sources of emotions and moods

SourceWhat the research shows
PersonalityPeople have a trait-level tendency, called affect intensity, to experience emotions strongly or weakly. Some people are dispositionally cheerful
Time of dayPositive affect typically rises through the morning, peaks around midday, and declines through the afternoon and evening. Negative affect is relatively flat
Day of the weekPositive mood is highest at the weekend and lowest early in the week. The Monday effect is real
WeatherNo real effect. People believe weather affects mood, which is an illusory correlation, an association we perceive where none exists
StressEven low levels of constant stress worsen mood, and the effect accumulates
SleepPoor sleep impairs mood, decision-making and emotional regulation, and increases negative affect the next day
ExerciseImproves mood, with the strongest effect for those who are already depressed
Social activitiesPhysical, informal and social activities raise positive mood more than formal or sedentary ones
AgeNegative emotion appears to decline with age; older people experience fewer negative emotions and they last less long
SexWomen report more intense emotions and more emotional expression, which is attributed largely to socialisation rather than biology
Exam tip · the weather point

The weather finding is a favourite, because it contradicts everyday belief and lets you name illusory correlation, which reappears in Unit 3 as a perceptual error. Two concepts for the price of one sentence.

2.20Emotional Labour

Definition

Emotional labour is an employee's expression of organisationally desired emotions during interpersonal transactions at work.

The concept originates with Arlie Hochschild, The Managed Heart

Every job with a customer, a patient, a student or a colleague requires it. The organisation has display rules, which are the norms about which emotions should be shown, and they are often unwritten.

Felt versus displayed emotions

Inside
Felt emotions
  • An individual's actual emotions.
  • What you genuinely feel.
Outside
Displayed emotions
  • The emotions that are organisationally required and considered appropriate for the job.
  • They are learned, not innate.
The key term

Emotional dissonance is the inconsistency between the emotions an employee feels and the emotions they project.

Surface acting and deep acting

Example

A call centre agent takes a fifth abusive call before lunch. The display rule is a warm, patient voice throughout.

Surface acting: she keeps her voice pleasant while her jaw is clenched and she is typing an angry message to a colleague. She gets through the call, and pays for it by mid-afternoon.

Deep acting: she reframes the caller as someone whose order genuinely did go missing and who has been on hold for twenty minutes, which actually is true, and generates some real patience.

Both produce a polite call. Only one of them leaves her able to do six more.

Why this matters for management

Emotional labour is real labour, and it is invisible on every performance metric. A service organisation that increases call volume without changing anything else has increased its employees' workload twice over, once in tasks and once in emotional labour, and will only see the second increase in its attrition figures six months later.

The practical responses are: allow recovery time between emotionally demanding interactions, train for deep acting rather than just instructing employees to smile, give employees some control over difficult interactions including the right to escalate or end abusive calls, and avoid display rules that are impossible to meet honestly.

2.21Emotional Intelligence

Definition

Emotional intelligence is a person's ability to perceive emotions in the self and others, understand the meaning of these emotions, and regulate one's own emotions accordingly.

The term was developed by Peter Salovey and John Mayer and popularised by Daniel Goleman, whose book is on your prescribed list. Goleman's five-component model is the one to reproduce.

The parallel Mayer and Salovey four-branch model is worth naming as the more academic version: perceiving emotions, using emotions to facilitate thought, understanding emotions, and managing emotions.

Example · the five components in one meeting

A project manager is told in a review that her timeline is unrealistic, in front of the client.

  • Self-awareness: she notices she is angry and that it is partly embarrassment.
  • Self-management: she does not defend herself immediately, and asks for the specific concern instead.
  • Motivation: she wants the project to actually work, which outranks wanting to be right in that room.
  • Empathy: she registers that her colleague raised it publicly because he is under pressure from the client, not to undermine her.
  • Social skills: she proposes a joint replanning session for the next morning, which ends the public argument and gives everyone a way out.

A manager with high IQ and low EI wins the argument in that room and loses the project.

Evaluating EI, which an L5 question requires

The case for
Arguments in favour
  • Intuitive appeal. Nearly everyone accepts that reading people is a distinct and valuable skill.
  • It predicts outcomes that matter, including job performance in emotionally demanding roles, leadership emergence and team effectiveness.
  • There is a biological basis. People with damage to the brain region governing emotional processing score normally on IQ tests yet make disastrous life and work decisions.
  • It can be developed, unlike IQ, which makes it useful for training.
The case against
Criticisms
  • It is too vaguely defined. Is it a skill, a trait, or a set of competencies? Different researchers answer differently, so studies are not comparable.
  • Measurement is weak. Most measures are self-report, and asking someone to rate their own empathy is obviously unreliable.
  • It may not be distinct from existing constructs. Critics argue EI largely overlaps with the Big Five, particularly emotional stability and agreeableness, plus general intelligence.
  • It can be used manipulatively. Reading emotions accurately is exactly the skill a high Mach needs, so EI is morally neutral rather than a virtue.
  • Commercial overclaiming. Popular books assert that EI matters more than IQ by wide margins, which the evidence does not support at that strength.
The verdict to write

EI is a genuinely useful idea that has been oversold. It adds real predictive value for jobs with heavy emotional demands, including sales, service, healthcare, teaching and leadership, and it is trainable. It adds little for jobs with low interpersonal content, its measurement remains the weakest link, and it is a capability rather than a virtue, since the same skill serves a good manager and a manipulator equally well.

2.22OB Applications of Emotions and Moods

This heading is asking: so what? Where does any of this change a management decision? Ten applications, and this is a ready-made ten-mark answer.

ApplicationWhat emotions research tells the manager
1. SelectionEI should be a hiring criterion for emotionally demanding jobs, especially high social interaction roles. Employers increasingly assess it
2. Decision-makingPositive moods and emotions help people make better decisions, using broader heuristics and more creative options. Emotions are not the opposite of rationality: people with damaged emotional processing decide worse, not better. However, intense negative emotion narrows attention and produces poor judgement
3. CreativityPeople in good moods are more creative, produce more ideas and see more options. Activated positive mood, being excited rather than merely calm, matters most
4. MotivationSuccess on a task raises mood, which raises confidence and effort, which raises success. A self-reinforcing loop, which also runs in reverse after failure
5. LeadershipEffective leaders rely on emotional appeals to convey a vision and build enthusiasm. Emotion is how a message becomes acceptable rather than merely understood
6. NegotiationDisplayed anger can extract concessions from an opponent, but it damages the relationship and the opponent's future willingness to deal. Negotiators who show disappointment tend to do better than those who show anger
7. Customer serviceAn employee's mood transfers to the customer through emotional contagion, and returns as customer behaviour, which then affects the employee. A loop that runs both ways
8. Job attitudesMoods at work do spill over into home life, and home moods spill into work. The effect dissipates overnight, so a bad day does not usually last, but a bad month does
9. Deviant workplace behaviourNegative emotions, especially anger and envy, are the most consistent precursors of theft, gossip, sabotage and aggression at work
10. Safety and injuryNegative mood and fatigue increase workplace injury. Employees in bad moods are more easily distracted and take more risks. So mood management is a safety control, not only a welfare issue
Emotional contagion

Emotional contagion is the process by which people catch emotions from others.

Example

A branch manager arrives visibly irritated on Monday morning and speaks curtly to the first two people he meets. By 11 am the tone at the counters has hardened, a customer is snapped at, the complaint reaches him in the afternoon, and he concludes his staff have an attitude problem.

He caused the thing he is now diagnosing. This is the single most practically useful idea in the topic: a manager's mood is not a private matter, because it is an input to everyone else's behaviour.

How managers can influence moods

  • Their own visible mood, since contagion runs downward far more strongly than upward.
  • Humour used well, which reliably lifts group mood.
  • Small gestures of recognition, which produce activated positive mood more cheaply than any incentive.
  • Selecting for positive affect in team-facing roles.
  • Managing the physical environment, which links back to environmental psychology in Unit 1.

2.23Work-Related Stress

Definition

Stress is an unpleasant psychological process that occurs in response to environmental pressures. It is an adaptive response, moderated by individual differences, that results from any action, situation or event which places special demands on a person.

Two distinctions to establish first

Not all stress is bad
Eustress vs distress
  • Eustress is positive, constructive stress. The alertness before a presentation you are prepared for.
  • Distress is negative, destructive stress. The dread before one you are not.
Two kinds of stressor
Challenge vs hindrance
  • Challenge stressors relate to workload, pressure to complete tasks, and time urgency. They are associated with higher performance and satisfaction.
  • Hindrance stressors keep you from reaching your goals: red tape, office politics, confusion over responsibilities. They produce strain, lower performance and turnover.
Exam tip

The challenge versus hindrance distinction is the most useful single idea in this topic, because it explains why "reduce stress" is bad advice. A demanding project and a broken approval process both register as stress, but only one of them should be removed. An answer that makes this distinction reads as understanding rather than recall.

The demands and resources model

Demands are the responsibilities, pressures, obligations and uncertainties faced at work. Resources are the things within a person's control that can be used to meet those demands. Strain occurs when demands exceed resources, which is why two employees under identical workload experience different stress.

The three role stressors, defined

StressorMeaningExample
Role conflictExpectations are hard to reconcile or satisfy togetherA supervisor told to cut overtime costs and to clear the month-end backlog
Role ambiguityRole expectations are not clearly understood, so the person does not know what to doA new joinee three weeks in with no job description and two people giving instructions
Role overloadMore is expected than can be done in the time availableAn article assistant on four audits in the same fortnight

Selye's General Adaptation Syndrome

Hans Selye described the body's response to a sustained stressor in three stages, and it is the standard physiological answer:

Consequences of stress

Physiological
The body

Headaches, high blood pressure, heart disease, disturbed sleep, digestive problems, lowered immunity, backache.

Psychological
The mind

Tension, anxiety, irritability, boredom, procrastination, job dissatisfaction, depression. Job dissatisfaction is the simplest and most obvious psychological effect.

Behavioural
The conduct

Reduced productivity, absenteeism, turnover, changes in eating habits, increased smoking or alcohol use, rapid speech, accidents, workplace aggression.

2.24Stress and Burnout

Definition

Burnout is a syndrome of emotional exhaustion, depersonalisation and reduced personal accomplishment, resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed.

Christina Maslach. The WHO classifies burnout in ICD-11 as an occupational phenomenon, not a medical condition
The most common mistake in this topic

Burnout is not "a lot of stress" and it is not tiredness. Tiredness is fixed by a holiday. Burnout is not, which is the diagnostic test. Note also the WHO wording: it is an occupational phenomenon, so it is caused by the job and not by a weakness in the person. That framing matters, because organisations routinely treat burnout as an individual resilience problem and then wonder why yoga sessions do not fix it.

The three dimensions

BasisStressBurnout
NatureOver-engagement. Too much pressureDis-engagement. Emptiness
EmotionsEmotions are over-reactive: anxiety, urgencyEmotions are blunted: numbness, detachment
EnergyProduces urgency and hyperactivityProduces helplessness and hopelessness
Primary damagePhysicalEmotional and motivational
DurationCan be short termChronic, develops over months or years
RecoveryRest and removing the stressor usually worksRest alone does not work. Requires the job conditions to change
Attitude to workStill cares, perhaps too muchHas stopped caring, which is the defining shift

The six areas of mismatch

Maslach and Leiter identified six areas where a gap between the person and the job produces burnout. This list is the practical diagnostic, and it is better than a symptom list in an answer.

AreaThe mismatch
WorkloadToo much work, too little time or resource, sustained without recovery
ControlLittle autonomy or say over how the work is done
RewardInsufficient recognition, financial or social, for the effort given
CommunityPoor relationships, isolation, unresolved conflict, no support
FairnessPerceived inequity in workload, pay, promotion or how disputes are handled
ValuesA conflict between what the person believes and what the job requires them to do
Example

A hospital nurse in her fourth year of double shifts. Year one, she remembered patients' names. Now she refers to "the bed seven case" and finds she is irritated by families asking questions. She is not a worse person, and a two-week holiday makes no difference beyond the first three days back.

Run the six areas: workload is excessive, control over the roster is nil, reward is a wage that has not moved, community has thinned as colleagues left, fairness is broken because the roster favours seniority, and values are in conflict because she entered nursing to care for people and the workload now makes that impossible. Five of those six are organisational decisions. A resilience workshop addresses none of them.

Presenteeism · the related term

Presenteeism is being physically at work while too unwell, exhausted or disengaged to be productive. It is more expensive than absenteeism in most studies, and completely invisible in attendance data. A workplace that punishes absence and rewards visible presence will reliably produce it.

2.25Management of Stress

The standard structure for this answer is individual approaches and organisational approaches. Give both, and then add the point at the end about which one actually matters.

The point that separates a good answer

Individual techniques treat the symptom. Organisational approaches treat the cause. A firm that offers meditation sessions while leaving role overload, unfair rosters and abusive supervision untouched has shifted responsibility for an organisational problem onto the individual, and employees read that correctly as a failure to act.

A useful framing borrowed from occupational health is three levels of prevention: primary prevention removes the stressor by redesigning the job, secondary prevention builds coping capacity through training and resilience work, and tertiary prevention treats the damage through counselling and EAPs. Most organisations invest almost entirely in the third, which is the least effective and the most expensive.

2.26Technostress and AI-Driven Work Stress

Definition

Technostress is the stress experienced by individuals as a result of their use of information and communication technologies, arising when a person cannot cope with the demands that technology places on them.

Term coined by Craig Brod; the five-stressor framework is from Monideepa Tarafdar and colleagues
Why this heading is worth extra attention

This is one of the newest headings in the syllabus and it is not covered well in any of the eight prescribed books, most of which predate it. That makes it a likely source of a fresh question, and it is one where a well-prepared answer stands out immediately.

The five techno-stressors

Techno-stressorMeaningExample
Techno-overloadTechnology forces you to work faster and longer, and to process more information than you can absorbFour hundred emails, plus Teams, plus WhatsApp groups, plus a ticketing system, all expecting a same-day response
Techno-invasionTechnology invades personal life and erodes the boundary between work and home. The "always available" expectationA manager's message at 10:40 pm with no stated deadline, which the employee now has to interpret
Techno-complexityThe technology is complex enough to make the employee feel inadequate, and forces continuous learning of new toolsThe third new reporting dashboard in eighteen months, each with its own logic
Techno-insecurityFear of losing one's job, either to automation or to colleagues with better technical skillsAn analyst watching an AI tool produce in seconds the summary that used to be half her job
Techno-uncertaintyConstant change and upgrades mean knowledge never settles, so expertise feels permanently provisionalBeing an expert in a system that is retired the following year

What AI specifically adds

AI does not just intensify the five stressors above. It introduces stressors of a different kind, and this is the part worth writing at length.

01
Algorithmic management

Work is allocated, timed and rated by a system rather than a person. There is nobody to explain a decision to, and no discretion to appeal to. Studied most in platform work.

02
Constant measurement

Keystroke logging, screen capture, call scoring and productivity dashboards mean surveillance is continuous rather than periodic, which is a known and potent stressor.

03
AI anxiety and job insecurity

Not fear of the tool, but uncertainty about whether the role survives. Uncertainty is more stressful than a known bad outcome, because it cannot be planned around.

04
Deskilling and loss of meaning

When AI takes the judgement part of the job and leaves the checking, the work loses the elements that made it satisfying. This is a direct hit on the "work itself" driver of satisfaction.

05
Opacity and loss of control

Employees are held accountable for outputs produced by a system whose reasoning they cannot inspect or explain to a customer.

06
Verification burden

A new and under-recognised load: checking AI output is cognitively harder than producing it, because errors are fluent and plausible rather than obviously wrong.

Example

A delivery rider's app assigns routes, times each drop, and lowers his rating if he is slow. He does not know how the rating is calculated, cannot see why a bad week happened, and has nobody to ask. His earnings depend on a number produced by a system he cannot interrogate.

Map it onto the stress model from 2.23. The demands are high, the resources are nil, and the two classic moderators, perceived control and social support, have both been designed out. This is not a technology problem. It is a job design problem expressed through technology.

Managing technostress

ApproachWhat it looks like
Boundary and availability normsExplicit rules on after-hours contact, the right to disconnect, scheduled-send by default, and no expectation of a reply outside hours. The norm must be modelled by seniors or it is ignored
Reduce channel proliferationDecide which channel is for what. Most techno-overload is an internal coordination failure, not a technology problem
Meaningful training and literacyRaises self-efficacy, which directly reduces techno-complexity and techno-insecurity. Training people before rollout rather than after
Involve employees in adoptionParticipation restores control, the strongest single moderator of stress. Employees who help choose and configure a tool experience it very differently
Algorithmic transparencyExplain how allocation and rating work, and provide a route to a human for review. Opaque systems generate stress out of proportion to their actual harshness
Human in the loop, with authorityKeep a person accountable for consequential decisions, and give them real power to override. A human who cannot override is not a safeguard, and everyone knows it
Honest communication about job impactVague reassurance increases anxiety, because employees assume the worst is being concealed. Specific statements about what changes and what does not reduce it
Protect the judgement content of jobsWhen automating, deliberately redesign the remaining role so it retains variety and discretion, rather than leaving only the residue

2.27Motivation: Meaning

Definition

Motivation is the process that accounts for an individual's intensity, direction and persistence of effort toward attaining a goal.

Robbins and Judge

Three elements, and every one of them carries a mark:

01
Intensity

How hard a person tries. This is what most people mean by motivation, and on its own it is not enough.

02
Direction

Whether that effort is channelled towards the organisation's goals. High intensity in the wrong direction produces nothing useful.

03
Persistence

How long the effort is maintained. Motivated people stay with a task long enough to finish it.

Example

An employee spends fourteen hours perfecting the formatting of a report nobody asked for, while the analysis remains undone. Intensity is very high, persistence is high, and direction is wrong. Calling him unmotivated would be inaccurate and would lead to the wrong intervention. He needs clarity about priorities, not encouragement.

From outside
Extrinsic motivation
  • Driven by external rewards: pay, bonus, promotion, recognition, or avoiding punishment.
  • Works fast, and works well for routine or unpleasant tasks.
  • Stops when the reward stops.
From inside
Intrinsic motivation
  • Driven by the work itself: interest, mastery, meaning, autonomy.
  • More durable, and better for complex and creative work.
  • Can be damaged by heavy extrinsic reward, an effect called the crowding-out or over-justification effect.
The crowding-out effect · worth naming

Paying someone for something they already enjoyed doing can reduce their motivation to do it once the payment stops, because the reason for doing it has been reattributed from interest to money. This is a real and counter-intuitive finding, and it is a good closing observation for any motivation answer.

The syllabus names four theories, all of them from the early or classical group. They are widely known, intuitively appealing, and only partly supported by evidence. Handle each the same way: explain it, apply it, then criticise it.

2.28Maslow's Need Hierarchy Theory

Abraham Maslow proposed that within every human being there is a hierarchy of five needs, and that as each is substantially satisfied the next becomes dominant.

Two principles govern the movement:

  • Deficit principle: a satisfied need no longer motivates. This is the single most useful managerial idea in the theory.
  • Progression principle: a need at one level does not become active until the level below is substantially satisfied.
Example · the deficit principle in action

A firm gives a well-paid senior analyst another 12 per cent raise to stop her leaving. She leaves anyway, for a smaller company at a lower salary, because she will lead a team there.

Her pay was already satisfying her lower-order needs, so more of it added almost nothing. She was operating on esteem and self-actualisation, and the firm kept offering a solution to a need she did not have. Managers reach for money because it is the easiest lever to pull, not because it is the right one.

Criticisms · required for an evaluate question
  1. Little empirical support. Maslow provided no empirical validation, and later research has not confirmed that the five levels exist as a hierarchy.
  2. The rigid sequence does not hold. People pursue higher needs while lower ones are unmet. Artists, activists and researchers routinely accept insecurity for meaning.
  3. Needs are not universal in this order. In collectivist cultures, belonging may outrank individual esteem, and self-actualisation as an individual project is a distinctly Western framing. This is a strong point to make given the Hofstede material in Part B.
  4. Self-actualisation cannot be measured or clearly defined, so the top of the hierarchy is untestable.
  5. One need at a time is unrealistic. People pursue several simultaneously.

Why it survives anyway: it is intuitive, easy to teach, and it made the genuinely important point that money is not the only motivator. Clayton Alderfer's ERG theory was a direct response, compressing the five into Existence, Relatedness and Growth, allowing more than one need to be active at once and adding a frustration-regression element: blocked at a higher need, people intensify their demand for a lower one. That last idea explains why employees denied development opportunities start bargaining harder over allowances.

2.29McGregor's Theory X and Theory Y

Douglas McGregor argued that managers hold one of two sets of assumptions about human nature, and that they manage in line with those assumptions whether or not the assumptions are true.

BasisTheory X assumptionsTheory Y assumptions
Attitude to workEmployees inherently dislike work and will avoid it if possibleEmployees can view work as being as natural as rest or play
ResponsibilityThey avoid responsibility and prefer to be directedThey will accept and even seek responsibility
AmbitionLittle ambition; security is the priorityCapable of self-direction and creativity
Control neededMust be coerced, controlled or threatened with punishmentWill exercise self-direction and self-control if committed to the objectives
Capacity for decisionsLimited; decisions should be made by managersThe average person can learn to make good decisions
Management style producedAutocratic, close supervision, detailed rules, extrinsic rewards and penaltiesParticipative, delegation, autonomy, involvement in goal setting, intrinsic rewards
Link to MaslowAssumes lower-order needs dominateAssumes higher-order needs dominate
Example

Two branch managers in the same bank. One requires written approval for any customer concession above ₹500 and checks the register twice daily. Staff there refer every judgement upward, including obvious ones, and the manager is permanently overloaded. The other sets a limit of ₹5,000 with a monthly review of exceptions. Her staff settle small complaints on the spot, and two of them have suggested process changes that were adopted.

Same bank, same recruitment pool, same pay scale. The difference in employee behaviour is a difference in managerial assumption.

Criticisms
  • No empirical evidence that either set of assumptions is valid, or that acting on Theory Y necessarily raises motivation.
  • The dichotomy is too simple. Most managers hold a mixture, and most employees do not fit either caricature.
  • Theory Y is not universally superior. For routine, low-discretion, high-risk or safety-critical work, or with genuinely inexperienced staff, direction and close supervision are appropriate. Blanket delegation to someone who is not ready is not empowerment but abandonment. This is the contingency point from Unit 1, and it is the mature conclusion to write.
  • It describes managers, not employees, which is arguably its real contribution, and it maps closely onto Keith Davis's autocratic and supportive models.

2.30Herzberg's Two-Factor Theory

Also called motivation-hygiene theory. Frederick Herzberg asked people to describe situations in which they felt exceptionally good and exceptionally bad about their jobs, and found something unexpected: the replies were not mirror images. The factors that made people satisfied were entirely different from those that made them dissatisfied.

Intrinsic · content of the job
Motivators (satisfiers)
  • Achievement
  • Recognition
  • The work itself
  • Responsibility
  • Advancement
  • Growth and development
Extrinsic · context of the job
Hygiene factors (dissatisfiers)
  • Company policy and administration
  • Supervision
  • Relationship with supervisor and peers
  • Working conditions
  • Salary
  • Job security
  • Status
Example

A firm loses people from its back office. It responds with a 15 per cent raise, a renovated floor and a better canteen. Attrition falls for two quarters, then returns to where it was, and the exit interviews say the same thing: "the work is the same thing every day and there is nowhere to go from here."

The firm fixed hygiene and never touched motivators. In Herzberg's terms it removed reasons to be unhappy without adding any reason to be engaged. Note the honest complication too: had the canteen and salary been genuinely poor, ignoring them would have guaranteed failure. Hygiene is necessary and insufficient, which is exactly the point.

The practical output: job enrichment

Herzberg's prescription is vertical job loading, meaning adding depth and responsibility to a job, which is job enrichment. Distinguish it from two weaker alternatives, because this comparison is often asked:

  • Job rotation: moving the employee between different jobs at the same level. Reduces boredom, does not add responsibility.
  • Job enlargement: horizontal loading, adding more tasks at the same level. In Herzberg's view this is simply "adding zero to zero".
  • Job enrichment: vertical loading, adding planning, control, discretion and accountability. The only one that touches the motivators.
Criticisms
  1. Method bias. Herzberg used a critical incident technique that invites self-serving attribution: people credit good outcomes to themselves, which shows up as motivators, and blame bad outcomes on the environment, which shows up as hygiene factors. The two-factor split may therefore be an artefact of how the question was asked.
  2. Limited and unrepresentative sample. Originally around two hundred engineers and accountants, whose work has unusually high intrinsic content. The theory travels poorly to routine manual work.
  3. Reliability of the raters. Interpretation of the responses required judgement, and different raters could classify the same incident differently.
  4. No overall measure of satisfaction. A person might dislike part of a job and still find the whole acceptable, which the method could not capture.
  5. Pay is ambiguous. Herzberg classified salary as hygiene, but it clearly functions as recognition and achievement for many people, especially in sales. Most later researchers treat it as both.
  6. It assumes a satisfaction-productivity link that it never tested.

Why it endures: it shifted management attention from the context of work to the content of work, and job enrichment and modern job design both descend from it.

2.31McClelland's Theory of Needs

David McClelland argued that three acquired needs, learned rather than innate, drive behaviour at work, and that people differ in which one dominates. Measured using the Thematic Apperception Test, a projective method in which the person writes a story about an ambiguous picture.

The high achiever profile

McClelland's findings on high nAch individuals are the most examinable part, and they are counter-intuitive on two points.

  • They prefer tasks of moderate difficulty, roughly a 50 per cent chance of success. Not easy tasks, because there is no satisfaction in a certainty, and not very hard tasks either, because the outcome would depend on luck rather than on their own skill. Most students get this second half wrong.
  • They seek personal responsibility for outcomes, so they can attribute success to their own effort.
  • They want rapid and unambiguous feedback on performance, in order to know whether they are improving.
  • They are not primarily motivated by money, though they use it as a measure of progress. Remove the scorekeeping function and the money matters much less.
  • They do well in entrepreneurship, sales and roles with clear individual accountability.
The finding students always get backwards

A high need for achievement does not make someone a good manager. High achievers are interested in doing things well themselves, which makes them reluctant to delegate and impatient with slower colleagues. The best managers in McClelland's research were high in need for power and low in need for affiliation, because management requires influencing people and being willing to make decisions that will be unpopular. A high nAff manager finds it very hard to say no, to discipline, or to make a necessary but resented decision.

The important qualification on power

McClelland distinguished two faces of the power need. Personalised power is the desire to dominate others for one's own benefit, which is destructive and shades into the Dark Triad from Part A. Socialised power is the desire to influence others for the benefit of the group or organisation, which is what effective leadership actually requires. Making this distinction is what turns a recalled fact into an evaluated one. It is also the bridge to the power and leadership material in Unit 5.

Example · three people on the same team

A three-person team is given a new client mandate.

  • High nAch (Divya) asks what the target is and how success will be measured, then wants her own piece of it so the result is clearly attributable to her.
  • High nPow (Rohit) asks who will lead the client relationship and who presents to the client board.
  • High nAff (Sneha) asks whether the three of them will still work together afterwards and whether anyone is being left out.

All three questions are reasonable. Assigning the same incentive to all three would motivate one of them properly and roughly half-motivate the others. That is the whole practical value of the theory.

Application

McClelland also showed that these needs can be trained, through achievement motivation training, which is his strongest claim to practical use. The other application is matching people to roles: high nAch to individually accountable, feedback-rich roles; high socialised nPow to leadership and negotiation roles; high nAff to coordination, HR and relationship-management roles.

Criticism: the TAT is a projective measure with well-known reliability problems and is slow and expensive to administer, so the theory has excellent research support but poor practical measurement.

The closing paragraph to write on any motivation question

The four theories in the syllabus are all content theories: each asks what people want. None of them explains the process by which a want becomes effort, which is why later work on goal setting, equity, organisational justice and expectancy was needed. Their shared weakness is that they treat needs as properties of individuals and largely ignore the situation, so they under-predict how much the same person's motivation changes when the job design, the fairness of the reward system or the manager changes. Their shared and lasting contribution is that they broke the assumption that pay is the motivator, and every modern job design practice follows from that.

Case study practice · Section C style

Arundhati runs a 140-seat customer support centre for a fintech. Last year the company deployed an AI assistant that drafts replies, scores every agent's tone and resolution time, and auto-assigns tickets. Average handling time has fallen 22 per cent and ticket volume per agent has risen 40 per cent. Agents are told to review each AI draft before sending, and are accountable for anything wrong in it.

In the last two quarters: attrition has risen from 18 to 46 per cent; the two most experienced agents have resigned, one saying "I am a proofreader now"; sick leave is up sharply and several agents work through obvious illness because leave is informally discouraged; customer satisfaction scores have fallen despite faster handling; and one agent told HR she cannot explain to customers why the system rejected their claim, so she now reads out the script and lets them get angry at her.

Arundhati's response so far has been a Friday yoga session, a "Star Agent" monthly award, and a 9 per cent across-the-board raise.

(a) Diagnose the situation using the concepts in this unit. (b) Evaluate her three interventions. (c) What should she do instead?

Answer skeleton for the case

(a) Diagnosis. Emotional labour has risen sharply: 40 per cent more interactions, tone now scored, and agents absorbing anger for decisions they cannot explain. The scripted reading with no ability to justify the outcome forces surface acting, the costlier form, and produces high emotional dissonance. Technostress on at least four of the five stressors: techno-overload (volume up 40 per cent), techno-insecurity ("I am a proofreader now"), techno-complexity, and the verification burden of checking fluent AI drafts while carrying the accountability. Algorithmic management has removed perceived control, the strongest moderator of stress, and the opacity means agents are accountable without authority. Burnout is visible on all three Maslach dimensions: exhaustion (sick leave), depersonalisation (reading the script and letting customers get angry), and inefficacy. Run the six mismatches and workload, control, reward, fairness and values are all breached. Presenteeism is present and is why sick leave understates the problem. Falling customer satisfaction despite faster handling is emotional contagion transmitting agent state to customers. On EVLN from Part B, the best people took exit and the rest have moved to neglect.

(b) Evaluating her interventions. All three are aimed at the wrong level. Yoga is tertiary prevention, addressing a symptom of an organisational cause, and its main effect is to signal that the problem is considered an individual failing. "Star Agent" is a single individual award in a workload-constrained team, so it cannot change the experience of the other 139 and may increase perceived unfairness. The 9 per cent raise is a hygiene intervention in Herzberg's terms, so at best it removes a reason for dissatisfaction, and since the complaint is about the content of the work it cannot motivate. Note also that experienced agents at reasonable salaries are on higher-order needs, so by Maslow's deficit principle more money buys little. This is the classic error of pulling the easiest lever.

(c) What to do instead. Primary prevention first: reset the volume target, because 40 per cent more interactions per agent is the root cause and no other intervention survives it. Build in recovery time after difficult contacts. Restore control and authority: give agents power to override the AI and to grant defined concessions without approval, which is Theory Y and the ₹5,000 limit example in practice. Insist on algorithmic transparency, so an agent can tell a customer why a claim was rejected, which removes both the dissonance and the falling satisfaction. Rebuild the job content through enrichment rather than enlargement: give agents ownership of complex cases, a role in improving the AI's prompts and templates, and a route into quality or training work, which addresses the "I am a proofreader" problem directly. Fix fairness by ending the informal discouragement of sick leave and making the metric set transparent. Train for deep acting rather than instructing agents to sound warm. Involve agents in configuring the tool, since participation is the most effective single stress moderator available. Keep the raise, because pay below fair level would sabotage everything else, but stop expecting it to do the work.
