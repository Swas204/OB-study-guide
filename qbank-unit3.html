Q1 · 5 marks

CO3 · L4 · Meaning of learning

Kaveri Bank made every branch employee complete an online module on the new KYC rules. All 400 staff passed the end-of-module quiz with over 80 percent. Three months later, an internal audit found that most branches were still opening accounts the old way. The training head insists, "Our people have learned the rules. The quiz proves it." Using the definition of learning, analyse whether learning has actually taken place, and suggest two changes to the programme.

Learning is any relatively permanent change in behaviour that occurs as a result of experience. Each key word can be tested against the bank's case.

  • Behaviour: learning must show up in what people do, not in what they know. Passing a quiz shows knowledge. Opening accounts the old way shows the behaviour has not changed, so the claim fails on this word.
  • Permanent: even if a few staff followed the new rules right after the module, the audit shows they slipped back. A change that does not last is not learning.
  • Experience: the change must come from practice, observation or consequences. A one-time online module gave none of these. Learning cannot be installed by a circular or a memo.
  • Change 1, practice on the live system: make staff open dummy accounts on the actual screen under the new rules, so the behaviour is practised, not just read about.
  • Change 2, consequences and checks: branch heads review new accounts every week, praise correct files and correct errors at once, so the right behaviour is reinforced.

So the training head is wrong. The quiz measured knowledge, and learning is judged by behaviour.

Marking scheme

Definition with the three key words applied to the case: 3 marks (1 each). Two practical changes linked to the definition: 2 marks (1 each).

Q2 · 5 marks

CO3 · L3 · Classical vs operant conditioning

Classify each of the following workplace moments as classical or operant conditioning, and justify each answer in one line.

  1. A designer's heart starts racing whenever the client's name flashes on her phone, after three tense calls with that client.
  2. A delivery executive at a quick-commerce firm starts wearing his helmet every day after riders who wear one get a weekly safety bonus.
  3. An intern feels relaxed the moment he hears the office coffee machine, because breaks with his friendly team always start there.
  4. A team stops sending late reports after the manager begins deducting points from the quarterly score for every delay.
  5. An accountant feels uneasy entering the conference room where layoffs were announced last year.

Classical conditioning links a neutral cue to an automatic, involuntary reaction. Operant conditioning shapes voluntary behaviour through the consequence that follows it.

  1. Classical. The name on the screen is a cue paired with stressful calls. The racing heart is involuntary.
  2. Operant. Wearing a helmet is a chosen behaviour, repeated because a reward (the bonus) follows it. This is positive reinforcement.
  3. Classical. The sound of the machine has been paired with pleasant breaks, so it now triggers relaxation on its own.
  4. Operant. Sending reports late is a voluntary act that falls because an unpleasant consequence (lost points) follows. This is punishment.
  5. Classical. The room is a cue paired with a painful event. The unease is automatic, not chosen.

The test in every case: if the reaction is automatic and set off by a cue that comes before it, it is classical. If the behaviour is chosen and driven by what follows, it is operant.

Marking scheme

1 mark per moment: 0.5 for the correct label and 0.5 for a justification that names the cue or the consequence.

Q3 · 5 marks

CO3 · L3 · The four consequence tools

Riya manages a customer-support team in Hyderabad. She wants more agents to update tickets before closing them, and fewer agents to take unplanned long breaks. Design one move for each of the four consequence tools of operant conditioning that Riya could use, and state the effect each will have on behaviour.

Operant conditioning gives a manager four tools. Positive means something is added and negative means something is removed. Two tools raise a behaviour and two reduce it.

  • Positive reinforcement (adds something pleasant, behaviour rises): name the agent with the most complete ticket updates in the daily huddle and add points towards a monthly reward.
  • Negative reinforcement (removes something unpleasant, behaviour rises): agents who keep 95 percent of tickets updated for a month are excused from the end-of-day manual ticket audit.
  • Punishment (adds something unpleasant, behaviour falls): an unplanned long break leads to a formal note in the attendance record.
  • Extinction (removes the reward that kept the behaviour going, behaviour fades): if agents stretch breaks because colleagues cover their queue, stop the informal covering so the long break no longer pays off.
  • Balance: Riya should lean on the two reinforcement tools for ticket updates and use punishment sparingly, because punishment shows what to stop, not what to do.
Marking scheme

1 mark for each tool correctly defined and applied to Riya's team (4 marks). 1 mark for stating the effect on behaviour or a sensible note on balance.

Q4 · 5 marks

CO3 · L4 · Accidental reinforcement

At a Chennai logistics office, the manager spends extra time with any employee who complains loudly in meetings, and often grants their requests to keep the peace. Quiet employees who raise issues by email get no reply for days. Over six months, loud complaining has doubled. Analyse which consequence tools the manager is using without realising it, and explain how he should correct the situation.

Under operant conditioning, behaviour followed by a pleasant consequence is repeated. Managers often reward the very behaviour they want to stop.

  • Accidental positive reinforcement: attention and granted requests follow loud complaining. A pleasant consequence is being added, so the behaviour rises. That is why it has doubled.
  • Accidental extinction of the right behaviour: calm emails get no response, so the reward that should keep them going is missing. That behaviour is fading.
  • Correction 1, extinction for the loud style: stop granting requests simply because they are loud. Acknowledge briefly and move the issue to a proper channel, so loudness no longer pays.
  • Correction 2, positive reinforcement for the right channel: reply to emailed issues within a fixed time and act on good ones, so raising issues calmly is what gets results.
  • Correction 3, consistency: apply the same rule to everyone. If loud complaints are rewarded even now and then, the behaviour becomes harder to remove.
Marking scheme

Identifying the accidental reinforcement and the accidental extinction: 2 marks. Three corrective moves using the correct tools: 3 marks (1 each).

Q5 · 5 marks

CO3 · L4 · Schedules of reinforcement

A Pune ed-tech start-up pays its counsellors a fixed monthly salary. The founders notice that calls to prospective students rise sharply in the last week of every month, when the manager reviews numbers, and slump in the first two weeks. Identify the reinforcement schedule that explains this pattern, and recommend a schedule that would keep effort steady, with reasons.

A schedule of reinforcement decides when a reward is given. It depends on whether the reward is tied to time (interval) or output (ratio), and whether it is fixed or variable.

  • Current schedule, fixed interval: the salary and the review come on a known date whatever the effort. Effort rises just before the reward or check, then drops. This matches the end-of-month spike.
  • Why it fails here: counsellors learn that the first weeks carry no consequence, so the timing of the review, not the work, drives behaviour.
  • Recommended for output, variable ratio: add a commission or incentive on enrolments. Any call could be the one that converts, so effort stays high and lasting.
  • Recommended for checks, variable interval: replace the fixed review date with unannounced call reviews at random times, so steady effort is the only safe strategy.
  • Balance: keep the salary as a base for security, and add the variable elements on top to drive steady effort.
Marking scheme

Correct identification with the reason for the spike: 2 marks. Recommended schedule(s) with reasons: 3 marks.

Q6 · 5 marks

CO3 · L3 · Cognitive theory of learning

Aman, a junior associate at a Gurugram consulting firm, was never formally trained on the client-billing process. He simply watched how invoices moved between teams during his first year. When the billing executive resigned suddenly, Aman ran the entire month-end billing without help. Explain this using Tolman's cognitive theory of learning, and state the lesson for managers.

Cognitive theory (Tolman) holds that learning is more than reflex. Thinking sits between the stimulus and the response.

  • Mental map: over the year Aman built a mental picture of the billing system: which team raises the invoice, who approves it, where it goes next.
  • Learning without immediate reward: nobody rewarded Aman for noticing the process. He learned it anyway, like Tolman's rats that learned the maze with no food in it.
  • Learning shows when it is useful: the knowledge stayed hidden until the resignation gave him a reason to use it, just as the rats ran fast once food was placed at the end.
  • Expectancy: Aman expected that stepping in would be valued, so he acted on that belief.
  • Lesson for managers: explain the why, not just the what. People who understand how a system works can handle new situations without being told every step. Exposing juniors to whole processes builds capacity for the future.
Marking scheme

1 mark each for mental maps, learning without reward, use when useful (Tolman's rats), and expectancy applied to Aman. 1 mark for the managerial lesson.

Q7 · 5 marks

CO3 · L4 · Social learning theory

At a Kolkata advertising agency, new joiners are seated next to Vikram, the top-billing account manager. Vikram gets results, but he skips client briefs, cuts corners on approvals and is openly praised by the founders. Within months, the new joiners are doing the same. Using the four processes of social learning theory, analyse why this happened.

Social learning theory (Bandura) says people learn by watching others and copying models they find credible or admired. They do not need to be rewarded themselves.

  • Attention: the joiners noticed Vikram because he sits beside them and is the most successful, admired person around. Credible models get attention, whether their habits are good or bad.
  • Retention: they see his shortcuts every day, so the behaviour is easy to remember and recall.
  • Reproduction: skipping briefs and rushing approvals takes no special skill, so the joiners can copy it at once.
  • Motivation: the founders openly praise Vikram. Seeing the model rewarded tells the joiners that this is how to succeed, so they want to copy it.
  • Conclusion: the agency taught the shortcuts without meaning to. Seating and praise made Vikram the model, and a leader or star is always modelling, whether they intend to or not.
Marking scheme

1 mark for each of the four processes applied to the case (4 marks). 1 mark for the conclusion on unintended modelling.

Q8 · 5 marks

CO3 · L5 · Principles of learning

A pharmaceutical company trains its new medical representatives with a single eight-hour lecture on 40 products, delivered in a hotel hall. Trainees take no part in role-plays. Their first feedback comes at the half-yearly appraisal. Most are struggling in the field. Evaluate this training against the five principles of learning.

The principles of learning are the conditions that make training stick. The programme breaks all five.

  • Motivation and readiness: 40 products in one day overloads new reps who lack the basics. They are not ready for that volume, and interest drops.
  • Active practice and repetition: a lecture means listening, not doing. Without role-plays, the reps never practise detailing a product to a doctor.
  • Timely feedback and reinforcement: the first feedback comes after six months. By then wrong habits are fixed. Feedback should follow each attempt.
  • Meaningful, whole-to-part material: a list of 40 products has no big picture. Reps should first see the doctor's journey and the therapy area, then the product details.
  • Transfer: a hotel hall is nothing like a doctor's clinic. Practice in clinic-like conditions, or on field visits with a senior, would carry over to the job.

Verdict: the content may be accurate, but the design ignores how people learn, so it fails to change field behaviour.

Marking scheme

1 mark for each principle named and evaluated against the case.

Q9 · 5 marks

CO3 · L3 · Shaping

Nisha has just joined a Bengaluru property firm as a sales executive. Her manager wants her to log every client interaction in the company's CRM with complete notes, a habit most new joiners resist. Design a shaping plan for Nisha's manager using the four steps of shaping.

Shaping means reinforcing successive approximations: rewarding each small step that moves closer to the target behaviour instead of waiting for the finished article.

  • Target behaviour: every client interaction logged in the CRM, with complete notes, on the same day.
  • Step 1, start where she is: in week one, praise Nisha for logging any interaction at all, even with one-line notes.
  • Step 2, raise the bar in stages: in week two, praise only entries made on the same day. In week three, only entries with the client's needs and next step recorded. The old level no longer earns praise.
  • Step 3, reward often early, then thin out: daily recognition in the first weeks builds the habit. Later, move to occasional checks and monthly recognition so it lasts without the manager.
  • Step 4, never punish the attempt: if an entry is incomplete, coach her on what to add instead of scolding. Punishing a rough step kills the willingness to try the next one.
Marking scheme

Definition and target behaviour: 1 mark. Each of the four steps applied to Nisha: 1 mark each (4 marks).

Q10 · 5 marks

CO3 · L3 · Learning for green behaviour

A private hospital chain in Kochi wants staff to cut single-use plastic in wards and canteens. Past awareness posters had no effect. Using learning for green behaviour, design a plan that applies modelling, reinforcement and shaping.

Learning for green behaviour applies the learning engine to sustainability. Green actions bring no immediate personal payoff, so they rarely become habits on their own. Posters inform, but they do not change behaviour.

  • Model (social learning): senior doctors, nursing superintendents and ward heads visibly use steel bottles and refuse plastic cutlery. Staff copy admired models far faster than they follow posters.
  • Green champions: appoint one respected nurse per ward as a champion, so there is a model close to every shift.
  • Reinforce (operant conditioning): publish ward-wise plastic reduction each month and recognise the best ward in the staff meeting. What gets reinforced gets repeated.
  • Shape (successive approximations): start with one easy habit, such as reusable cups in the canteen. Then move to cloth bags in pharmacies, then plastic-free ward supplies.
  • Link to values: connect the plan to patient and community health, so the habit carries meaning beyond the rule.
Marking scheme

Modelling, reinforcement and shaping, each correctly linked to its theory and applied: 1 mark each (3 marks). 2 further relevant points (champions, why posters fail, link to values): 1 mark each.

Q11 · 5 marks

CO3 · L4 · Factors influencing perception

Two regional managers at a Mumbai insurance firm rate the same employee, Farhan, very differently. Manager A, who prefers people who speak up, rates him "average, lacks initiative". Manager B, who works with him on long data projects, rates him "excellent". Farhan is quiet in meetings and was rated during a hectic quarter-end week. Analyse the difference using the three factors that influence perception.

Perception is the process by which individuals organise and interpret sensory impressions to give meaning to their environment. What is perceived can differ from reality, and three factors explain why.

  • The perceiver, attitudes and interests: Manager A values visible talk, so he reads silence as low initiative. Manager B values careful analysis, so he notices the quality of Farhan's work.
  • The perceiver, experience and expectations: Manager B has seen Farhan's output over long projects. Manager A's view rests on meetings alone.
  • The target: loud, active people grab attention. Farhan is quiet, so he is less noticeable to someone who judges by meetings.
  • The situation: a hectic quarter-end week is a poor setting for judging anyone. Context shapes how the same behaviour is read.
  • Conclusion: the difference lies more in the perceivers and the setting than in Farhan. Since people act on perception, the firm should base ratings on evidence of output, not impressions.
Marking scheme

Definition: 1 mark. Perceiver, target and situation each applied to the case: 1 mark each (3 marks). Conclusion or managerial implication: 1 mark.

Q12 · 5 marks

CO3 · L3 · Inattentional blindness

A production supervisor at an auto-parts plant in Chakan is under pressure to hit daily output targets. He walks the floor every hour counting completed units. After a minor accident, an inspection finds that a guard rail had been missing for two weeks, right next to his walking route. Explain this using inattentional blindness, and outline three safeguards management should put in place.

Inattentional blindness is the failure to see something in plain sight because attention is fully committed to something else. In Simons' selective attention test, viewers counting passes miss something impossible happening right in front of them.

  • Why it happened: the supervisor's attention was locked on counting units. Attention is limited, so the missing rail was not seen, even though he walked past it every hour.
  • Not a personal failing: this is how attention works. Anyone given one narrow task tends to miss the rest.
  • Safeguard 1, a second pair of eyes: assign a separate safety walk by a different person, whose only focus is hazards.
  • Safeguard 2, structured checklists: a short safety checklist on each round forces attention to the items an output-focused eye would skip.
  • Safeguard 3, open reporting: let any worker flag a hazard instantly, so the team's eyes cover what one person misses.
Marking scheme

Definition and explanation of the case: 2 marks. Three safeguards: 3 marks (1 each).

Q13 · 5 marks

CO3 · L4 · Attribution theory

Deepa, an analyst at a Noida fintech, submitted her monthly risk report late. Her manager gathers these facts. Deepa is late only with this report and is on time with everything else. Four of the five analysts who prepare the same report were also late this month. Deepa has never been late with this report before. Apply Kelley's attribution theory to decide whether the delay is internally or externally caused.

Attribution theory says we judge whether behaviour is internally caused (under the person's control) or externally caused (forced by the situation). Kelley's three signals guide that judgement.

  • Distinctiveness is high: Deepa is late only on this report, not on everything. High distinctiveness points to an external cause.
  • Consensus is high: most analysts preparing the same report were also late. When everyone faces the same result, the situation is the likely cause. This points external.
  • Consistency is low: she has never been late with this report before. A one-off points to something in the situation that month, not to the person.
  • Verdict: all three signals point to an external cause, such as late source data, a system change or a new reporting format.
  • Managerial action: fix the cause in the process instead of blaming Deepa. Blaming her would be the fundamental attribution error.
Marking scheme

Each of the three signals correctly read and applied: 1 mark each (3 marks). Correct overall attribution: 1 mark. Managerial implication: 1 mark.

Q14 · 5 marks

CO3 · L3 · Shortcuts in judging people

Identify the perceptual shortcut at work in each of the following moments at a hiring and appraisal committee, and explain it briefly.

  1. "She topped her MBA batch, so her client-handling must be excellent too."
  2. "He's from a small town. He won't manage our international clients."
  3. "I never leave before 9 pm, so anyone who leaves at 6 can't be serious about the job."
  4. "After that brilliant last candidate, this one looks very ordinary."
  5. A manager who believes the sales team is lazy remembers only the days they came in late.

Perceptual shortcuts save time when judging people, but each one injects bias into hiring and reviews.

  1. Halo effect: one standout trait (academic rank) colours the whole impression, including skills nobody has checked.
  2. Stereotyping: the candidate is judged by the group he is placed in (small-town background), not as an individual.
  3. Projection: the manager assumes others share his own values about long hours. He mistakes his mirror for a window.
  4. Contrast effect: the candidate is judged against the one who came just before, not against a fixed standard.
  5. Selective perception: the manager notices only evidence that fits his existing view and filters out the rest.
Marking scheme

1 mark per moment: 0.5 for the correct shortcut and 0.5 for the explanation.

Q15 · 10 marks

CO3 · L4 · Three theories of learning

Compare and contrast stimulus-response theory, cognitive theory and social learning theory. Illustrate how a software services firm could apply each of them while onboarding fresh graduates.

Learning is any relatively permanent change in behaviour that occurs as a result of experience. The three theories explain different slices of how that change happens.

BasisStimulus-responseCognitiveSocial learning
Key thinkerPavlov (classical), Skinner (operant)TolmanBandura
How learning happensThrough a bond between a stimulus and a response, and through consequencesThrough thinking: mental maps and expectanciesThrough watching and copying credible or admired models
Role of rewardCentral. What gets rewarded gets repeatedNot needed at once. Learning can happen without reward and be used laterThe learner need not be rewarded. Seeing the model rewarded is enough
Learner seen asResponding to cues and consequencesAn active thinkerAn observer
Classic evidencePavlov's dogs, Skinner's reinforcementTolman's rats in a mazeThe Bobo doll experiment

Similarities

  • All three explain a lasting change in behaviour that comes from experience.
  • All three give managers a lever: consequences, understanding or models.

Application to onboarding fresh graduates

  • Stimulus-response: praise and points on a leaderboard for every code review passed on the first attempt. A quick, positive consequence makes the habit stick. Late or sloppy submissions get corrective feedback.
  • Cognitive: before any task, walk graduates through how the whole delivery process works, from client request to release. With a mental map, they can handle new problems without being told every step.
  • Social learning: pair each graduate with a respected senior developer whose habits are worth copying. Graduates pay attention to admired models, remember what they see, practise it and copy what they see rewarded.

Conclusion

The theories do not compete. Stimulus-response explains habits built by consequences, cognitive theory explains understanding, and social learning explains copying. A complete manager uses all three together.

Marking scheme

Explanation of the three theories: 3 marks (1 each). Contrast on at least three bases: 2 marks. Similarities: 1 mark. Application of each theory to onboarding: 3 marks (1 each). Conclusion: 1 mark.

Q16 · 10 marks

CO3 · L5 · Consequence tools

The operations head of a large Indian retail chain says, "Punishment is the fastest and most reliable way to change employee behaviour. Fines for late arrival and cash shortages work better than any reward." Evaluate this statement using the four consequence tools of operant conditioning, and recommend the combination store managers should use.

Operant conditioning holds that voluntary behaviour is shaped by its consequences. It gives managers four tools. Two increase behaviour (positive and negative reinforcement) and two reduce it (punishment and extinction).

Where the statement is partly valid

  • Punishment can stop a behaviour quickly: a fine for a cash shortage adds an unpleasant consequence, so careless cash handling may fall soon after.
  • It sets clear limits: for serious breaches, such as safety or theft, a firm consequence signals that the line cannot be crossed.

Where the statement fails

  • Punishment shows what to stop, not what to do: a fine for lateness does not teach good till discipline or customer service. Only reinforcement builds the desired behaviour.
  • It kills the willingness to try: staff who fear fines hide mistakes instead of reporting them, so shortages get covered up rather than fixed.
  • It is less durable: behaviour often returns when the threat is absent, for example on days the manager is away.
  • It ignores the other three tools: positive reinforcement (recognition for zero-shortage weeks), negative reinforcement (staff with clean records are excused from daily cash recounts) and extinction (no longer rewarding a behaviour) can change behaviour without the fear.

Recommended combination

  • Lead with positive reinforcement: publicly recognise punctual staff and accurate tills each week.
  • Use negative reinforcement: remove irritating checks for consistently accurate cashiers.
  • Use extinction: stop the informal rewards that keep bad habits going, such as covering for latecomers.
  • Keep punishment for serious or repeated breaches, and apply it consistently and promptly.

Conclusion

The statement is valid only for stopping serious behaviour in the short term. As a general rule it is invalid, because lasting change comes from reinforcing the right behaviour.

Marking scheme

Explanation of the four tools: 2 marks. Points supporting the statement: 2 marks. Points against: 3 marks. Recommended combination: 2 marks. Conclusion with a clear verdict: 1 mark.

Q17 · 10 marks

CO3 · L5 · Schedules of reinforcement

A consumer-durables company pays its showroom staff a fixed monthly salary and a single annual bonus decided in March. Sales slump for most of the year and jump in February. Management also wants staff to keep showrooms clean and follow up with customers after delivery. Evaluate the current reward system using the schedules of reinforcement, and redesign it to suit each of these goals.

A schedule of reinforcement sets when a reward is given. Rewards can be tied to time (interval) or output (ratio), and can be fixed or variable. Each schedule produces a different pattern of effort.

Evaluation of the current system

  • Monthly salary, fixed interval: it arrives on a known date whatever the effort, so it gives security but no reason to push harder on any given day.
  • Annual bonus decided in March, also fixed interval, with a long gap: effort rises just before the reward and drops afterwards. This explains the February spike and the slump for the rest of the year.
  • Weak link between act and reward: a reward months later teaches very little about any single sale.

Redesign by goal

  • Sales, variable ratio: pay a commission per sale, with occasional bonus prizes on random high-value sales. Any customer could be the one that pays, so effort stays high all year. This is the schedule that makes a slot machine more addictive than a salary.
  • Sales targets, fixed ratio: a fixed incentive for every set number of units, for example every tenth air conditioner, gives high, steady output with short pauses after each reward.
  • Showroom upkeep, variable interval: surprise inspections at random times, with recognition for clean showrooms. Because the check could come any day, standards stay steady.
  • Customer follow-up, fixed interval plus feedback: a monthly recognition for the best follow-up record, combined with immediate praise when a customer compliments a call.
  • Keep the salary: the fixed base provides security. The variable elements drive daily effort on top of it.

Conclusion

The current system rewards the calendar, not the behaviour. Matching each goal to the right schedule, with variable schedules for effort and steady standards, spreads performance across the whole year.

Marking scheme

Explanation of the four schedules: 2 marks. Evaluation of the current system: 3 marks. Redesign for sales, upkeep and follow-up: 4 marks. Conclusion: 1 mark.

Q18 · 10 marks

CO3 · L4 · Attribution errors

After a poor quarter, the zonal head of a bank writes in his report: "Our three underperforming branch managers lack commitment. The two branches that did well succeeded because of the strategy I personally designed." Analyse the attribution errors in this statement, and outline four mechanisms the bank should put in place to make performance attributions fairer.

Attribution theory explains how we decide whether behaviour is internally caused (under the person's control) or externally caused (by the situation). Two predictable errors distort that judgement.

Errors in the zonal head's statement

  • Fundamental attribution error: he blames the three managers' character ("lack commitment") and ignores their situations. Local competition, staff vacancies or a weak local economy may explain the results. When judging others, we overweight character and underweight the situation.
  • Self-serving bias: he credits the successful branches to his own strategy. For ourselves we flip the rule: wins are our talent and failures are someone else's fault.
  • Combined effect: together the two errors make him harsh on others and easy on himself. The bank may then remove capable managers and repeat a strategy that did not cause the success.
  • Missing evidence: he has not checked Kelley's signals. Did all branches in similar markets struggle (consensus)? Do these managers fail on everything (distinctiveness)? Have they failed every quarter (consistency)?

Four mechanisms for fairer attribution

  1. Structured attribution review: before any judgement, the reviewer must record the three Kelley signals for each manager, with data.
  2. Argue the situation first: every review must list external factors (market, staffing, systems) before personal ones. This is the fair-manager habit.
  3. Peer-group comparison: compare each branch with others facing similar conditions, so situational causes become visible.
  4. Independent second reviewer: a reviewer not involved in the strategy checks both the blame and the credit, which limits self-serving bias.

Conclusion

The statement reflects both attribution errors. Fair attribution needs evidence on all three signals and a habit of checking the situation before blaming the person.

Marking scheme

Brief explanation of attribution theory: 1 mark. Fundamental attribution error and self-serving bias identified and applied: 4 marks (2 each). Four mechanisms: 4 marks (1 each). Conclusion: 1 mark.

Q19 · 10 marks

CO3 · L4 · Perception is reality

An employee survey at a Chennai IT company shows that 60 percent of staff believe promotions are decided by favouritism. HR data shows that promotions closely followed performance ratings. The HR head says, "The data proves we are fair, so the survey can be ignored." Analyse why this view is risky, using the concept of perception and the factors that influence it, and suggest how management should respond.

Perception is the process by which individuals organise and interpret sensory impressions to give meaning to their environment. What people perceive can differ greatly from objective reality.

Why ignoring the survey is risky

  • Perception is reality for behaviour: people act on what they believe, not on the HR dataset. Staff who believe promotions are unfair may cut effort, disengage or leave, whatever the data shows.
  • Facts do not travel on their own: if employees cannot see how ratings lead to promotions, the fair process stays invisible.

How the three factors may be producing the belief

  • The perceiver: employees who were passed over have motives and past experiences that colour their reading. Expectations of unfairness lead them to notice cases that seem to confirm it.
  • The target: a few promotions of people close to senior managers are highly noticeable, so they stand out more than the many routine, merit-based ones.
  • The situation: if promotions are announced without explanation, the setting leaves room for rumour. A tense appraisal season makes negative readings more likely.

How management should respond

  • Make the process visible: publish the promotion criteria and show how ratings link to decisions.
  • Explain individual outcomes: give each candidate who was not promoted specific feedback on the gap.
  • Correct the salient cases: where a promotion looks like favouritism, explain the evidence behind it.
  • Listen: hold small forums to understand which cases drive the belief, then address them.

Conclusion

The HR head is managing facts but not perceptions. Because people behave according to their perception, the firm must manage both.

Marking scheme

Definition and "perception is reality": 2 marks. Perceiver, target and situation applied: 3 marks. Four management responses: 4 marks. Conclusion: 1 mark.

Q20 · 10 marks

CO3 · L4 · Shortcuts in hiring

A large FMCG company runs campus interviews where each panel meets 30 candidates in a day. Analyse how the five common shortcuts in judging people could distort the panel's choices, and outline a safeguard against each.

Reading people fully is slow, so the brain takes perceptual shortcuts. They save time but quietly inject bias into hiring, especially when a panel is tired and rushed.

  • Selective perception: panellists notice what matches their own background and interests. An engineer on the panel may notice only technical answers and filter out a candidate's strong people skills.
    Safeguard: score every candidate on the same written criteria, so each panellist must look at every dimension.
  • Halo effect (and horn effect): one standout trait, such as fluent English or a top college name, colours the whole impression. One weak trait, such as nervousness, can drag everything down.
    Safeguard: rate each competency separately before giving an overall score.
  • Contrast effect: after 30 interviews, an average candidate shines after a weak one and sinks after a brilliant one. Interview order should never decide a hire, but here it easily could.
    Safeguard: compare each candidate to a fixed standard, not to the previous one, and review scores together at the end.
  • Stereotyping: judging candidates by gender, region or college type instead of as individuals. It is quick and often wrong.
    Safeguard: use mixed panels and structured questions that test the same skills for everyone.
  • Projection: panellists assume that candidates who share their own values or style will fit best. They mistake their own mirror for a window.
    Safeguard: define "fit" in terms of job behaviours agreed in advance, not personal likeness.

Conclusion

Each shortcut can push the panel to hire the wrong person or reject the right one. Structure, fixed standards and diverse panels keep the shortcuts in check.

Marking scheme

For each of the five shortcuts: 1 mark for explaining its effect on hiring and 1 mark for a matching safeguard (10 marks).

Q21 · 10 marks

CO3 · L5 · Perception and decision-making

The marketing director of a snacks company has always been sceptical about the new millet-based product line. After one weak quarter in two pilot cities, she recommends shutting it down. She does not look at the four cities where sales are growing, or at a supply disruption in the pilot cities. Critically evaluate her decision using the link between perception and individual decision-making.

Individual decision-making is a response to a perceived problem. A manager cannot decide about a situation until she has first perceived and interpreted it, so every distortion in perception carries straight into the choice.

How her perception shaped the problem

  • The perceiver's expectations: her early scepticism set her up to read any weak result as proof that the line had failed. She saw the product as she already saw it.
  • Selective perception: she noticed the two weak cities that matched her view and filtered out the four growing cities that did not.
  • Attribution error: she put the weak quarter down to the product itself (internal to the product) and ignored the supply disruption (an external, situational cause). This mirrors the fundamental attribution error.
  • The situation: a single quarter in two pilot cities is a narrow context. Judging the whole line on it reads a temporary setback as a permanent fault.

Evaluation of the decision

  • Well executed, wrong problem: shutting the line could be carried out perfectly and still be the wrong decision, because the problem was misperceived. The real problem was supply, not demand.
  • Cost of the error: the company would lose a product that is growing in four cities and an early position in a healthy-snacks segment.

Checks the company should apply

  • Review the full picture, all six cities, before any decision.
  • Ask what in the situation could explain the result before blaming the product.
  • Have a colleague without a prior view examine the same data.

Conclusion

A decision is only as clean as the perception it is built on. Hers was built on a distorted reading, so it should be paused until the perception is corrected.

Marking scheme

Statement of the perception and decision link: 2 marks. Perception distortions identified in the case (any three): 3 marks. Evaluation of the decision: 2 marks. Corrective checks: 2 marks. Conclusion: 1 mark.

Q22 · 10 marks

CO3 · L5 · Learning for green behaviour

A Bengaluru tech park launched a sustainability drive with posters, an email from the CEO and a one-time webinar asking employees to save power and segregate waste. Six months later, nothing has changed. Evaluate why the drive failed using learning theory, and design a learning-based programme to make green behaviour a habit.

Learning for green behaviour applies the learning engine to sustainability. Green actions rarely bring an immediate personal payoff, so they do not become habits on their own.

Why the drive failed

  • Information is not learning: posters, an email and a webinar gave knowledge. Learning is a relatively permanent change in behaviour that comes from experience, and the drive provided no experience.
  • No consequence followed the behaviour: under operant conditioning, behaviour that brings no reward fades. Nobody noticed or recognised green actions.
  • No visible models: under social learning, people copy admired models. If leaders kept lights and screens on, staff copied that instead.
  • Too much at once, no practice: the drive broke the principles of learning. There was no active practice, no timely feedback and no small starting step.

A learning-based programme

  • Model: leaders and floor-level green champions visibly switch off screens, carry their own bottles and segregate waste. Staff learn faster from what they see than from what they read.
  • Reinforce: show each floor its weekly power use on a screen in the lobby and recognise the best floor every month. What gets reinforced gets repeated.
  • Shape: start with one easy habit, switching off monitors at night. Once that sticks, add waste segregation, then car-pooling.
  • Timely feedback: smart bins and meters that show results the same day link the action to its effect.
  • Practice and transfer: place segregated bins at every desk cluster, so the habit is practised where people actually work.

Conclusion

The drive told people what to do but never taught them to do it. Modelling, reinforcement and shaping turn green intentions into everyday habits.

Marking scheme

Reasons for failure linked to learning theory (any three): 3 marks. Programme using model, reinforce and shape: 3 marks. Two further points from the principles of learning: 2 marks. Explanation of green learning: 1 mark. Conclusion: 1 mark.

Q23 · 10 marks

CO3 · L5 · Shaping vs punishment

A call-centre team leader in Gurugram trains new agents on a complex insurance script. He waits for a fully correct call before giving any praise, and publicly criticises every imperfect call. Attrition among new agents is high and few master the script. Critically evaluate his approach, and design a shaping-based alternative.

Shaping means reinforcing successive approximations, rewarding each small step that moves closer to the target behaviour instead of waiting for the finished article.

Evaluation of the team leader's approach

  • Waiting for perfection means no reinforcement: a complex script is rarely right at first. If praise comes only for a perfect call, new agents may go weeks with nothing to reward, so there is no consequence to build the behaviour.
  • Punishing every attempt: public criticism is punishment. It tells agents what is wrong but not what to do, and it kills their willingness to try the next call.
  • Fear and exit: constant criticism with no recognition explains the high attrition. Agents leave before they can learn.
  • Ignores how complex skills are built: people do not leap to competence. They climb in steps.

Shaping-based alternative

  1. Define the target: a full call that follows the script, with correct disclosures and a clear close.
  2. Start where they are: in week one, praise a correct, confident greeting and verification, even if the rest of the call is rough.
  3. Raise the bar in stages: in week two, praise only calls that also explain the policy correctly. In week three, only calls that handle objections. In week four, only the full script with the close.
  4. Reward often early, then thin out: immediate praise after almost every good call in the first weeks, then occasional recognition once the habit is in place, so it lasts without him.
  5. Never punish the attempt: replace public criticism with private coaching on the one next thing to improve.

Conclusion

The current approach relies on punishment and waits for a finished performance that never comes. Shaping builds the skill step by step and keeps agents willing to learn.

Marking scheme

Definition of shaping: 1 mark. Critical evaluation of the current approach (any three points): 3 marks. Shaping plan using the four steps applied to the script: 5 marks. Conclusion: 1 mark.

Q24 · 10 marks

CO3 · L5 · Social learning and modelling

"A leader's visible behaviour teaches more than any policy document." Evaluate this statement using social learning theory, and explain how an organisation can use modelling deliberately to build ethical and customer-focused behaviour.

Social learning theory (Bandura) holds that people learn by watching others and copying models they find credible or admired, without needing a reward of their own.

Why the statement holds

  • Evidence from the Bobo doll experiment: children who watched an adult hit an inflatable doll copied the same aggression when left alone. Nobody rewarded them. Watching was enough.
  • Leaders are the most watched models: they are credible and admired, so they capture attention. A policy document competes poorly with what people see every day.
  • Seeing outcomes motivates copying: if a leader cuts corners and is rewarded, staff learn that this is how to succeed, whatever the policy says.
  • Leaders model all the time: a leader is always modelling, whether they mean to or not.

Limits of the statement

  • Copying happens only if all four processes occur. Staff must notice the leader (attention), remember the behaviour (retention), be able to do it (reproduction) and want to do it (motivation).
  • Policies still set the standard. Modelling works best when the leader's behaviour and the written policy say the same thing.

Using modelling deliberately

  • Attention: place respected, ethical performers where new joiners can watch them, for example in customer calls and client meetings.
  • Retention: share short stories of leaders handling ethical dilemmas or difficult customers well, so the behaviour is remembered.
  • Reproduction: let new joiners practise the modelled behaviour in role-plays before facing real customers.
  • Motivation: publicly reward models who show ethical and customer-focused behaviour, so others see that it pays.

Conclusion

The statement is largely valid. People copy what credible leaders do, and the organisation should design who gets watched and what gets rewarded, not rely on documents alone.

Marking scheme

Explanation of social learning with the Bobo doll evidence: 2 marks. Arguments supporting the statement: 2 marks. Limits: 1 mark. Deliberate modelling using the four processes: 4 marks. Conclusion: 1 mark.

Q25 · 15 marks

CO3 · L5 · Learning theories in onboarding

Zentrix Analytics, a fast-growing data firm in Hyderabad, hires 60 graduates every quarter. New hires spend their first week watching recorded videos on company tools, then join project teams. They take about four months to reach full productivity. Team leads complain that new hires copy the habits of whoever sits nearest, including some seniors who skip documentation and miss code reviews but are known as "fast deliverers" and often praised in all-hands meetings.

Recognition at Zentrix comes only in the annual awards. New hires say they rarely get feedback in their first months and often do not know whether they are doing well. Several have said, "Nobody explains why we follow these processes." Last quarter, 12 of the 60 new hires left within six months.

(a) Critically evaluate Zentrix's onboarding using the theories and principles of learning. [7.5]

(b) As an OB consultant, formulate a five-step learning redesign plan for the first three months of a new hire's journey. [7.5]

(a) Evaluation of the current onboarding

Learning is any relatively permanent change in behaviour that occurs as a result of experience. Zentrix's onboarding produces learning, but often of the wrong behaviour.

  • Social learning working against the firm: new hires pay attention to the seniors near them, especially those praised as "fast deliverers". They remember and copy skipped documentation and missed reviews, and the public praise gives them the motivation to do so. Zentrix is teaching bad habits through its models.
  • Operant conditioning missing: the only recognition is annual, a fixed interval with a very long gap. Good behaviour in the first months brings no consequence, so it is not reinforced. Praise for speed alone also reinforces cutting corners.
  • Cognitive learning ignored: "Nobody explains why" shows that new hires have no mental map of how the processes fit together. Without understanding, they cannot handle situations on their own.
  • Principles of learning broken: recorded videos give no active practice. There is no timely feedback. The material is not whole-to-part. Practice away from real projects does not transfer to the job.
  • Outcome: slow productivity, inconsistent habits and the loss of 12 of 60 hires within six months show that the onboarding is not producing lasting, correct behaviour.

(b) Five-step learning redesign plan

  1. Week 1, build the mental map (cognitive): replace videos with a guided walk through the full delivery process, from client request to release, explaining why documentation and reviews matter.
  2. Weeks 1 to 2, choose the models (social learning): pair each new hire with a senior selected for good habits, not just speed. Stop praising speed alone in all-hands meetings, so the wrong models lose their pull.
  3. Weeks 2 to 6, shape the target behaviour: break the target (well-documented, reviewed code) into steps. Reward the first documented module, then only reviewed and documented work, then full independent delivery.
  4. Weeks 2 to 12, reinforce often, then thin out (operant): give weekly feedback and recognition in the first month, move to fortnightly, then to monthly. Add a quarterly award for quality as well as speed.
  5. Month 3, practise on real work and review: new hires deliver a small live task under supervision (transfer), followed by a structured review of what they learned. Track productivity and early attrition to check the redesign is working.

Conclusion: by choosing models, explaining the why and reinforcing the right behaviour early, Zentrix can shorten the path to productivity and keep more of its new hires.

Marking scheme

(a) Social learning, operant conditioning and cognitive theory applied: 4.5 marks (1.5 each). Principles of learning: 2 marks. Overall judgement: 1 mark. (b) Five steps in a logical sequence, each led by management and linked to a learning concept: 7.5 marks (1.5 each).

Q26 · 15 marks

CO3 · L5 · Perception in performance appraisal

At Meridian Financial Services in Mumbai, appraisals are written by team leads in the final week of March, alongside year-end closing. This year, several complaints reached HR. Sunita, a quiet analyst who built the firm's new reconciliation tool, was rated "meets expectations" with the comment "not a team player, rarely speaks up". Arjun, a confident presenter, was rated "outstanding" though two of his three projects were delayed. Ramesh, aged 54, was left off the automation project because "he may struggle with new tools". One team lead rated everyone who stayed late as "highly committed", saying, "That is how I built my career."

HR also found that a candidate reviewed right after the year's best performer was rated much lower than in previous years, though her output had improved. Employees now say the ratings "depend on who you are, not what you do", and two strong performers have started looking for other jobs.

(a) Analyse the perception errors in Meridian's appraisals using the factors influencing perception and the common shortcuts in judging people. [7.5]

(b) Formulate a five-step intervention plan for Meridian's leadership to make appraisals fair. [7.5]

(a) Analysis of perception errors

Perception is the process of organising and interpreting sensory impressions to give meaning to the environment. At Meridian, what team leads perceived differs from what employees actually achieved.

  • Halo and horn effects: Arjun's confident presenting created a halo that hid his delayed projects. Sunita's quietness worked as a horn effect that hid her major contribution.
  • Stereotyping: Ramesh was judged by his age group ("may struggle with new tools") instead of as an individual.
  • Projection: the team lead who equated staying late with commitment assumed others share his own values. He mistook his own mirror for a window.
  • Contrast effect: the candidate reviewed after the best performer was judged against him, not against a fixed standard, so her improvement was missed.
  • Selective perception: once a lead saw Sunita as "not a team player", he noticed the meetings where she stayed silent and filtered out the reconciliation tool she built for the whole team.
  • The perceiver: leads who built their careers on visibility and long hours carry attitudes and expectations that favour people like themselves, so their ratings reflect their own values.
  • The target: loud, visible behaviour grabs attention. Quiet, steady work is less noticeable, so it gets under-rated.
  • The situation: writing appraisals in the busiest week of the year gives leads little time to check evidence, so they fall back on shortcuts.
  • Perception is reality: employees now believe ratings depend on who you are. Because people act on perception, strong performers are starting to leave.

(b) Five-step intervention plan

  1. Pause and re-review the disputed ratings: leadership reopens the complaints (Sunita, Arjun, Ramesh and the contrast case) and re-rates them on documented evidence of output.
  2. Redesign the appraisal criteria: introduce written, behaviour-based criteria and rate each criterion separately before the overall score. This limits halo and horn effects.
  3. Train team leads on perception errors: run a short workshop on the five shortcuts, using anonymised examples from this year, so leads can recognise their own patterns.
  4. Change the setting and process: move appraisals away from year-end closing, ask for evidence throughout the year, and calibrate ratings across teams against a fixed standard, which counters the contrast effect.
  5. Communicate and monitor: explain the new process to employees, give each person specific feedback, and track rating patterns by age, gender and team to catch stereotyping early.

Conclusion: Meridian's ratings reflect the raters' shortcuts more than employees' work. Evidence, structure and a better setting will make appraisals fair and help retain strong performers.

Marking scheme

(a) Shortcuts correctly identified in the case (any four): 4 marks. Perceiver, target and situation factors: 2.5 marks. Link to "perception is reality" and retention: 1 mark. (b) Five steps in sequence, led by leadership: 7.5 marks (1.5 each).

Q27 · 15 marks

CO3 · L5 · Attribution in a sales team

Sterling Home Appliances moved its 40 field sales staff in Gujarat to a new mobile ordering app in July. In the following quarter, 32 of the 40 missed their targets. Before July, most of them had met targets for two years. Many said the app crashed in rural areas with weak signal. The same staff had no trouble with other tasks such as collections and dealer visits.

The regional sales head, Mr Mehta, told the board, "The team has become complacent. They need tighter discipline." In the same meeting, he said the strong results of the previous year were "thanks to my targeting model". He has proposed salary cuts for all staff who missed targets.

(a) Apply Kelley's attribution theory to the facts, and assess the validity of Mr Mehta's statements, identifying any attribution errors. [7.5]

(b) Formulate a five-step plan for Sterling's management to respond fairly and restore performance. [7.5]

(a) Attribution analysis

Attribution theory says we judge whether behaviour is internally caused (under the person's control) or externally caused (by the situation), using three signals.

  • Distinctiveness is high: the staff struggle only with app-based ordering, not with collections or dealer visits. High distinctiveness points external.
  • Consensus is high: 32 of 40 missed targets at the same time. When nearly everyone fails together, the situation is the likely cause. This points external.
  • Consistency is low: the same people met targets for two years before July. The failure is new, which points to a recent change in the situation, the app.
  • Verdict: all three signals point to an external cause, the app failing in weak-signal areas.

Validity of Mr Mehta's statements

  • "The team has become complacent" is not valid. It is the fundamental attribution error: he overweights the staff's character and ignores the situation.
  • "Thanks to my targeting model" shows self-serving bias. He credits past success to himself while blaming the current failure on others.
  • Consequence: salary cuts would punish people for a situational problem, damage morale and push good staff to leave, while the real cause, the app, stays unfixed.
  • A decision built on a wrong reading: individual decision-making is a response to a perceived problem. Mr Mehta perceives a discipline problem, so his decision targets discipline. Even if the cuts are carried out perfectly, they solve the wrong problem and targets will still be missed.

(b) Five-step plan

  1. Diagnose with evidence: management collects data on app crashes by area and compares target misses in high-signal and low-signal regions to confirm the external cause.
  2. Withhold the salary cuts: suspend the proposed penalty until the review is complete, so staff are not punished for the situation.
  3. Fix the situation: add an offline ordering mode and a phone back-up channel for weak-signal areas, and work with the vendor on the crashes.
  4. Adjust targets and support: set fair, temporary targets for the transition, give hands-on app support in the field, and recognise staff as performance recovers.
  5. Build fair attribution into reviews: require managers to record distinctiveness, consensus and consistency before linking any shortfall to individuals, and have a second reviewer check both blame and credit.

Conclusion: the evidence points to the app, not the people. Fixing the situation and judging fairly will restore performance faster than discipline.

Marking scheme

(a) Three Kelley signals applied: 3 marks. Correct overall attribution: 1 mark. Fundamental attribution error and self-serving bias identified, with an assessment of the statements: 3.5 marks. (b) Five steps in sequence, led by management: 7.5 marks (1.5 each).

Q28 · 15 marks

CO3 · L5 · Consequences and modelling in safety

Vardhan Textiles runs a spinning mill near Coimbatore. Workers earn a piece-rate bonus for every kilogram of yarn above target. To save time, many skip the two-minute machine safety check at the start of each shift. Supervisors, who are also judged on output, rarely do the check themselves and joke about it being "paperwork". When a worker is caught skipping the check, the penalty is a small fine, applied only when the safety officer visits, about once every two months.

Workers who do the check carefully produce slightly less yarn and earn smaller bonuses. In the last year there have been four minor injuries. Management has put up new safety posters and sent a circular, but behaviour has not changed.

(a) Analyse why workers keep skipping the safety check, using operant conditioning, schedules of reinforcement and social learning theory. [7.5]

(b) Formulate a five-step plan for Vardhan's management to make the safety check a lasting habit. [7.5]

(a) Why the unsafe behaviour persists

  • Skipping is positively reinforced: skipping the check saves time, which adds yarn and bonus. A pleasant consequence follows the unsafe behaviour, so it is repeated.
  • Doing the check is effectively punished: careful workers earn smaller bonuses. A loss follows the safe behaviour, so it fades.
  • The piece-rate bonus is a fixed ratio schedule: it rewards every extra kilogram, which produces high output. Nothing in it rewards safety, so the strongest schedule in the mill pushes the wrong way.
  • The penalty is weak and rare: a small fine every two months is punishment applied on an infrequent schedule. Workers learn that the chance of being caught on any day is low. A delayed, rare consequence teaches very little.
  • Safe behaviour gets no recognition: nobody praises workers who do the check, so the reward that could keep careful habits alive is missing. Over time even careful workers drift towards skipping.
  • Social learning: supervisors are credible models. Workers pay attention to them, see them skip the check and joke about it, and see them rewarded for output. This gives the motivation to copy.
  • Information alone fails: posters and a circular change knowledge, not behaviour. Learning needs experience and consequences.

(b) Five-step plan

  1. Change the models first: management requires supervisors to perform the check visibly at every shift start and judges them on safety as well as output. Joking about the check stops.
  2. Remove the reward for skipping: redesign the bonus so it is paid only on shifts with a completed, signed safety check. Skipping no longer pays.
  3. Reinforce the safe behaviour: add a small safety bonus for every month without a skipped check, and recognise safe teams in the weekly meeting.
  4. Make checks frequent and unpredictable: replace the two-monthly visit with random spot checks across shifts, a variable interval schedule, so the safe behaviour stays steady.
  5. Shape and monitor: start by rewarding completion of the check, then reward checks done correctly, and track injuries and compliance monthly. Thin out the extra rewards once the habit is stable.

Conclusion: the mill's consequences and models teach workers to skip the check. Changing what is rewarded and who is copied will make safety the habit.

Marking scheme

(a) Operant conditioning (reinforcement of skipping, loss for checking): 2.5 marks. Schedules (piece-rate and rare penalty): 2 marks. Social learning through supervisors: 2 marks. Why posters fail: 1 mark. (b) Five steps in sequence, led by management: 7.5 marks (1.5 each).

Q29 · 15 marks

CO3 · L5 · Green behaviour and perception

Aurora Consulting, a Delhi-based firm of 800 people, announced a "Net Zero Office" campaign. Employees were asked to cut printing, avoid single-use plastic and use the metro for client visits. The CEO launched it with a long email and a poster series. In the same month, the leadership team flew business class to an offsite in Goa, and partners continued to print full decks for every meeting.

Employees began calling the campaign "green theatre". Those who had joined from firms where similar campaigns quietly faded expected the same here. Staff who started carrying bottles and taking the metro received no acknowledgement. Within three months, printing and plastic use were back to earlier levels. A survey shows most employees believe "leadership does not mean it".

(a) Critically evaluate why the campaign failed, using learning for green behaviour and the concept of perception. [7.5]

(b) Formulate a five-step plan for Aurora's leadership to relaunch the campaign so that green behaviour becomes a habit. [7.5]

(a) Why the campaign failed

Learning for green behaviour applies modelling, reinforcement and shaping to sustainability. Perception is how people organise and interpret what they see, and people act on that interpretation.

  • Wrong modelling: under social learning, staff copy credible leaders. Leaders flying business class and partners printing decks modelled the opposite of the campaign, and that teaches more than any email.
  • No reinforcement: employees who carried bottles and took the metro got no acknowledgement. Behaviour that brings no reward fades, which is what happened within three months.
  • No shaping: three big changes were asked for at once, with no small starting habit and no staged progress.
  • Information instead of learning: an email and posters gave knowledge, not experience. Learning requires a lasting change in behaviour.
  • The perceiver's expectations: staff who had seen campaigns fade elsewhere expected the same, so they read every sign through that lens.
  • Selective perception: the business-class trip was the most noticeable fact, and it confirmed their expectations, so the CEO's words were filtered out.
  • Perception is reality: because employees believe "leadership does not mean it", they behave as if that is true, whatever the leaders intended.

(b) Five-step relaunch plan

  1. Leaders model first and visibly: leadership commits publicly to rail or economy travel for short trips and paperless meetings, and starts before asking anything of staff.
  2. Address the perception openly: the CEO acknowledges the offsite mistake in a town hall and shares measurable targets, so the perception of "green theatre" is challenged with evidence.
  3. Start small and shape: relaunch with one habit, paperless internal meetings. Add plastic-free floors once that sticks, then metro travel for local client visits.
  4. Reinforce what people do: publish monthly printing and plastic data by team, recognise the best teams, and thank individuals who take the metro. Quick, visible recognition links the action to a reward, so the behaviour is repeated.
  5. Sustain with champions and review: appoint green champions in each practice as visible models, review progress each quarter, and thin out the special recognition once the habits hold.

Conclusion: the campaign failed because leaders modelled the opposite, nobody reinforced the right behaviour, and employees' perception confirmed it was not serious. Visible modelling, open communication and steady reinforcement can turn it into a habit.

Marking scheme

(a) Learning for green behaviour, covering modelling, reinforcement and shaping: 3.5 marks. Perception, covering perceiver expectations, selective perception and "perception is reality": 3 marks. Overall judgement: 1 mark. (b) Five steps in sequence, led by leadership: 7.5 marks (1.5 each).
