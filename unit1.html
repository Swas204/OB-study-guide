1.1Meaning of Organisational Behaviour

Definition to memorise

Organisational Behaviour is a field of study that investigates the impact that individuals, groups and structure have on behaviour within organisations, for the purpose of applying such knowledge towards improving an organisation's effectiveness.

Stephen P. Robbins and Timothy A. Judge

Examiners give marks for breaking that definition apart, so learn the four working parts:

  1. It is a field of study, not a department. Nobody's job title is "OB manager". It is a body of knowledge every manager uses.
  2. It studies three things: individuals, groups and structure.
  3. It studies them inside organisations. Behaviour at a family dinner is not OB. Behaviour in a team meeting is.
  4. It is applied. The knowledge exists to improve effectiveness, not to satisfy curiosity.

Two supporting definitions

  • Keith Davis: "OB is the study and application of knowledge about how people act within an organisation."
  • Fred Luthans: "OB is the understanding, prediction and management of human behaviour in organisations."

Luthans is the most useful to quote, because it names the three goals of OB in sequence.

Example · why OB exists as a subject

Two data entry clerks at a bank, Ravi and Meera. Same age, same qualification, same salary, same desk, same supervisor. Ravi clears 120 files a day and volunteers to train the new joinee. Meera clears 70 and leaves at 5:30 exactly.

Economics cannot explain this, because the pay is identical. Accounting cannot. OB is the subject that asks why the difference, and what can the manager do. The answer might lie in Meera's personality, her attitude to the job, her perception that promotions are unfair, the norms of her small work group, or the design of her role. Each of those is a topic in this course.

The three levels of analysis

OB is built like a three-storey building, and you cannot understand the upper floors without the lower ones. Notice that this is exactly the order of your syllabus, which makes the whole course one argument instead of five disconnected lists.

Why this matters to a commerce student

Technical skill gets you the job. People skill decides how far you go. A chartered accountant who cannot handle a client who disagrees, a finance analyst who cannot present to a sceptical board, or an audit senior who cannot motivate three juniors through busy season will hit a ceiling that has nothing to do with their accounting knowledge.

Example · the systems approach in one decision

A company introduces a cash incentive for the single top salesperson each month. One small change. The side effects: salespeople stop sharing leads with each other, team cohesiveness falls, and customer service is flooded with complaints from customers who were oversold. The systems approach predicts exactly this kind of spillover, which is why OB treats an incentive scheme as a behavioural intervention and not an accounting entry.

1.4Challenges and Opportunities for OB

The framing that earns marks

Open your answer with this line: every one of these is simultaneously a challenge, because it creates a problem for managers, and an opportunity, because it creates a situation where OB knowledge adds value. Then group them rather than listing seventeen items randomly.

A. Economic and global pressures

1. Responding to economic pressure. In a downturn managers must handle layoffs, pay freezes, longer hours and falling morale. OB helps with how to communicate bad news and keep the survivors engaged.

Example

During the 2023 Indian IT slowdown, firms froze hiring and cut variable pay. The managers who explained the reasoning honestly kept their teams. The ones who went silent lost their best people first, because silence is read as bad news plus contempt.

2. Continuing globalisation. Four sub-points examiners look for:

  • Increased foreign assignments, so you may manage people whose needs and attitudes differ from yours.
  • Working with people from different cultures. What motivates an American engineer may not motivate a Japanese one.
  • Adapting to differing cultural and regulatory norms, including working hours, holidays, gender norms, and what counts as a gift rather than a bribe.
  • Overseeing the movement of jobs to low-cost countries, and the ethics of that.
Example

An Indian team working with a Dutch client finds the Dutch feedback rude. The Dutch find the Indian team's "yes sir" unhelpful, because it hides bad news. Neither side is wrong. This is the power distance gap, covered in Unit 2 Part B.

B. Workforce composition

3. Workforce demographics. Ageing populations in some countries, very young workforces in others. India's median age is around 29, so Indian firms manage large cohorts of first-job employees whose expectations differ sharply from their parents' generation.

4. Workforce diversity. Covered fully in 1.5.

C. The changing nature of work

  • 5. The gig economy. Full-time jobs replaced by contract, freelance and platform work.
  • 6. Networked and virtual organisations. Teams that never meet, managed by output rather than observation.
  • 7. Social media at work. Where is the line between a private post and damage to the employer's reputation?
  • 8. Work-life conflict. The boundary between office and home has dissolved because the office is a phone in your pocket. Flexitime, work from home and the right to disconnect are the OB responses.
  • 9. Coping with temporariness. Jobs, teams and products all have shorter lives, so employees must live with continuous flux.
Example · the gig problem

A Swiggy or Zomato delivery partner has no fixed manager, no appraisal, and is directed by an algorithm. How do you motivate, train or build loyalty in someone the company does not formally employ? OB has to answer that now, and none of the classical theories were built for it.

D. Technology and artificial intelligence

10. Technology, automation and AI. AI changes three things at once: which jobs exist, how decisions get made, and how employees feel about being measured. Fear of replacement is now a live OB variable. Technostress (Unit 2 Part C) and AI in managerial decision-making (Unit 3) both descend from this challenge.

Example

A bank installs an AI tool that scores loan applications. Credit officers now feel deskilled and distrusted, so they quietly override it. The technology worked. The behaviour around it did not, and only one of those was budgeted for.

E. People and management practices

  • 11. Improving customer service. Most employees now work in service roles where their mood is part of the product, which creates emotional labour.
  • 12. Improving people skills. Explaining, listening, motivating, giving feedback, designing jobs.
  • 13. Working in flatter organisations and empowering people. Fewer layers means employees decide more for themselves, which requires trust.
  • 14. Stimulating innovation and change, against the reality that people resist change (Unit 5).
  • 15. Improving ethical behaviour. Employees face ethical dilemmas where right and wrong are genuinely unclear. Organisations respond with codes of conduct, ethics training and whistle-blower protection.
  • 16. Sustainability and green pressure. Regulation, ESG investors and employee expectation now push firms to reduce environmental harm, which is why sections 1.9 to 1.12 exist.
  • 17. Creating a positive work environment. The rise of Positive Organisational Behaviour and Psychological Capital (Fred Luthans).

Psychological Capital has four components, remembered as HERO:

1.5Diversity in Organisations

Definition

Workforce diversity means organisations are becoming more heterogeneous, made up of people who differ in gender, age, race, ethnicity, caste, religion, ability, sexual orientation and background.

The key distinction: surface-level versus deep-level diversity

This is the single most examinable idea in the diversity topic.

Surface-level diversityDeep-level diversity
What it isDifferences in characteristics that are easily observedDifferences in values, personality, attitudes and work preferences
ExamplesAge, gender, race, ethnicity, disability, language, physical appearanceWork ethic, risk tolerance, openness, religious belief, political view, communication preference
Visible?Yes, immediatelyNo, only after interaction
Effect over timeMatters a lot at first, then fadesMatters little at first, then becomes decisive
Main riskTriggers stereotypes and prejudiceCauses real, lasting friction if ignored
Example

A five-person project team has two men and three women, ages 22 to 48, from three different states. That is surface-level diversity, and on day one everybody notices it. By week three nobody cares. What matters by then is that two members believe in finishing work early while one prefers to start the night before the deadline. That deep-level difference is what causes the argument.

The managerial lesson

As people get to know each other, surface-level differences become less important and deep-level similarities become more important. So the fastest way to reduce prejudice in a diverse team is not a training session. It is creating conditions where members interact enough to discover what they actually share.

Discrimination: the dark side of diversity

Discrimination in organisational terms means allowing our behaviour to be influenced by stereotypes about groups of people rather than by the individual's actual characteristics.

FormMeaningExample
Discriminatory policies or practicesActions denying equal opportunity or rewardOlder workers systematically not sent for training on new software
Sexual harassmentUnwanted sexual advances or conduct creating a hostile environmentInappropriate jokes tolerated in a sales team
IntimidationOvert threats or bullying aimed at members of a groupA senior repeatedly threatening a junior from a minority community
Mockery and insultsJokes and stereotypes, often passed off as humourImitating a colleague's regional accent in meetings
ExclusionLeaving certain people out of opportunities, events or informationThe team lunch plan shared only on a group that excludes the one woman on the team
IncivilityDisrespectful treatment, interrupting, ignoring opinionsA manager checking their phone every time one particular colleague speaks
The point students miss

The bottom three forms are the hardest to police, because they are informal, often unintentional, and easily denied. No policy document prevents someone from checking their phone. This is precisely why inclusion matters more than headcount targets, and it is the bridge to section 1.7.

Example · stereotyping in one sentence

"She just got married, so she will not want the Dubai posting." The manager never asked her. That single unasked question is discrimination in practice, and it will not appear in any complaint register.

1.6Biographical Characteristics

Definition

Biographical characteristics are personal characteristics that are objective and easily obtained from an employee's personnel records.

Read this before the sub-topics

The exam wants to know what the research actually found, and on most of these the evidence contradicts the common assumption. Students lose marks by writing the stereotype instead of the finding. The pattern below is: state the stereotype, then state the evidence.

1. Age

  • Stereotype: older workers are rigid, bad with technology and less productive.
  • Evidence: there is no relationship between age and job performance in most jobs. Older workers show lower avoidable absence but higher unavoidable absence from genuine illness. They have lower turnover, and job satisfaction tends to be higher, though the relationship is sometimes U-shaped across a career.
Example

A 55-year-old branch manager is slower on the new core banking software but makes far fewer compliance errors and almost never quits. Judging her on typing speed alone would be an expensive mistake.

2. Sex and gender

  • Evidence: there are few important differences between men and women affecting job performance. Problem-solving ability, analytical skill, competitive drive, motivation, sociability and learning ability show no consistent difference.
  • Where differences appear they usually reflect role expectations and social conditioning, not capability. Historically higher absence and turnover among women largely tracks household and caregiving load.
  • The real issue is persistent pay gaps, fewer women in senior roles, and bias in evaluation.
Example

Identical resumes sent out with a male and a female name receive different callback rates. The resume did not change. The perception did.

3. Race and ethnicity, and in India caste, religion and region

  • Employees tend to favour colleagues of their own group in evaluations, promotion decisions and pay raises.
  • Race-related discrimination persists in hiring even where formal policy prohibits it.
  • In India the operative variables are caste, religion, region and language, and reservation policy is the legal response.

4. Disability

  • Law in India: the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016 requires non-discrimination and reasonable accommodation.
  • Evidence: workers with disabilities receive higher performance evaluations on average, yet are perceived as less employable and face lower hiring rates. There is also evidence they face lower performance expectations, which is its own problem.
  • Reasonable accommodation means adjusting the job or workplace so a capable person can do it: screen readers, ramps, flexible hours, sign interpreters.
Example

A visually impaired analyst with screen-reader software processes reconciliation files as accurately as anyone else. The disability was never the barrier. The absence of the software was.

5. Tenure

Evidence: tenure and productivity are positively related. Tenure is a strong negative predictor of turnover and absence, and relates positively to satisfaction. Past behaviour in a job predicts future behaviour better than most tests do.

6. Religion

Religion affects work through dress, dietary needs, prayer times, holidays and occasionally refusal of certain duties. Discrimination on religious grounds is prohibited, but subtle bias is common, such as assuming a visibly religious employee is less suited to client-facing work.

Example

A team schedules every important review at 1:00 pm on Friday. A Muslim colleague misses half of them because of Jumu'ah prayers. Nobody intended to exclude him. The calendar did it, which is how most exclusion actually happens.

7. Sexual orientation and gender identity

Often invisible, so the employee must decide whether to disclose, which is itself a source of stress. Organisational responses include non-discrimination policies that name orientation and identity explicitly, gender-neutral washrooms, partner benefits and correct pronoun use.

8. Cultural identity

Employees carry an attachment to the culture of their country or region of origin, and they want it respected rather than erased.

Ability, which is taught alongside

Seven dimensions
Intellectual abilities
  • Number aptitude
  • Verbal comprehension
  • Perceptual speed
  • Inductive reasoning
  • Deductive reasoning
  • Spatial visualisation
  • Memory

Ability-job fit

Performance suffers when ability is below the job's requirement, which is obvious, and also when it is far above it, which is not. The overqualified employee becomes bored, disengaged and dissatisfied, then leaves. Hiring the most qualified candidate available is not automatically the right decision.

1.7Diversity Management

Definition

Diversity management is the process and set of programmes by which managers make everyone more aware of and sensitive to the needs and differences of others, so that diversity improves performance instead of causing conflict.

The distinction to lead with

Diversity is about headcount. Inclusion is about culture. A firm can be statistically diverse and still exclusionary. Or in the line that is worth quoting: diversity is being invited to the party, inclusion is being asked to dance.

Stage detail

Attracting and selecting. Use recruitment sources that reach under-represented groups. Strip biased language from job ads, phrases like "aggressive go-getter" or "young and energetic". Use structured interviews with identical questions for every candidate, because the unstructured interview is where bias enters most easily. Blind screening removes name, gender and college.

Diversity in groups. Whether diversity helps or hurts depends on the task:

  • For creative, problem-solving and judgement tasks, diverse groups perform better, because they bring more perspectives.
  • For routine, speed-based tasks needing tight coordination, diverse groups can perform worse at first because of communication friction.
  • The benefit appears only when the group has enough time and a shared goal. Without those, it fragments into subgroups, called faultlines.

Effective diversity programmes do three things: teach managers the legal framework for equal opportunity, show how a diverse workforce serves a diverse customer base, and build personal skills for communicating across differences.

Inclusion practices: Employee Resource Groups such as a women's network or a differently-abled employees' forum; mentoring and sponsorship, because under-represented employees typically have weaker informal networks; bias interruption in appraisal meetings, where someone is formally tasked with challenging vague judgements like "not leadership material"; and psychological safety (1.12), which is what actually lets minority voices speak.

Why manage diversity at all? Three arguments

Argument 1
Legal and compliance

Discrimination is prohibited under Articles 14, 15 and 16 of the Constitution of India, the Equal Remuneration Act, and the RPwD Act 2016.

Argument 2
Ethical and human values

Equal treatment is right in itself, whether or not it produces a business benefit. This is the argument the syllabus's "human values" theme wants.

Argument 3
Business case

Wider talent pool, better understanding of diverse markets, better decisions, stronger employer brand.

Example · the business case in one story

A bank designing a loan product for women entrepreneurs had an all-male product team, and the launch failed. They rebuilt the team with women who had actually applied for such loans. The redesign succeeded. The first team was not less intelligent. It was less informed, and no amount of market research substitutes for someone in the room who has lived the problem.

What goes wrong

  • Tokenism: hiring one person from a group to display diversity, then ignoring their input.
  • Backlash: majority employees perceiving diversity efforts as reverse discrimination.
  • Diversity fatigue: repeated training with no change in who actually gets promoted, which destroys the programme's credibility permanently.

1.8Organisational Behaviour Models

Two different things share this name

"OB models" can mean Keith Davis's five models of managerial assumptions, or the Robbins and Judge inputs-processes-outcomes model. The question could ask for either, so learn both. If the question just says "explain OB models", answer with Davis's five, then add the I-P-O model as a second part.

Part A · Keith Davis's five models of OB

These describe the assumptions managers hold about people, and they evolved historically. Each model produces a different employee response.

ModelManagerial orientationEmployee orientationPsychological resultNeed metPerformance result
1. AutocraticPower, authorityObedienceDependence on bossSubsistenceMinimum
2. CustodialMoney, economic resourcesSecurity and benefitsDependence on organisationSecurityPassive cooperation
3. SupportiveLeadership, supportJob performanceParticipationStatus and recognitionAwakened drives
4. CollegialTeamwork, partnershipResponsible behaviourSelf-disciplineSelf-actualisationModerate enthusiasm
5. SystemTrust, community, meaningPsychological ownershipSelf-motivationWide range, self-actualisationPassion, commitment to goals
One example per model
  1. Autocratic: a small construction site where the contractor shouts orders and workers who argue are sent home. Output is the bare minimum.
  2. Custodial: a traditional public sector undertaking with a permanent job, pension, quarters and medical benefits. Employees are loyal but not energetic, because the benefits arrive regardless of effort.
  3. Supportive: a manager who coaches rather than commands and asks "what is blocking you?" Employees start taking initiative.
  4. Collegial: a design studio or research lab where the boss is first among equals, titles are loose, and people police their own quality.
  5. System: a firm where employees genuinely believe in the mission and describe the work as "ours". A well-run social enterprise, or the Tata group's stated ethos of trusteeship.
The point that lifts a ten-mark answer

Do not write that the models are a straight progression from bad to good. The right model is contingent on the work, the workforce and the environment. An autocratic model is correct in a fire drill or an army operation, where speed and unquestioned compliance save lives. A system model is correct for knowledge work. Saying this shows you have understood the contingency approach from section 1.2 rather than memorised a list.

Part B · The Robbins and Judge OB model

This is the model that organises the entire textbook, and by extension your syllabus.

The outcome variables, defined

OutcomeMeaningExample

Example · the whole model in one line

An input (an employee high on conscientiousness) feeds a process (she sets herself demanding goals, which is motivation) and produces an outcome (high task performance and low absenteeism). Change the input or the process and the outcome changes. That is OB in a sentence.

1.9Green Organisational Behaviour (GOB)

Definition

Green Organisational Behaviour is the study and application of how individuals, groups and organisational systems behave in ways that reduce environmental harm and support environmental sustainability at work.

Put simply: normal OB asks how we get people to perform. GOB asks how we get people to perform without damaging the environment, and how environmental responsibility becomes part of ordinary work behaviour rather than a poster on the wall.

Why it entered the syllabus

Three pressures converged. Regulation: India's BRSR disclosures are now mandatory for the top listed companies, so environmental performance has to be measured and reported. Investors: ESG funds screen companies on environmental behaviour. Employees: younger employees increasingly choose employers partly on environmental record.

None of those pressures achieve anything unless individual employees change what they do. That is a behaviour problem, so it belongs in OB.

Green Human Resource Management

The HR practices that actually produce green behaviour. This list is easy marks.

HR functionGreen version
RecruitmentSignal environmental values in the employer brand; ask about environmental awareness
SelectionInclude environmental values in person-organisation fit assessment
TrainingEnvironmental awareness, waste segregation and energy-saving training
Performance appraisalPut green targets for paper, energy and waste into the KRA sheet
RewardRecognition and incentives for environmental suggestions and measured savings
Job designBuild environmental responsibility into the role itself
Employee involvementGreen suggestion schemes, green teams, environmental committees
Exit and disciplineTreat serious environmental negligence as a disciplinary matter

Five types of green behaviour at work

The Ones and Kaplan taxonomy, which is useful because it describes things employees actually do.

01
Working sustainably

Changing how you do your own task to reduce impact, such as batching lab tests to save power.

02
Conserving

Avoiding waste, reusing, recycling, switching off.

03
Avoiding harm

Preventing pollution, following environmental rules, reporting spills.

04
Influencing others

Educating and encouraging colleagues, advocating green practices.

05
Taking initiative

Lobbying for change and starting green programmes, sometimes at personal risk.

Example · why the OB approach beats the poster

A printing firm wanted to cut paper waste by 20 per cent.

The non-OB approach: a poster saying "Save Paper". Nothing happened.

The GOB approach: at the individual level, operators were trained on why misprints happen and given a target, which is learning plus motivation. At the group level, each shift reported its own waste figure on a visible board, so a team norm formed. At the organisation level, waste reduction entered the supervisor's appraisal and the best shift was recognised monthly.

Waste fell 31 per cent. The difference was not a better poster. It was using OB variables.

Barriers to green OB

  • Attitude-behaviour gap: employees say they care but do not change behaviour, usually because the green option is inconvenient.
  • Greenwashing: the organisation announces green goals without changing systems, which makes employees cynical.
  • Low green self-efficacy: employees do not believe their small action matters.
  • Conflicting targets: you cannot ask someone to cut travel while paying them per client visit made.

1.10Sustainability and Workplace Behaviour

Definition

Sustainability is meeting present needs without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.

Brundtland Commission, 1987
Example

A textile unit cuts costs by dumping untreated dye water into a canal. Profit rises this quarter, so Profit passes. The surrounding village loses its water, so People fails. The river is polluted, so Planet fails. Two years later the plant is shut by the pollution control board. The cost saving was never a saving. It was a deferred liability that nobody booked.

How sustainability shows up in behaviour

1. Organisational Citizenship Behaviour for the Environment (OCBE)

Definition

OCBE is voluntary, discretionary employee behaviour aimed at improving environmental outcomes, which is neither required by the job nor formally rewarded.

Three types: eco-helping (helping colleagues adopt green behaviour), eco-initiatives (suggesting and starting improvements), and eco-civic engagement (participating in the organisation's environmental programmes).

Example

An employee carries a steel bottle, switches off the conference room AC after a meeting nobody else attended, and points out that the office is over-ordering stationery. No KRA requires any of that, and no appraisal will record it.

2. Green behaviour becomes part of task performance

Once green targets enter the appraisal, green behaviour shifts from voluntary OCBE into required task performance. That is a deliberate managerial move, and it is how green behaviour stops depending on goodwill.

3. Sustainability alters the psychological contract

The psychological contract is the unwritten set of mutual expectations between employee and employer. Employees increasingly expect environmental responsibility, so a visible gap between stated values and actual behaviour is experienced as a breach of that contract. The result is cynicism and exit.

Example

A firm publicises a net-zero pledge in its annual report while its own office tips carefully segregated waste into one bin. Employees notice. Engagement scores fall, and the annual report is the reason.

4. Person-organisation fit

Candidates increasingly self-select on environmental reputation, so sustainability becomes a recruitment and retention variable rather than a reporting one.

5. Sustainability demands long-horizon behaviour, which humans are poor at

People discount future costs heavily, a bias called present bias. Sustainability asks for a cost now against a benefit in twenty years, which is why it needs structural support through defaults, norms and targets, and not appeals to conscience.

1.11Environmental Psychology in the Workplace

Definition

Environmental psychology is the study of the relationship between people and their physical surroundings, including how the built environment affects behaviour, mood, health and performance.

The core claim

The physical setting is not neutral background. It is an active variable, and it is one of the very few OB variables a manager can change without changing a single person. That is what makes it practically attractive.

Nature and greeneryPlants, natural materials and views of nature reduce stress and restore attention. Called biophilic designEven a single potted plant at the desk measurably lowers reported stress
The standard illustration · the open-plan office

Open-plan offices were adopted to increase collaboration and cut cost. Research found the opposite of the intended effect: face-to-face interaction fell sharply when firms moved to open plan, while electronic messaging rose.

The explanation is pure environmental psychology. With no acoustic or visual privacy, employees manufacture privacy for themselves by putting on headphones and switching to text. The lesson: ask how people will adapt to a space, not only what the space is meant to encourage.

Concepts to name in an answer

  • Restorative environments: settings that let depleted attention recover, typically involving nature, moderate stimulation and a sense of being away. This is Attention Restoration Theory.
  • Environmental stressors: noise, crowding, poor air, heat. Their effect is worse when uncontrollable and unpredictable. A loud noise you can stop bothers you far less than the identical noise you cannot.
  • Perceived control: giving employees even token control, an adjustable blind or a desk fan, reduces stress more than the physical change alone justifies.
  • Biophilia: the human tendency to seek connection with nature, used as a design principle.
  • Place attachment and territoriality: emotional bonding with a physical space, which supports identification with the organisation.
  • Environmental cues and nudges: physical prompts that change behaviour without instruction, such as placing the recycling bin nearer than the general waste bin.
The bridge to the next section

The physical workplace both shapes green behaviour, through bin placement, visible energy meters and default printer settings, and signals whether the organisation's green claims are genuine. An office with a single overflowing mixed-waste bin under a "Zero Waste Campus" poster teaches employees that the policy is decoration. The bin is a more credible communication than the poster, and employees read it correctly.

1.12Pro-Environmental Behaviour and Psychological Safety at Work

The syllabus joins these two headings deliberately, because psychological safety is a major reason pro-environmental behaviour either happens or does not. Part C below makes that link, and it is the likely ten-mark question.

Part A · Pro-Environmental Behaviour (PEB)

Definition

Pro-environmental behaviour is behaviour that consciously seeks to minimise the negative impact of one's actions on the natural and built world.

At work it is often called Employee Green Behaviour (EGB)

Two theories worth naming:

  • Theory of Planned Behaviour (Ajzen): behaviour follows intention, and intention is formed by attitude towards the behaviour, subjective norms (what others expect), and perceived behavioural control (whether I believe I can do it). Useful because it explains why someone with green attitudes still does nothing: the norms or the control are missing.
  • Norm Activation Model: behaviour follows a personal norm, which activates only when a person is aware of the consequences and accepts responsibility for them.
The attitude-behaviour gap · the most examinable problem here

Ninety per cent of an office says recycling matters. The segregated bins still come out mixed. Why?

  • Inconvenience: the correct bin is two floors down.
  • No feedback: nobody ever learns whether their segregation made any difference.
  • Diffusion of responsibility: "someone in housekeeping sorts it anyway".
  • No social proof: nobody else is doing it, so the norm says do not bother.
  • Low self-efficacy: "one cup will not change the climate".

How to close it: make the green option the easy default, make behaviour visible, give feedback, build a group norm, and have leaders model it. Four of those five are OB interventions, not environmental ones.

Part B · Psychological safety at work

Definition

Psychological safety is a shared belief held by members of a team that the team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking.

Amy Edmondson, Harvard Business School

In plain language: can I speak up here, admit a mistake, ask a basic question, or disagree with the boss, without being punished, humiliated or labelled?

Two clarifications examiners reward
  1. It is not niceness. It is not everyone agreeing and being pleasant. High psychological safety often looks like frequent, direct, uncomfortable disagreement, precisely because people are not afraid to have it.
  2. It is not lowered standards. Edmondson's matrix below makes this precise.

The target is the learning zone: high standards and high safety together. The most dangerous quadrant is the anxiety zone, because problems there are concealed until they become disasters.

The evidence · Google's Project Aristotle

Google studied 180 of its own teams to find what separated the effective ones. It was not IQ, not seniority, not who was on the team. The strongest predictor was psychological safety, followed by dependability, structure and clarity, meaning, and impact.

Consequences
High psychological safety
  • Errors reported early
  • People ask for help
  • Bad news reaches the top
  • Employees suggest improvements
  • Learning from failure
  • Minority views heard, so diversity pays off
Consequences
Low psychological safety
  • Errors hidden until they explode
  • People pretend to understand
  • Bad news filtered out at every level
  • Employee silence
  • The same failure repeats
  • Diversity present but unheard, so only tokenism
Example · the aviation classic

In cockpits with a steep authority gradient, junior pilots who noticed the captain's error did not clearly say so, and aircraft crashed. The industry's response was Crew Resource Management, which is essentially engineered psychological safety: any crew member is required to challenge any other, using a fixed escalation script. Accident rates fell.

Example · the same thing in an Indian office

A junior article assistant spots that a client's TDS reconciliation is off by a large amount, but says nothing, because the last person who questioned that senior was mocked in front of the team. The error goes into the filing. The cost of low psychological safety is not hurt feelings. It is a notice from the department.

Practical manager behaviours

  • Speak last in a meeting, not first, so your view does not anchor everyone.
  • Ask "what are we missing?" rather than "any questions?"
  • React to the first piece of bad news with thanks, because everyone is watching your reaction and deciding whether to report the second piece.
  • Admit your own errors out loud. This grants permission more effectively than any policy document.
  • Attack the problem, never the person.

Related concepts to name

  • Employee voice: discretionary communication of ideas and concerns intended to improve things.
  • Employee silence: deliberately withholding relevant information. Three types: acquiescent (resigned, nothing will change), defensive (fear of consequences), prosocial (withheld to protect others or confidentiality).
  • Learned helplessness: after repeated futile attempts to be heard, employees stop trying even once conditions improve.
  • Trust: willingness to be vulnerable to another. Psychological safety is essentially trust at the group level.

Part C · Why the syllabus puts these two together

Hold this argument ready, because it is the likely ten-mark link question.

Most environmental improvement at work starts as bad news or an inconvenient suggestion from someone junior: the effluent reading looks wrong, we are over-ordering, this supplier's claim does not add up, that valve is leaking. Every one of those is an interpersonal risk, because the employee is contradicting a process, a senior, or a cost target.

The one-line version for an answer

Green policies are designed at the top, but environmental problems are seen at the bottom, and psychological safety is what decides whether that information ever travels upward. A firm can have a perfect environmental policy, green targets in the appraisal and a glossy sustainability report, and still fail, because the people who can see the problems do not believe it is safe to mention them.

Case study practice · Section C style

A mid-sized garments exporter in Tirupur announces a sustainability programme: segregated waste bins on every floor, a target to cut water use by 15 per cent, and a "Green Champion" award. Six months later water use is unchanged, the bins come out mixed, and the award has gone twice to the same manager, who nominated himself. Meanwhile a supervisor who reported that the dyeing unit was bypassing the treatment plant at night has been transferred to another shift.

(a) Identify the OB concepts at work here. (b) Why did the programme fail despite a correct policy? (c) What would you recommend?

Answer skeleton for the case

Concepts to name: attitude-behaviour gap (the bins), convenience and default design, absent green HRM linkage (no appraisal consequence, no credible award process), tokenism and greenwashing, destroyed psychological safety (the transferred supervisor teaches everyone else to stay silent), low green self-efficacy, no feedback loop on the water data, and leadership failing to model the behaviour.

Recommendations: put green targets into supervisory KRAs so behaviour stops being voluntary; publish unit-level consumption data weekly so there is feedback; redesign the physical defaults so the green option is the easy one; fix the voice channel and visibly protect the person who raised the effluent issue, because nothing else will work until that is reversed; and use green teams so peer norms carry the behaviour rather than posters.
