Power, Conflict and Negotiation: Dynamics of Organisational Behaviour

Four forces run every organisation: who leads, who holds power, how people clash and settle, and how the whole place changes. This page follows the depth of the class notes, with Aswathappa as the anchor text.

CO5 · L5 Evaluate Depth follows class notes Sustainability Human Values Professional Ethics

Leadership · sections 5.1 to 5.5

Definition

Leadership is the ability to influence a group toward the achievement of a vision or set of goals.

Robbins and Judge

The syllabus asks for five contemporary lenses on leadership. Learn each one by its one-line signature, because that line is what an examiner looks for first.

5.1 · Shared leadership

Shared leadership means leadership is distributed across the team, not owned by one person. Members lead where they are strongest. The person with the most relevant knowledge takes the lead on that part of the work, then steps back when the task moves on.

Example

In a product launch team at a Bengaluru start-up, the data analyst leads the pricing decision, the designer leads the packaging call, and the sales lead runs the dealer plan. The team manager coordinates but does not decide everything.

5.2 · Transformational leadership

It is easiest to learn by contrast with transactional leadership.

Transactional
Maintains the system
  • Leads through exchange
  • Clear tasks
  • Rewards for hitting targets
  • Correction when targets are missed
Transformational
Changes the system
  • Inspires people to go beyond self-interest
  • Works through the four I's (below)
  • Raises the team's sense of purpose
IIdealised influence: a role model people admire and trust
IInspirational motivation: a vision that energises
IIntellectual stimulation: encourages people to question and think afresh
IIndividualised consideration: attention to each person's growth
Exam tip

Close any comparison with the line: where transactional leadership maintains the system, transformational leadership changes it.

5.3 · Authentic leadership

Authentic leaders are self-aware, transparent and values-driven. They know who they are and what they stand for, and they act on those values openly. They lead by being genuine, and this is what builds trust, the main outcome of this style. Followers who trust the leader share information and commit to the leader's goals.

Example

A branch head admits in a team meeting that she misjudged a target and explains what she will change. Her team trusts her more, because what she says matches what she does.

5.4 · Ethical and responsible leadership

  • Ethical leadership: leads by personal moral principle, fairness, honesty and integrity, and role-models right conduct for others.
  • Responsible leadership: widens accountability beyond shareholders to employees, society and the environment, and owns the full impact of decisions.

The difference: ethical leadership is about the leader's own conduct, while responsible leadership is about whom the leader answers to.

Watch for
What pushes good people to bad calls
  • Peer pressure
  • Obedience to authority
  • Hubris
Guard with
Ethical decision-making safeguards
  • A clear code of conduct
  • Safe whistleblower channels
  • An independent board or audit committee

5.5 · Leadership for sustainability

Leadership for sustainability embeds long-term environmental and social goals into the vision of the organisation. Success is judged on the triple bottom line: people, planet and profit, not profit alone. Such leaders think long term and weigh the effect of each decision on all three.

Example

A managing director makes water reduction a target for every plant head, reports it next to profit in the board pack, and links part of the bonus to it. The goal sits inside the vision, not in a separate CSR file.

5.6Power: Meaning and Sources

Definition

Power is the capacity to influence. Politics is what people do to get and use it.

French and Raven identified five sources of power:

SourceWhere it comes from
LegitimatePosition and title
RewardAbility to give valued things
CoerciveAbility to punish or threaten
ExpertKnowledge and skill
ReferentBeing liked and admired
Exam tip · expert power

Expert power is the most tenuous source. It is narrow to the field, it is lost as others learn the same thing, and it is gone if the knowledge stops mattering. Yet on a problem that turns on that expertise, it can outweigh a legitimate superior.

5.7Power and Politics in Organisations

Definition

Organisational politics is the use of power to get outcomes not sanctioned by the organisation.

Politics rises when resources are scarce, goals are unclear and trust is low.

Tactics to watch for:

  • Impression management: shaping how others see you, for example taking visible credit.
  • Coalition building: lining up allies to push a position.
  • Controlling information: holding back or filtering what others get to know.

Managing politics:

  • Set clear goals and rules.
  • Allocate scarce resources fairly and openly.
  • Communicate with transparency.
  • Lead by example.
  • Confront destructive politicking early.

5.8Conflict: Process, Sources and Outcomes

Conflict builds in five stages.

  • Sources (Stage 1): communication, structure and personal variables. Role conflict, clashing expectations from two masters such as customers and management, is a common structural source.
  • Functional conflict lifts the quality of ideas, surfaces problems early and drives change.
  • Dysfunctional conflict drains energy and trust.
Example · role conflict

A bank relationship manager is told by customers to waive charges and by management to collect them. The clash comes from the structure of the role, not from either person.

5.9Conflict Management

There are five handling styles, placed on two dimensions: assertiveness (trying to satisfy your own concerns) and cooperativeness (trying to satisfy the other party's).

  • Competing: push to win your point.
  • Collaborating: work on the real problem so both sides fully win.
  • Compromising: each side gives a little.
  • Avoiding: step back from the disagreement.
  • Accommodating: give in to keep the relationship smooth.
Exam tip

There is no best style, only the right one for the situation. Say this in any answer on conflict handling.

5.10The Negotiation Process

Negotiation is how two or more parties settle a conflict by agreeing terms. It runs in five steps:

  1. Preparation: know your goals, the other side and the facts.
  2. Ground rules: agree who negotiates, where, by when and how.
  3. Clarification: each side explains and justifies its position.
  4. Bargaining: the give and take that produces an agreement.
  5. Closure: the agreement is formalised and put into action.
Example

A union and plant management prepare their wage numbers, agree to meet weekly for a month, explain their positions, trade wages against shift timings, and close with a signed settlement.

5.11Negotiation Strategies

Distributive
Win-lose · fixed pie
  • One side's gain is the other's loss
  • Each tries to claim the biggest share
Integrative
Win-win · grow the pie
  • Both sides look for a solution where each gains
  • Builds the long-term relationship

Distributive bargaining suits a one-off deal with a stranger. Integrative bargaining suits parties who will keep working together, such as a firm and its long-term supplier.

Example

Two managers fighting over one fixed budget are in a distributive negotiation. If they find that one needs the money this quarter and the other next quarter, they can both get what they need, which is integrative.

5.12Global Implications of Negotiation

Culture shapes three things in a negotiation:

  • Pace: some cultures move fast to a deal, others take time.
  • Style: direct and open in some cultures, indirect and polite in others.
  • Deal or relationship first: some cultures want the contract settled first, others want trust built before any deal.
Example

An Indian supplier meeting a Japanese buyer should expect several meetings to build the relationship before price is discussed, while an American buyer may want terms on the table in the first meeting.

5.13Organisational Culture: Definition and Functions

Definition

Organisational culture is a system of shared meaning held by members that distinguishes the organisation from other organisations.

Robbins and Judge

Functions of culture:

  • Defines boundaries, so one organisation is distinct from another.
  • Gives members a sense of identity.
  • Builds commitment to something larger than self-interest.
  • Gives stability, the social glue that holds the organisation together.
  • Acts as a control mechanism that shapes attitudes and behaviour.
Remember

Culture can also be a liability. A strong culture can block change, make diversity harder and clash with another culture after a merger.

5.14Creating and Sustaining Culture

Creating: culture starts with the founders. They hire people who think like them, socialise those people into their way of doing things, and act as role models.

Sustaining: three forces keep it alive.

  • Selection: hiring people who fit the existing values.
  • Top management: what senior leaders say and do sets the norms.
  • Socialisation: new employees learn the culture in three stages, prearrival, encounter and metamorphosis.

Employees learn culture through stories, rituals, material symbols and language.

5.15Organisational Culture and Climate

BasisCultureClimate
MeaningShared values and assumptionsShared perceptions of what it feels like to work here
DepthDeep, often unspokenSurface level, felt day to day
ChangeSlow to changeCan change quickly
Measured byObservation over timeEmployee surveys

Climate can be specific, such as a safety climate or an ethical climate. In short, culture is what the organisation is, and climate is how it feels.

5.16Creating a Culture for Sustainability and Innovation

Both need people who will try new things. Traits of such a culture:

  • Freedom to experiment, and tolerance of failure.
  • Psychological safety, so people raise ideas and concerns.
  • Time and resources set aside for new ideas.
  • Rewards for new ideas and sustainable results, alongside output.
  • Leaders who model the behaviour and set long-term goals.
Example

A firm that runs a yearly idea challenge for cutting waste, funds the winning ideas, and does not punish pilots that fail is building this culture.

5.17Green Organisational Culture

A green organisational culture is one where environmental values are shared by members and shape everyday decisions and behaviour.

  • Leadership commitment to environmental goals.
  • Green values in selection, training and appraisal.
  • Employee involvement through green teams and suggestions.
  • Recognition and rewards for green behaviour.
  • Open reporting of environmental performance.

The test is behaviour: when the green option costs more, does the organisation still choose it?

Remember

Logos, slogans and posters are not a green culture. If green claims are not matched by practice, it is greenwashing.

5.18Spirituality and Organisational Culture

Definition

Workplace spirituality is the recognition that people have an inner life that nourishes and is nourished by meaningful work in the context of a community.

Robbins and Judge

It is not about organised religion. Characteristics of a spiritual organisation:

  • A strong sense of purpose.
  • Trust and respect.
  • Humanistic work practices, such as flexible hours.
  • Tolerance of employee expression.

Outcomes: higher commitment and job satisfaction, and lower absenteeism and turnover.

5.19Organisational Change: Meaning and Forces for Change

Organisational change is any alteration in the structure, technology, people or processes of an organisation. Planned change is intentional and goal oriented.

Forces for change:

  • Nature of the workforce: more diversity, new generations.
  • Technology: automation, AI, digital tools.
  • Economic shocks: recessions, price swings, a pandemic.
  • Competition: global rivals, e-commerce, new entrants.
  • Social trends: environmental expectations, work-life balance.
  • World politics: regulation, trade policy.

5.20Resistance to Change and Overcoming It

Individual sources
Why people resist
  • Habit
  • Security
  • Economic factors
  • Fear of the unknown
  • Selective information processing
Organisational sources
Why organisations resist
  • Structural inertia
  • Limited focus of change
  • Group inertia
  • Threat to expertise
  • Threat to power
  • Threat to resource allocation

Overcoming resistance (Kotter and Schlesinger):

  • Education and communication
  • Participation and involvement
  • Facilitation and support
  • Negotiation and agreement
  • Manipulation and co-optation
  • Explicit and implicit coercion
Exam tip

Start with education and participation. Manipulation and coercion work fast but damage trust, so present them as last resorts.

5.21Approaches to Managing Organisational Change

Lewin's three-step model:

  1. Unfreezing: prepare people by showing why the old way must go.
  2. Movement: introduce the new way.
  3. Refreezing: make the new way stick through rules and rewards.

Kotter's eight steps:

  1. Create a sense of urgency.
  2. Form a powerful guiding coalition.
  3. Create a vision.
  4. Communicate the vision.
  5. Empower others to act on it.
  6. Plan for and create short-term wins.
  7. Consolidate gains and produce more change.
  8. Anchor the changes in the culture.

Action research: collect data, diagnose the problem, act, then evaluate, in a cycle. Organisational development (OD) uses planned interventions such as team building and survey feedback to improve the organisation.

5.22Behavioural Change for Sustainability

Sustainability targets are met only when everyday behaviour changes. Tools that work:

  • Nudges: make the green option the easy default, such as double-sided printing as standard.
  • Feedback: show people their energy or waste figures regularly.
  • Social norms: tell people what most colleagues already do.
  • Green champions: respected employees who model the behaviour.
  • Rewards and recognition: celebrate green results.
Example

An office puts each floor's monthly power use on a screen in the lobby and names the most improved floor. Usage falls without any new rule.

Revision and case practice

Five-mark questions

  1. Distinguish transactional from transformational leadership.
  2. Explain the four I's of transformational leadership.
  3. What pushes good people to bad ethical calls, and how can an organisation guard against it?
  4. Explain French and Raven's five sources of power. Why is expert power the most tenuous?
  5. What is organisational politics? When does it rise?
  6. Explain the five conflict-handling styles.
  7. Distinguish distributive from integrative negotiation.
  8. Distinguish organisational culture from organisational climate.

Ten-mark questions

  1. Explain shared, transformational, authentic, ethical and responsible leadership. Which suits a sustainability initiative best?
  2. Discuss power and politics in organisations, the common political tactics, and how a manager can manage politics.
  3. Explain the conflict process. Distinguish functional from dysfunctional conflict.
  4. Explain the negotiation process and the impact of culture on global negotiations.
  5. Explain the sources of resistance to change and the ways to overcome it, with reference to Lewin's model.
Case study practice · Section C style

Arjun, a new plant head, is asked to cut energy use by 20 per cent. He announces the target, puts up posters, and threatens to dock bonuses if numbers do not fall. The senior supervisors, who have run the plant for twenty years, agree in meetings but change nothing. Bonuses are still paid only on output. A young engineer suggests a cheaper motor upgrade, but the maintenance chief blocks it and keeps the cost data to himself. After six months energy use is down 3 per cent and two good engineers have resigned.

(a) Identify the power, politics and change problems in this case. (b) What should Arjun do now?

Answer outline
  • Power: Arjun relies on legitimate and coercive power only. He has no referent power with the supervisors.
  • Politics: the maintenance chief is controlling information to protect his position.
  • Resistance: habit, security and threat to expertise among the senior supervisors. The output-only bonus is structural inertia.
  • Lewin: there was no unfreezing. Posters and threats skipped the step of showing why change is needed.
  • Way forward: education and participation first, link part of the bonus to energy savings, approve the motor upgrade as a short-term win, and share cost data openly.
  • Leadership: move from transactional threats to transformational and shared leadership, letting the engineer lead on the technical fix.
