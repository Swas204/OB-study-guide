Leadership · sections 5.1 to 5.5

Definition, needed before the five types

Leadership is the ability to influence a group toward the achievement of a vision or set of goals.

Robbins and Judge
BasisManagementLeadership
Concerned withCoping with complexityCoping with change
Core activityPlanning and budgeting, organising and staffing, controlling and problem solvingSetting direction, aligning people, motivating and inspiring
Source of influenceFormal authority attached to the positionMay be formal or informal. Non-sanctioned influence is often more important

The line to use, from John Kotter: organisations need both, and are generally over-managed and under-led. Note also that not all leaders are managers and not all managers are leaders, which the informal groups in Unit 4 demonstrate.

5.1 · Shared leadership

Definition

Shared leadership is a dynamic, interactive influence process among individuals in a group, in which leadership is distributed among team members rather than concentrated in a single appointed leader, and members lead one another towards the achievement of goals.

When it works: knowledge work; highly interdependent tasks; teams of experts where no single person holds all the expertise; complex, novel problems; self-managed and cross-functional teams; virtual teams across time zones where a single leader cannot be present for every decision.

Benefits
Why use it
  • Uses the expertise of all members rather than one person's.
  • Higher commitment, since members have real ownership.
  • Faster decisions, because they do not queue behind one person.
  • Develops leadership capability across the team, which is a succession benefit.
  • Reduces the single point of failure.
Risks
What goes wrong
  • Ambiguous accountability, which is the group decision weakness from Unit 4. If everyone leads, nobody is answerable.
  • Coordination cost, and slower decisions where speed matters.
  • Potential for power struggles over who leads what.
  • Struggles in high power distance cultures, where members wait for direction.
  • Requires high trust, maturity and psychological safety, so it fails in exactly the teams that most need help.
The point to make

Shared leadership does not abolish the formal leader. It changes the formal leader's job, from making decisions to creating the conditions in which others can: setting the shared purpose, securing resources, defining decision rights, and holding the boundary. A vertical leader who simply withdraws has not created shared leadership, only a vacuum.

5.2 · Transformational leadership

Bernard Bass, building on James MacGregor Burns. Transformational leaders inspire followers to transcend their own self-interests and have an extraordinary effect on them. The contrast is with transactional leaders, who guide followers towards established goals by clarifying role and task requirements.

Transactional leadership, for the comparison

  • Contingent reward: exchanges rewards for effort, promises rewards for good performance.
  • Management by exception, active: watches for deviations from rules and standards and takes corrective action.
  • Management by exception, passive: intervenes only when standards are not met.
  • Laissez-faire: abdicates responsibility and avoids decisions. The least effective style of all, and worse than no leader at all, because the position is occupied.
The full range leadership model

These are not opposites but a range, from laissez-faire at the bottom through management by exception and contingent reward to the four I's at the top. The key point: transformational leadership builds on transactional leadership rather than replacing it. A leader who inspires but never clarifies tasks or delivers promised rewards will not produce performance. Vision without contingent reward is a speech.

How it works: transformational leaders encourage creativity, decentralise responsibility, set more challenging goals, build follower self-efficacy (Unit 3), create a shared vision that produces higher goal commitment, and generate trust. The evidence base is strong, with links to higher performance, satisfaction, creativity and lower turnover across levels, countries and job types.

Criticisms, needed for the L5 evaluation
  1. Conceptual overlap. The four dimensions are highly correlated with one another and the construct overlaps heavily with charismatic leadership, so it may not be four distinct things.
  2. The dark side. The same skills serve destructive ends. Charisma plus a self-serving purpose produces a leader who is highly effective at achieving something harmful, and history supplies plenty of examples. This is why socialised charismatic leadership, oriented to others' interests, is distinguished from personalised charismatic leadership, which serves the leader. Connect this to the Dark Triad in Unit 2 and to McClelland's two faces of power.
  3. Follower dependence. Very strong charisma can produce dependence rather than development, so the team cannot function without the leader.
  4. Vision can outrun reality, and inspired followers are less likely to challenge it, which is groupthink from Unit 4.
  5. Charisma is situationally dependent. It matters most where the task has high ideological content and where there is stress or uncertainty, which is why crises produce charismatic leaders.

5.3 · Authentic leadership

Definition

Authentic leaders know who they are, know what they believe in and value, and act on those values and beliefs openly and candidly. Their followers consider them ethical people.

Developed by Bruce Avolio, William Gardner and Fred Luthans, whose text is on your reading list

The primary outcome is trust, and trust is the mechanism through which authentic leadership produces its other effects: higher OCB, engagement, and willingness to raise concerns. Because followers believe the leader means what they say, information travels upward.

Example · relational transparency in one sentence

A managing director asked in a town hall whether there will be layoffs says: "I do not know yet. Here is what would have to be true for it to happen, here is when I will know, and I will tell you the same day I do."

Compare "our people are our greatest asset". The first answer is uncomfortable and believable. The second is comfortable and destroys credibility, because everyone in the room knows it is not an answer. Authenticity is frequently less pleasant than its alternative, which is why it is rare.

Criticisms

The construct is young and loosely defined, and measurement relies heavily on self-report, which is a problem when the thing being measured is self-awareness. "Be yourself" is also not usable advice if the authentic self is arrogant or indecisive, and the theory does not adequately explain what a leader should do when their genuine values conflict with the organisation's legitimate requirements.

5.4 · Ethical and responsible leadership

Ethical leadership

Brown and Treviño's framing is the most useful, because it explains why good personal character is insufficient. An ethical leader must be both:

Necessary, not sufficient
The moral person
  • Honest, trustworthy, fair.
  • Acts with integrity in personal conduct.
  • This is about who the leader is.
  • A leader who is only this is seen as a decent person with no influence on the organisation's ethics.
The part that is usually missing
The moral manager
  • Talks about ethics explicitly and often.
  • Role models ethical conduct visibly.
  • Uses rewards and discipline to hold people accountable for how results were achieved.
  • This is about what the leader does to others' behaviour.

The link to Unit 3 is direct: because most employees operate at Kohlberg's conventional level, what counts as ethical is largely determined by what the organisation signals, and the leader is the loudest signal through social learning. A personally honest leader who never disciplines a high-performing but unethical subordinate has taught the organisation that results outrank conduct.

Servant leadership

Robert Greenleaf. The leader's motivation is to serve followers rather than to be served. Characteristics: listening, empathy, persuasion rather than authority, stewardship, building community, and developing followers' capability. Outcomes include higher OCB, team performance and commitment. It is particularly congruent with Indian and other collectivist settings, and with the Tata ethos of trusteeship.

Responsible leadership

Definition

Responsible leadership is the exercise of leadership with accountability to the full range of stakeholders, including employees, customers, suppliers, communities, future generations and the natural environment, rather than to shareholders alone.

It differs from ethical leadership in scope. Ethical leadership concerns the leader's conduct towards those they deal with. Responsible leadership concerns the leader's accountability for the organisation's effects on parties who are not in the room and cannot hold them to account. That widening is exactly what the next section requires.

The opposite, worth naming

Abusive supervision is sustained hostile verbal and non-verbal behaviour by a supervisor, excluding physical contact: ridicule, public criticism, the silent treatment, taking credit, blaming. It is one of the most damaging variables in all of OB, predicting turnover, deviance, poor health and family spillover, and it frequently coexists with high measured performance, which is why it survives. A question on ethical leadership is improved by naming what its absence looks like.

5.5 · Leadership for sustainability

Definition

Leadership for sustainability is the practice of directing and influencing an organisation so that environmental and social outcomes are treated as core objectives alongside financial performance, and are embedded in strategy, structure and daily behaviour rather than added on.

The distinctive competencies

01
Systems thinking

Seeing the organisation as part of an environmental and social system, and anticipating second-order effects. The systems approach from Unit 1 applied to leadership.

02
Long-term orientation

Willingness to accept a near-term cost for a long-term outcome, against quarterly market pressure. This is the hardest competency because the incentive structure opposes it.

03
Stakeholder inclusion

Actively bringing in parties with no formal power: communities, workers in the supply chain, future generations.

04
Comfort with ambiguity

Sustainability problems have no settled right answer and incomplete data, so decisions must be made and revised.

05
Visible role modelling

The strongest single lever, because of social learning. A leader who flies for a one-hour meeting has settled the travel policy regardless of what the policy says.

06
Ambidexterity

Running the existing business while simultaneously building the thing that replaces it. Covered in 5.16.

What a sustainability leader actually has to do

  1. Embed rather than bolt on. Put environmental and social targets into the core strategy, the capital allocation process and every relevant KRA, rather than into a CSR department. A separate sustainability function with no authority is the structural version of a green team without a budget.
  2. Change the measurement system, because until the appraisal and the incentive change, nothing else will. This is the reward-system point that recurs throughout this course.
  3. Resolve the conflict openly. Sustainability targets genuinely conflict with cost and growth targets in the short run, and a leader who pretends otherwise loses credibility. Naming the trade-off and deciding it publicly is what makes the target believable.
  4. Protect the people who raise problems, which is the psychological safety argument from Unit 1 and the whistle-blowing argument from Unit 2.
  5. Build capability, through training that explains mechanisms rather than rules, so behaviour transfers.
  6. Report honestly, including failures, since selective disclosure is greenwashing and employees detect it first.
The barriers to state

Short-termism in capital markets and in executive incentives; measurement difficulty, since environmental and social outcomes are harder to quantify than profit and therefore lose to it; agency and time-horizon mismatch, because the leader's tenure is shorter than the payback period; low moral intensity from Unit 3, since the harm is distant and diffuse; and greenwashing temptation, because announcing is cheap and changing is not.

5.6Power: Meaning and Sources

Definition

Power refers to a capacity that A has to influence the behaviour of B so that B acts in accordance with A's wishes.

Robbins and Judge

Three features of the definition to bring out:

  • It is a capacity, not an act. Power can exist and never be used, and it still influences behaviour, because B anticipates it.
  • The key is DEPENDENCE. B's dependence on A is the basis of A's power. The greater B's dependence, the greater A's power.
  • It does not require compatible goals, unlike leadership, which requires goal congruence. Power only requires dependence.
What creates dependence

A resource creates dependence when it is important, scarce, and non-substitutable. All three must hold. This one line explains almost every power difference in an organisation, and it is worth quoting.

Example: the only employee who understands the legacy payroll system has real power, even at a junior grade, because the skill is important, scarce and has no substitute. Document the system and train two others, and the power evaporates without anyone being demoted.

French and Raven's five bases of power

The finding that must be in your answer

Personal power is the most effective. Expert and referent power are positively related to employee satisfaction, commitment and performance. Coercive power is negatively related to all three, and reward and legitimate power appear largely unrelated to these outcomes.

The practical implication is uncomfortable for hierarchical organisations: the two bases managers are handed on appointment are the least useful, and the two that actually work have to be earned. Coercion may produce short-term compliance and reliably produces resentment, concealment and withdrawal, which is the punishment argument from Unit 3 arriving at the organisational level.

Upward influence relies on rational persuasion; downward influence can additionally use inspirational appeals and pressure; lateral influence depends on rational persuasion, consultation and exchange, since no authority exists.

Power and harassment

Sexual harassment is fundamentally an abuse of power, not a matter of attraction. It is most likely where there is a substantial power differential and where the harasser can affect the target's career. In India, the Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal) Act, 2013 requires every workplace with ten or more employees to constitute an Internal Committee, and the committee's independence is the structural answer to the power problem: a complaint cannot be routed through the person who holds power over the complainant. That is the same design principle as the whistle-blower mechanism in Unit 2.

5.7Power and Politics in Organisations

Definition

Political behaviour consists of activities that are not required as part of one's formal role in the organisation, but that influence, or attempt to influence, the distribution of advantages and disadvantages within the organisation.

  • Legitimate political behaviour: normal everyday politics. Complaining to a supervisor, bypassing the chain of command, forming coalitions, obstructing policy through inaction, developing contacts outside the organisation.
  • Illegitimate political behaviour: extreme behaviour that violates the implied rules of the game. Sabotage, whistle-blowing done for revenge, symbolic protest, deliberate leaks.
Why politics is inevitable, not a defect

Politics arises because organisations are made up of individuals and groups with differing values, goals and interests, and because resources are limited. Where resources are scarce, allocation cannot be settled by fact alone, and the criteria themselves are contestable. Add to that the ambiguity of most performance measurement, and politics is not a failure of professionalism. It is what fills the space that facts cannot settle.

That is why "we should have no politics here" is not a realistic goal. The realistic goal is to narrow the space in which politics operates, by making criteria explicit, decisions documented and performance measurable.

Impression management

Impression management is the process by which individuals attempt to control the impression others form of them. Techniques include conformity, favours, excuses, apologies, self-promotion, enhancement (claiming an outcome was more valuable than others realise), flattery, and exemplification (visible dedication, such as being seen to work late).

The evidence is nuanced and worth stating: impression management improves interview outcomes and performance appraisal ratings, but only when it is not perceived as manipulation. Self-promotion works badly in interviews and better in appraisals; ingratiation works in both if undetected. High self-monitors (Unit 2) are better at it.

How employees respond to organisational politics

Where politics is perceived as high, the consequences are consistent: decreased job satisfaction, increased anxiety and stress, increased turnover, and reduced performance. Employees also respond with defensive behaviours: overconforming, passing the buck, playing safe, justifying, scapegoating, stalling, and avoiding blame by avoiding action. Defensive behaviour is individually rational and organisationally corrosive, because it converts capable people into people who will not decide anything.

Two qualifications: employees who understand the politics of their organisation suffer less, since ambiguity is a large part of the stress; and those who see political activity as an opportunity rather than a threat report higher satisfaction. So political skill, meaning the ability to read and work within the system, is a real and legitimate competence, not merely a vice.

5.8Conflict: Process, Sources and Outcomes

Definition

Conflict is a process that begins when one party perceives that another party has negatively affected, or is about to negatively affect, something that the first party cares about.

Note the word perceives. Conflict begins with a perception, which puts it squarely in the territory of Unit 3, and it means a conflict can exist with no objective incompatibility at all.

The three views of conflict

ViewPositionImplication for the manager
Traditional viewAll conflict is bad, harmful and must be avoided. It indicates a malfunction in the groupEliminate conflict. Attend to its causes and correct them
Human relations viewConflict is a natural and inevitable outcome in any group, and need not be harmfulAccept it; it may even be a positive force
Interactionist viewSome conflict is absolutely necessary for a group to perform effectively. A harmonious, peaceful group is prone to becoming apathetic and unresponsive to changeManage the level and type of conflict. Sometimes stimulate it

The interactionist view is the current position and the one to argue for. It leads directly to the functional and dysfunctional distinction.

Functional and dysfunctional conflict, and the three types

TypeWhat it is aboutEffect
Task conflictThe content and goals of the workFunctional at low to moderate levels. Improves decision quality, surfaces assumptions, prevents groupthink. Becomes dysfunctional at high levels
Relationship conflictInterpersonal relationships, personality clashes, animosityAlmost always dysfunctional. The friction and hostility raise personality clashes and reduce mutual understanding, which obstructs the task. There is no beneficial level
Process conflictHow the work gets done: who does what, delegation, rolesFunctional only at very low levels. A little clarification is useful; sustained argument about who does what is highly destructive, because it often masks relationship conflict
The most examinable sentence in the whole topic

Encourage task conflict, suppress relationship conflict, and minimise process conflict. The manager's skill is not reducing conflict but converting relationship conflict back into task conflict, which is done by keeping the discussion on the problem, on evidence and on shared goals, and never on the person. This is also what makes psychological safety and functional conflict compatible rather than opposed.

Also name the loci of conflict: dyadic (between two people), intragroup (within a group), and intergroup (between groups). Intergroup conflict is the most damaging organisationally, because group loyalty from Unit 4 hardens each side.

The conflict process

5.9Conflict Management

The five conflict-handling intentions, from the Thomas-Kilmann framework, plotted on two dimensions: assertiveness, the degree to which one tries to satisfy one's own concerns, and cooperativeness, the degree to which one tries to satisfy the other's.

How to score full marks here

Do not write that collaborating is always best. Every style is correct in some situation, and the mature answer states the contingency: a manager who collaborates on a trivial issue wastes the organisation's time, and one who avoids a serious structural conflict lets it fester into relationship conflict. The skill is style-situation fit. Most people, however, have one default style they overuse, and the practical development goal is to expand the repertoire.

Conflict resolution techniques

Beyond the five intentions, name these structural techniques: problem solving, superordinate goals (a shared goal that cannot be reached without cooperation, which is the most reliable cure for intergroup conflict), expansion of resources, avoidance, smoothing, compromise, authoritative command, altering the human variable through behavioural training, and altering the structural variables by redesigning jobs, transfers, or creating coordinating roles.

Conflict stimulation techniques

The interactionist view requires these, and they are the part students forget: use communication to raise ambiguity deliberately, bring in outsiders with different backgrounds and values, restructure the organisation to disturb the status quo, and appoint a devil's advocate. Including this section shows you have understood that conflict management means managing the level in both directions.

Example · diagnosing before choosing a style

Sales and credit control are in open conflict. Sales says credit control is blocking revenue; credit control says sales is bringing in customers who will not pay.

A manager who treats this as a personality problem will run a team-building session and change nothing, because the source is structural: sales is paid on booked revenue and credit control is measured on bad debt, so the two are being rewarded for opposing behaviour. Both teams are behaving correctly given their incentives.

The interventions that work are structural: a superordinate goal such as collected revenue, which requires both; a shared metric in both scorecards; and a joint pre-approval process for large accounts. Collaborating as a style is only possible once the structure permits it.

5.10The Negotiation Process

Definition

Negotiation is a process in which two or more parties exchange goods or services and attempt to agree on the exchange rate for them.

The two bargaining strategies

CharacteristicDistributive bargainingIntegrative bargaining
GoalGet as large a share of the fixed pie as possibleExpand the pie so both parties gain
MotivationWin-loseWin-win
FocusPositions ("I cannot go beyond this figure")Interests ("why do you need it by Friday?")
InterestsOpposedCongruent or at least different rather than opposed
Information sharingLow. Sharing information only helps the other sideHigh. Sharing is necessary to find joint gains
Duration of relationshipShort termLong term
ExampleHaggling over the price of a used car with a strangerAgreeing annual terms with a supplier you will use for ten years

The five steps of the negotiation process

  1. Preparation and planning. Establish what you want and why, what the other party wants and why, your target point and resistance point, and above all your BATNA. Anticipate their BATNA. This stage decides most negotiations, and it is the one most people skip.
  2. Definition of ground rules. Who will negotiate, where, over what time frame, what issues are on the table, and what procedure will apply if there is deadlock.
  3. Clarification and justification. Both sides explain, amplify, clarify and justify their original demands. This is informative rather than confrontational, and it is the stage where integrative possibilities are discovered, because the parties learn each other's interests rather than just their positions.
  4. Bargaining and problem solving. The actual give and take, where concessions are made and an agreement is worked out.
  5. Closure and implementation. Formalising the agreement and developing procedures to implement and monitor it. A negotiation that produces agreement with no implementation mechanism has not concluded, and this stage is where long-term relationships are either protected or damaged.

5.11Negotiation Strategies

Claiming value
Distributive tactics
  • Anchor first, and anchor aggressively. From Unit 3: the first number named exerts a powerful pull on the final outcome, and the party who opens usually does better.
  • Make the first offer and have reasons for it, so it is not dismissed.
  • Framing: present the same terms as a gain to them rather than a cost.
  • Reveal a deadline, which research suggests helps rather than harms the party who discloses it.
  • Keep your resistance point concealed and probe for theirs.
  • Make concessions small, slow and reciprocal.
Creating value
Integrative tactics
  • Separate the people from the problem. Attack the issue, not the party, which is the task-versus-relationship conflict rule.
  • Focus on interests, not positions. Ask why, repeatedly.
  • Invent options for mutual gain before evaluating any of them, so evaluation does not kill creativity, which is the brainstorming rule from Unit 4.
  • Insist on objective criteria, an external standard both can accept, rather than on will.
  • Exploit differences in priority: trade something you value little for something they value greatly. This is where joint gain actually comes from.
  • Build trust through information sharing and by making the first small disclosure.
Example · why integrative is not just being nice

A supplier demands a 9 per cent price increase; the buyer has budgeted 3 per cent. Purely distributive, they will land near 6 per cent and both will be dissatisfied.

On questioning, the supplier's real problem is cash flow, caused by the buyer's 90-day payment terms, not margin. The buyer's real constraint is the unit cost line in this year's budget, not total cash. So: 4 per cent increase with payment in 15 days, plus a two-year commitment. The supplier's cash position improves more than 9 per cent would have delivered, and the buyer stays inside the budget line.

Nobody conceded. The gain came from a difference in priority, and it was only discoverable because someone asked why.

Individual differences in negotiation

  • Personality: agreeableness and extraversion are weak negatives for distributive bargaining, since agreeable people concede and extraverts disclose too much. Agreeableness helps in integrative bargaining. Self-efficacy is a consistent positive predictor.
  • Mood and emotion: displayed anger can extract concessions from a weaker opponent, and damages the relationship and future dealings. Faked anger tends to be detected and backfires. Displayed disappointment is often more effective. Negotiators in a positive mood reach more integrative agreements.
  • Gender: the evidence points to situation rather than ability. Women negotiate less often for themselves, partly because assertive negotiation by women is more likely to be penalised socially, which is a real and documented backlash effect rather than a difference in skill. Women negotiate as well or better when acting on behalf of someone else.
  • Culture: section 5.12.

5.12Global Implications of Negotiation

Negotiation style is strongly culturally patterned, and the differences are systematic enough to be listed. Use the Hofstede dimensions from Unit 2 Part B as the explanatory frame rather than presenting the differences as national quirks.

Dimension of differenceHow it variesHofstede link
Relationship before businessSome cultures require a personal relationship to be established before substantive talks begin, and treat rushing to the agenda as untrustworthy. Others treat relationship-building as a delayCollectivism
Directness and disagreementDirect refusal is normal in some cultures and offensive in others, where "we will consider it" or silence is the refusal. Misreading this produces false confidence in an agreement that does not existPower distance and collectivism
Time orientationDeadline-driven negotiators concede as the deadline nears, and negotiators from long-term-oriented cultures use that patience deliberatelyLong-term orientation
Who decidesA single empowered negotiator, versus a delegation that must refer back to a group or a hierarchy. Assuming the person opposite can commit is a common and expensive errorPower distance and collectivism

Emotional displayRanges from cultures where visible emotion signals sincerity to those where composure signals seriousnessRestraint and assertiveness
Persuasion styleReliance on data and logic, versus on precedent and relationship, versus on principle and ideologyVarious
Example · the classic comparison

American negotiators tend to be impatient, individually empowered, and oriented to signing quickly. Japanese negotiators typically use larger delegations, require internal consensus before committing, spend far longer on the relationship, and treat a signed contract as the start of a relationship rather than the end of a negotiation. Neither side is being obstructive.

Indian negotiators, on Hofstede's profile of high power distance, low uncertainty avoidance and a collectivist tilt, typically expect the senior person to decide, treat terms as renegotiable if circumstances change, and prefer relationship-based flexibility over strict procedure. Working with a German counterparty who scores high on uncertainty avoidance, this produces predictable friction: one side experiences the other as rigid, and the other experiences the first as unreliable.

The caution to include

All of this is a set of hypotheses about likely friction, not predictions about the individual in the room. Using a national average to predict a specific negotiator is the ecological fallacy, and applied to a person it becomes stereotyping, which is the Unit 3 shortcut. The practical advice is to use cultural knowledge to ask better questions and to avoid misreading a behaviour as bad faith, and then to observe the actual person.

5.13Organisational Culture: Definition and Functions

Definition

Organisational culture is a system of shared meaning held by members that distinguishes the organisation from other organisations.

Robbins and Judge

The seven characteristics that capture a culture

CharacteristicThe question it asks
1. Innovation and risk takingHow far are employees encouraged to be innovative and take risks?
2. Attention to detailHow far are employees expected to exhibit precision, analysis and attention to detail?
3. Outcome orientationDoes management focus on results rather than on the techniques and processes used to achieve them?
4. People orientationDo management decisions take into account the effect on people in the organisation?
5. Team orientationAre work activities organised around teams rather than individuals?
6. AggressivenessAre people aggressive and competitive rather than easygoing?
7. StabilityDo activities emphasise maintaining the status quo rather than growth?

Schein's three levels, which explain why culture is hard to change

Dominant culture, subcultures, and strong versus weak

  • Dominant culture: expresses the core values shared by a majority of members. This is what "organisational culture" usually means.
  • Subcultures: mini-cultures within the organisation, typically defined by department or geography. They include the core values of the dominant culture plus additional values unique to the unit. A firm can have a strong dominant culture and a very different subculture in its sales floor or its plant.
  • Strong culture: core values are intensely held and widely shared. It produces high behavioural consistency, which reduces the need for formal rules and reduces turnover. Its cost is rigidity.
  • Weak culture: values are not widely shared, so behaviour depends on formal rules and individual discretion.

Culture and formalisation are substitutes: a strong culture achieves predictability without a rule book, so the more formal rules an organisation needs, the weaker its culture is in practice.

Functions of culture

01
Boundary-defining

It creates distinctions between one organisation and others, which is the basis of identity and employer brand.

02
Conveys identity

It gives members a sense of who they are as part of something.

03
Generates commitment

It facilitates commitment to something larger than individual self-interest, which is the affective commitment from Unit 2.

04
Enhances social system stability

It is the social glue that holds the organisation together by providing standards for what members should say and do.

05
Sense-making and control

It guides and shapes attitudes and behaviour, and helps members make sense of ambiguous situations where no rule applies.

06
Reduces uncertainty for new joiners

It tells them how things are done here, which is the socialisation process in 5.14.

Culture as a liability · the part that earns marks
  1. Institutionalisation. When an organisation becomes valued for itself rather than for what it produces, behaviour patterns become taken for granted and are no longer questioned, and the organisation can persist long after its purpose is served.
  2. Barrier to change. A strong culture is a liability when the environment is dynamic, because the shared values that produced consistency now enforce the wrong behaviour.
  3. Barrier to diversity. Strong cultures pressure new members to conform, which erodes the very differences that diverse hiring was meant to bring. This is the link to Unit 1: a strong culture and genuine inclusion are in tension, and organisations rarely admit it.
  4. Barrier to acquisitions and mergers. Cultural incompatibility is now regarded as a leading cause of merger failure, ahead of financial and strategic factors.
  5. Toxicity and abuse. A strong culture can institutionalise bullying, overwork or dishonesty, and the strength itself makes it harder for members to recognise as abnormal.

5.14Creating and Sustaining Culture

How a culture begins

Culture derives ultimately from the organisation's founders, who have a vision, are unconstrained by previous custom, and typically impose it on a small organisation in three ways: they hire and retain employees who think and feel as they do, they indoctrinate and socialise those employees, and their own behaviour acts as a role model. Note that all three are the social learning mechanisms from Unit 3.

Socialisation: the three-stage process

Organisations choose among socialisation options, and the choice has consequences worth naming:

  • Formal or informal: a segregated induction programme, or learning on the job.
  • Individual or collective: one at a time, or in a batch. Collective socialisation builds a strong peer cohort, which is why batch hiring produces lasting loyalty.
  • Fixed or variable: a known timetable for the transition, or an open-ended one.
  • Serial or random: an experienced role model or mentor guides the newcomer, or nobody does.
  • Investiture or divestiture: investiture affirms and builds on the newcomer's existing qualities; divestiture strips them away to remake the person. Professional firms and armed forces use divestiture deliberately; it produces strong culture and suppresses exactly the diverse thinking the organisation may have hired for.

How employees learn culture

01
Stories

Narratives about the organisation's founders, rule breaking, past crises and how someone was treated. They anchor the present in the past and legitimise current practice. What a firm tells new joiners in its first week is a deliberate statement of values.

02
Rituals

Repetitive sequences of activities that express and reinforce key values: the annual awards, the Monday morning meeting, the way a deal closing is marked, the induction ceremony.

03
Symbols

Material artefacts conveying who is important and what behaviour is appropriate: office size, corner rooms, reserved parking, dress, the executive dining arrangement, the quality of the chairs on the shop floor.

04
Language

Jargon, acronyms and terminology unique to the organisation. Learning it signals acceptance of the culture, and it also marks the boundary that excludes outsiders.

5.15Organisational Culture and Climate

Definition

Organisational climate refers to the shared perceptions that organisational members have about their organisation and work environment.

BasisOrganisational cultureOrganisational climate
What it isShared values, beliefs and assumptionsShared perceptions of practices, policies and procedures
DepthDeep. Includes unconscious assumptionsSurface. Conscious and reportable
Question it answers"Why do we do things this way?""What is it like to work here right now?"

MeasurementQualitative: observation, interviews, stories, artefactsQuantitative: survey instruments with scales
Disciplinary rootAnthropology and sociologyPsychology
RelationshipClimate is the observable manifestation of culture. Culture is the cause; climate is the symptom you can measure. A single organisation has one culture and many climates, varying by team and by manager
Why the distinction is practically important

Climate is what surveys measure and what a manager can change fairly quickly, within their own team. Culture is what the organisation is, and takes years. This explains a common and demoralising pattern: an engagement survey shows two teams in the same company with completely different scores. The culture is identical. The climates differ because the two managers differ, and that is genuinely actionable rather than a matter for a corporate values exercise.

Specific climates worth naming

  • Safety climate: shared perceptions about the priority given to safety. Strongly predicts accident rates, more strongly than safety rules do.
  • Service climate: predicts customer satisfaction and loyalty.
  • Ethical climate: shared perception of what is right and how ethical issues are handled. Predicts misconduct rates and willingness to report.
  • Diversity climate: shared perception of fairness towards under-represented groups, which is the inclusion measure from Unit 1 and is distinct from the headcount.
  • Green or environmental climate: shared perception of the organisation's real commitment to environmental performance. This is the strongest predictor of employee green behaviour, stronger than the policy or the training.
  • Innovation climate: shared perception that experimentation and dissent are safe, which is psychological safety at unit level.

5.16Creating a Culture for Sustainability and Innovation

The syllabus joins these two, which is apt, because both require the same underlying conditions: tolerance of failure, a long time horizon, and permission to challenge how things are done.

An innovative culture: the characteristics

ConditionWhat it requiresWhy it is rare
Tolerance of failureFailed experiments must not damage careers. Distinguish an intelligent failure, a well-designed test that did not work, from incompetenceAppraisal systems reward outcomes, so failure is punished by default even where speeches say otherwise
Psychological safetyPeople must be able to propose something unconventional and be wrong in publicRequires a leader who reacts well to the first bad idea, which everyone is watching for

The ambidextrous organisation

The central structural problem is that the organisation must do two incompatible things at once:

  • Exploitation: running the existing business efficiently. Rewards consistency, control, incremental improvement and predictability.
  • Exploration: creating what will replace it. Rewards variance, autonomy, experimentation and tolerance of failure.

An organisation optimised for one is structurally hostile to the other, which is why efficient companies are usually poor innovators and it is not a failure of imagination. The ambidextrous organisation resolves this by holding both, typically by separating the exploratory unit structurally, giving it different metrics and a different climate, while keeping it linked to the core through senior leadership. Sustainability initiatives face exactly this problem, which is why they are usually starved in an exploitation-optimised firm.

A culture for sustainability

Beyond the innovation conditions, sustainability adds three requirements:

  1. A long time horizon built into decision rules, not just into speeches. Capital allocation criteria, payback thresholds and appraisal periods all have to permit it, or the culture will reject sustainability proposals automatically and correctly, given its own rules.
  2. Stakeholder consciousness as a basic assumption, meaning it becomes unthinkable to evaluate a decision without asking who else it affects. This is Schein's Level 3, and it is the difference between a firm that has a sustainability policy and one that has a sustainability culture.
  3. Honesty about trade-offs. A culture that claims sustainability and profit never conflict is training its employees not to believe official statements, which contaminates everything else the organisation says.

5.17Green Organisational Culture

Definition

A green organisational culture is one in which environmental values and assumptions are shared by members, embedded in the organisation's artefacts, espoused values and basic underlying assumptions, and expressed consistently in everyday behaviour.

Characteristics of a genuine green culture

  • Environmental criteria in ordinary decisions, not in a separate process. Procurement, capital allocation and product design all carry them.
  • Green targets in line management KRAs, with real weight.
  • Leaders visibly modelling the behaviour, which is the strongest transmitter.
  • Green stories, rituals, symbols and language, the four learning mechanisms from 5.14 deliberately used.
  • Protected voice for environmental concerns, from Unit 1.
  • Honest measurement and reporting, including of failures and of targets missed.
  • Green teams with authority, from Unit 4.
  • Supply chain inclusion, since a firm with a clean site and an unexamined supply chain has relocated the problem rather than solved it.
Greenwashing as a culture failure

Greenwashing is conveying a misleading impression of environmental responsibility, typically by investing in Levels 1 and 2 while leaving Level 3 untouched. Treat it as a culture problem rather than a communications problem, because that is the examinable insight.

Its internal cost is the one organisations underestimate. Employees see both the claim and the reality, so the gap is experienced as a psychological contract breach from Unit 2, producing cynicism, disengagement and exit among exactly the employees who cared most. Externally there is now regulatory exposure, since India's BRSR disclosures are assured and misstatement carries consequences. A green culture is therefore not the marketing version of a green policy; it is what makes the policy survive contact with a bad quarter.

5.18Spirituality and Organisational Culture

Definition

Workplace spirituality is the recognition that people have an inner life that nourishes and is nourished by meaningful work taking place in the context of community.

The clarification to make first, every time

Workplace spirituality is not about organised religion, religious practice or theology. It is about employees finding meaning, purpose and community in their work. Making this distinction at the start of the answer is essential, because the topic is otherwise easily misread, and it also pre-empts the main objection.

Why interest has grown

  • Spirituality can counterbalance the pressures and stress of modern working life, including the technostress of Unit 2.
  • Formalised religion has not worked for many people as a source of meaning, so they seek it elsewhere, and work occupies most of their waking hours.
  • Job demands have made the workplace dominant in people's lives, so the need for involvement and connection is met there or not at all.
  • A desire to integrate personal life values with professional life, rather than being a different person at work.
  • Growing numbers of people find that the pursuit of material acquisition leaves them unfulfilled.

Characteristics of a spiritual organisation

01
Benevolence

Kindness towards others and a wish to promote the happiness of employees and other stakeholders.

02
Strong sense of purpose

Members build the culture around a meaningful purpose. Profit matters, but it is not the primary value.

03
Trust and respect

Mutual trust, honesty and openness, and employees are treated with esteem and value.

04
Open-mindedness

Flexibility of thinking and creativity are valued, including openness to unconventional ideas.

05
Employee expression

Employees are not stifled. They can be who they are and express their moods and feelings without guilt or fear of reprimand.

Outcomes

The research reports positive associations with creativity, employee satisfaction, job involvement, team performance, commitment, ethical behaviour and reduced turnover. Note the links back: a strong sense of purpose is the meaning component of psychological empowerment from Unit 2, and employee expression without fear of reprimand is psychological safety from Unit 1 stated in different vocabulary.

Criticisms, needed for an evaluate question
  1. The question of scientific foundation. What exactly is workplace spirituality, and can it be measured distinctly from job satisfaction, meaningfulness and organisational commitment? The construct overlaps heavily with things already named.
  2. The question of legitimacy. Do organisations have any business imposing spiritual values on employees? There is a real risk of intrusion into the private sphere, and of pressure on employees who do not share the espoused values.
  3. The question of economics. Are spirituality and profit compatible? The evidence is suggestive rather than settled.
  4. The risk of instrumentalisation. The most serious practical objection: purpose and community can be used to extract discretionary effort in place of pay, and an employee told that the work is its own reward while being underpaid is being exploited in the language of meaning. This is a strong point to make, and it connects to emotional labour in Unit 2.
  5. Boundary with religion. In a country as religiously plural as India, an organisation's attempt to build spiritual practice can slide into privileging one tradition, which becomes a diversity and rights problem.

5.19Organisational Change: Meaning and Forces for Change

Definition

Change is making things different. Planned change is change that is intentional and goal oriented, aimed at improving the organisation's ability to adapt to its environment and at changing employee behaviour.

Two distinctions to name:

  • Planned versus unplanned (reactive) change.
  • First-order change: incremental, linear and continuous, within the existing framework. Second-order change: radical, multidimensional and discontinuous, which questions the framework itself. The two require entirely different management, and treating a second-order change as first-order is a common cause of failure.

5.20Resistance to Change and Overcoming It

Reframe resistance before you list its sources

Resistance is usually presented as an obstacle, and there is a better framing that earns marks. Resistance has a benefit: it provides stability and predictability, and it forces a discussion that improves the change. Change that meets no resistance at all is probably change nobody has understood or nobody cares about. Resistance is also information: it frequently means the people closest to the work can see a problem the designers cannot. The managerial task is therefore to surface and use resistance, not merely to overcome it.

The real difficulty is that resistance is often implicit and deferred rather than open and immediate, which makes it far harder to manage. Open opposition can be argued with. Loss of loyalty, loss of motivation, increased absenteeism, reduced effort and accumulated errors cannot, because nobody has said anything.

Five sources
Individual resistance
  • Habit. We respond to situations in our accustomed way, and change requires conscious effort.
  • Security. Change threatens the feeling of safety in the familiar.
  • Economic factors. Fear that the change will reduce income, particularly where pay is tied to output and a new method resets the learning curve.
  • Fear of the unknown. Ambiguity and uncertainty are themselves aversive, which is the stress point from Unit 2.
  • Selective information processing. People perceive selectively to keep their perceptions intact, so they hear the parts that confirm their existing view. This is Unit 3.
Six sources
Organisational resistance
  • Structural inertia. Selection, training, formalisation and reward systems all exist to produce stability, so they actively counteract change.
  • Limited focus of change. Changing one subsystem while leaving the interdependent ones untouched means the change is nullified by the larger system, which is the systems approach from Unit 1.
  • Group inertia. Group norms constrain individuals who might otherwise be willing, which is Unit 4.
  • Threat to expertise. Change may make specialised expertise irrelevant, and the experts know it first.
  • Threat to established power relationships. Redistribution of decision-making authority threatens those who hold it. This is the most powerful and least openly stated source.
  • Threat to established resource allocations. Those who currently control resources see change as loss.

The eight tactics for overcoming resistance

TacticHow it worksCaution
1. Education and communicationReduces resistance by explaining the logic of the change. Resistance often rests on incomplete or inaccurate informationWorks only where trust and credibility already exist. In a low-trust setting, more communication is read as more spin
2. Participation and involvementPeople find it hard to resist a decision they helped make. Draws on the affected parties' expertise and increases commitmentTime consuming, and there is potential for a poor solution. Do not offer participation you do not mean, since fake consultation is worse than none
3. Building support and commitmentCounselling and therapy, new skills training, short paid leave. Addresses anxiety and low self-efficacy directlyCostly, and does not address legitimate objections
4. Developing positive relationshipsEmployees accept change more readily if they trust the managers implementing it. Where trust exists, they will accept change they do not fully understandCannot be built at the point of need. This is capital accumulated in advance
5. Implementing changes fairlyProcedural justice matters as much as the outcome. Being consistent and transparent about process, and explaining the reasoning, reduces resistance even where the outcome is unwelcomeRequires accepting genuine constraints on managerial discretion

The politics of change

Add this and the answer moves up a band. Change threatens the existing power distribution, so it is inherently political. Two consequences follow. First, those who hold power resist change most, and they resist it in the least visible ways, since open opposition would reveal the self-interest. Second, long-term career managers and those who have grown up inside the organisation are typically the greatest resisters, because their expertise and their status are built on the current arrangement. This is why radical change is so often initiated by outsiders, newly hired executives, external consultants, or new owners, and why "we brought in someone from outside" is frequently a political statement rather than a talent one.

5.21Approaches to Managing Organisational Change

A · Lewin's three-step model

B · Kotter's eight-step plan

John Kotter built on Lewin, after analysing the common errors managers make in implementing change.

C · Action research

A change process based on the systematic collection of data and then selection of a change action based on what the analysed data indicates. Five steps: diagnosis, analysis, feedback, action and evaluation. Its two advantages are that it is problem focused rather than solution focused, meaning the change follows the diagnosis rather than the manager's favourite remedy, and that because employees are involved throughout, resistance is substantially reduced.

D · Organisational Development interventions

Organisational Development (OD) is a collection of planned change interventions built on humanistic and democratic values, seeking to improve both organisational effectiveness and employee well-being. Its underlying values are respect for people, trust and support, power equalisation, confrontation of problems rather than avoidance, and participation.

InterventionWhat it is
Sensitivity trainingUnstructured group interaction to change behaviour, increasing awareness of one's own behaviour and how others perceive it. Directly the Johari Window from Unit 4
Survey feedbackQuestionnaires identify discrepancies among member perceptions; results are fed back to groups to diagnose problems and develop solutions
Process consultationA consultant helps the client understand and act on process events, such as communication, decision-making and conflict, rather than solving the problem for them
Team buildingHigh interaction among team members to increase trust and openness, clarify goals and roles, and analyse team process
Intergroup developmentChanging the attitudes, stereotypes and perceptions that groups hold of each other. Directly addresses the intergroup conflict of 5.8
Appreciative inquirySeeks to identify and build on what the organisation does well, rather than looking for problems to fix. Four steps: discovery, dreaming, design and destiny
Which approach to recommend

If asked to choose, structure the answer by the nature of the change. Lewin is the conceptual foundation and its force field analysis is the best diagnostic tool. Kotter is the best implementation sequence for a large planned change with senior sponsorship. Action research is best where the problem is not yet well understood and employee buy-in is critical. OD interventions are the toolkit for the people side of any of them. The common failure across all four is the same: organisations invest in movement and neglect unfreezing and refreezing, so behaviour reverts and the next initiative begins with an audience that has learned not to believe it.

5.22Behavioural Change for Sustainability

The final heading, and it closes the course by returning to the theme of Unit 1: sustainability targets are set at the top and delivered by thousands of small individual behaviours, so a sustainability strategy is a behaviour-change programme whether or not anyone calls it one.

Why sustainability behaviour is unusually hard
  • The value-action gap from Unit 1. Stated environmental concern is high and behaviour lags far behind.
  • Low moral intensity from Unit 3: consequences are distant, diffuse, probabilistic, and the victims are not present.
  • Present bias: a certain cost now against an uncertain benefit later.
  • Invisibility of the outcome. The employee never sees the effect of their action, so the learning principle of knowledge of results is absent.
  • Low self-efficacy: "one cup will not change the climate."
  • Competing incentives: the existing reward system usually pays for the opposite behaviour.

Notice that only the first of these is about attitude. Five of the six are about design, which is why exhortation fails and structural intervention works.

Nudges and choice architecture

Choice architecture is the deliberate design of the environment in which choices are made. A nudge alters behaviour predictably without forbidding any option or significantly changing economic incentives. The EAST framework is a clean and memorable structure for this answer.

The other mechanisms

  • Feedback loops. Real-time or frequent consumption data, per team, is among the most reliably effective environmental interventions available, because it supplies the missing learning principle from Unit 3.
  • Reinforcement and shaping. Reinforce approximations, raise the standard progressively, and use variable recognition rather than a single annual award.
  • Commitment devices. Public, specific, voluntary commitments produce far more behaviour change than private intentions, because of consistency pressure.
  • Habit formation. Most green behaviour is repeated and low-attention, so target the cue rather than the belief.
  • Remove the structural conflict. Fix the incentive that pays for the opposite behaviour. Nothing else in this list survives an incentive pointing the other way.
Three traps to name
  • The rebound effect. Efficiency gains are partly consumed by increased use. A more fuel-efficient vehicle gets driven further, so the saving is less than calculated.
  • Moral licensing. Having done one green act, people feel entitled to relax elsewhere. Someone who carries a steel bottle takes a longer flight with a clearer conscience. This is why single-behaviour campaigns can produce disappointing net effects.
  • Nudging as a substitute for structural change. Nudges are cheap and visible, which makes them attractive to organisations that do not want to change incentives, capital allocation or supply chains. Bin placement is worth doing and is not a climate strategy. Making this criticism is what stops the answer sounding promotional.
Putting it together · the closing argument of the course

A sustainability behaviour-change programme works when it runs at all three levels of analysis at once, which is exactly the structure this syllabus has been building since Unit 1.

Individual (Units 2 and 3): build green self-efficacy, supply knowledge that transfers, reinforce approximations, use defaults and feedback. Group (Unit 4): establish team norms, use green teams with real authority, provide peer modelling and team-level data. Organisation (Unit 5): leadership modelling, green targets in KRAs, culture at Schein's Level 3, protected voice, and Lewin's unfreezing and refreezing so the behaviour does not revert.

A programme that operates at only one level fails, and the level organisations choose is almost always the cheapest one: a poster, which is an artefact at Schein's Level 1 addressed to an individual, with no group norm, no incentive change and no feedback. That is the whole course in one diagnosis.

Case study practice · Section C style

Sanjana Rao is brought in from outside as CEO of a 40-year-old family-controlled auto components manufacturer with 2,800 employees. The board's mandate is to cut the firm's emissions by 30 per cent in three years, because its two largest European customers have made it a supply condition.

She launches "Mission Green 30" in month one: a new values statement, a rebranded logo in green, segregated bins at every plant, a Sustainability Head appointed within the CSR department, and an all-hands address in which she describes the target as non-negotiable. She sets up a Green Council chaired by herself.

Eighteen months later emissions are down 4 per cent. The three plant heads, each with over twenty years' service, have been publicly supportive and have delivered nothing; one told a colleague that "the customers will forget this in two years, they always do". Production bonuses remain tied solely to output and rejection rates. The Sustainability Head has no budget and cannot get plant data. A young process engineer who proposed a kiln modification with a six-year payback was told by finance that the approval threshold is three years. Two shift supervisors who reported that emission monitors were being switched off during peak runs were transferred. The quarterly sustainability report describes the programme as "on track". Attrition among engineers under thirty has doubled, with exit interviews citing "nothing here is real".

(a) Diagnose this failure using the concepts of this unit. (b) Evaluate Sanjana's leadership approach. (c) Set out what she should do now.

Answer skeleton for the case

(a) Diagnosis. Culture at the wrong level: every intervention sits at Schein's Level 1 artefacts (logo, bins, signage) and Level 2 espoused values (values statement, the address), while Level 3 basic assumptions are untouched. Apply the five Level 3 tests and the firm fails all of them: the green option loses on cost by default, output beats emissions when they conflict in the same month, the two people who reported a breach were transferred, plant heads carry no green KRA, and the capital approval threshold structurally forbids the very projects the target requires. Structural resistance: the production bonus still pays for output alone, so plant heads behaving as they do is rational, not obstructive. This is Lewin's point exactly, that she has pushed the driving forces hard and removed no restraining force. Politics of change: the plant heads are long-tenured insiders whose expertise and status rest on the existing arrangement, so they are the predicted greatest resisters, and their resistance is implicit and deferred, public support with no delivery, which is far harder to manage than open opposition. Power: the Sustainability Head has been given a title with no reward, coercive or informational power and no budget, so she is dependent on the plant heads rather than the reverse. Structure: housing sustainability inside CSR guarantees it cannot cross functions, and a single-function body cannot solve a cross-functional problem, which is the Unit 4 point. Green Council chaired by the CEO, with a target announced as non-negotiable, sets up exactly the groupthink conditions: a directive leader, anchoring, self-censorship and an illusion of unanimity, which is why nobody told her it was failing. Psychological safety destroyed by the two transfers, which is why the monitor tampering will continue and why no bad news reaches her. Greenwashing: the "on track" report is a culture failure and now a disclosure exposure, and the young engineers' "nothing here is real" is a psychological contract breach from Unit 2, producing exactly the attrition pattern observed, since those who cared most leave first. Kotter: she skipped step 1, urgency was asserted rather than established, and step 6, no short-term wins, and never reached step 8.

(b) Evaluating her leadership. The approach is transformational in style and transactional in substance, and she has delivered neither. She supplied inspirational motivation and idealised influence through vision and the all-hands address, but no contingent reward, which the full range model says transformational leadership must be built on. A vision with an unchanged incentive system is a speech. There is no intellectual stimulation, since declaring the target non-negotiable and chairing the council herself closed off challenge, and no individualised consideration, as shown by the treatment of the engineer and the supervisors. On authentic leadership she fails balanced processing, because contrary information was structurally excluded, and relational transparency, because the "on track" report is untrue. On ethical leadership she may be a moral person and is not functioning as a moral manager: the reward system still pays for the wrong behaviour and reporting a breach was punished, so the organisation has learned that results outrank conduct. On responsible leadership the target is being pursued for customer compliance rather than stakeholder accountability, which is why it reads as external and temporary to the plant heads, and their judgement that "the customers will forget" is a rational reading of her own framing.

(c) What she should do now. Remove restraining forces first, which is Lewin's priority. Change the production bonus so emissions carry real weight alongside output, and put the 30 per cent target into each plant head's KRA. Raise the capital approval threshold for emissions projects, since a three-year payback rule makes the target arithmetically impossible and no amount of leadership fixes that. Approve the kiln modification, which also becomes a step-6 short-term win and a visible signal. Fix power and structure. Move sustainability out of CSR into a cross-functional council with operations, procurement, finance, engineering and quality, with a budget, defined decision rights and a senior sponsor who is not her, and give the Sustainability Head access to plant data as of right. Stop chairing it herself, speak last rather than first, appoint a devil's advocate, and hold a second-chance review, to break the groupthink that kept the failure invisible for eighteen months. Reverse the transfers publicly and act on the monitor tampering, which is both a compliance breach and the single most important cultural act available to her: until the people who reported it are visibly protected, nobody will report the next one, and she will continue to receive reports saying "on track". Correct the sustainability report, since continuing to describe a 4 per cent outcome as on track is a disclosure risk under BRSR assurance and is the reason her own engineers no longer believe her. Reframe the purpose from customer compliance to the firm's own viability and stakeholder responsibility, so the plant heads cannot reasonably wait it out. Build coalition rather than compliance: a change of this order cannot be delivered over the three plant heads, so either bring them in with real influence over the how, or accept that a leadership change is part of the plan, and be explicit with the board about which. Then refreeze: embed the new criteria in selection, induction, capital process and the appraisal cycle, use plant-level emissions feedback so behaviour becomes learnable, and anchor it in the stories and rituals of the firm rather than in a values statement on the wall.
