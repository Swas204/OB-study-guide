Power, Conflict and Negotiation: Dynamics of Organisational Behaviour

Four forces run every organisation: who leads, who holds power, how people clash and settle, and how the whole place changes. This page follows the depth of the class notes, with Aswathappa as the anchor text.

Leadership · sections 5.1 to 5.5

Definition

Leadership is the ability to influence a group toward the achievement of a vision or set of goals.

Robbins and Judge

The key word is influence. A manager can order people because of a job title, but a leader gets people to want to move. This unit looks at five modern answers to one question: what kind of influence works best today?

The five lenses build on each other. Shared leadership asks who leads. Transformational leadership asks how a leader moves people. Authentic, ethical and responsible leadership ask what kind of person the leader is and whom the leader answers to. Leadership for sustainability asks what the leader is aiming at. Learn each one by its one-line signature, because that line is what an examiner looks for first.

5.1 · Shared leadership

Signature: leadership is distributed across the team, not owned by one person.

In the traditional picture, one person at the top makes the calls and everyone else follows. Shared leadership drops that picture. Each member leads where they are strongest. The person with the most relevant knowledge takes the lead on that part of the work, and steps back when the task moves to someone else's area.

Why it matters: modern work is too complex for one person to know everything. When leadership moves to whoever knows most, decisions are better informed and members feel ownership. The formal manager still exists, but the role shifts from deciding everything to coordinating the people who lead each part.

Example

In a product launch team at a Bengaluru start-up, the data analyst leads the pricing decision, the designer leads the packaging call, and the sales lead runs the dealer plan. The team manager coordinates but does not decide everything.

5.2 · Transformational leadership

Signature: inspires people to go beyond self-interest and changes the system.

It is easiest to understand by contrast with transactional leadership, which leads through an exchange. The leader sets clear tasks, rewards people who hit their targets and corrects them when they miss. The deal is simple: you do the work, you get the reward. This keeps the system running well, but people do only what the deal pays for.

Transformational leadership goes further. The leader inspires people to care about a purpose bigger than their own reward, so they give more than the deal asks. It works through the four I's:

IIdealised influence: the leader behaves as a role model, so people admire, trust and copy them.
IInspirational motivation: the leader paints a vision that gives the work meaning and energy.
IIntellectual stimulation: the leader invites people to question old ways and think afresh.
IIndividualised consideration: the leader pays attention to each person's needs and growth.
Exam tip

The two styles are not enemies. Transactional basics keep the place running and transformational leadership lifts it. Close any comparison with: where transactional leadership maintains the system, transformational leadership changes it.

5.3 · Authentic leadership

Signature: leads by being genuine.

Authentic leaders have three qualities, and each one leads to the next:

  • Self-aware: they know their own strengths, weaknesses and values.
  • Values-driven: they act on those values, even when it is inconvenient.
  • Transparent: they are open about what they think and why, so there is no gap between the public face and the private one.

The outcome is trust. When people see that a leader's words and actions match, they believe what the leader says. Trust is what lets people share bad news early and commit to the leader's goals.

Example

A branch head admits in a team meeting that she misjudged a target and explains what she will change. Her team trusts her more, because what she says matches what she does.

5.4 · Ethical and responsible leadership

These two are close, so learn the difference first. Ethical leadership is about the leader's own conduct. Responsible leadership is about whom the leader answers to.

  • Ethical leadership: the leader acts on personal moral principle, with fairness, honesty and integrity, and role-models right conduct so others copy it.
  • Responsible leadership: the leader widens accountability beyond shareholders to employees, society and the environment, and owns the full impact of each decision.

Why good people make bad calls. Three pressures push them:

  • Peer pressure: when everyone around is cutting corners, it feels normal to join in.
  • Obedience to authority: people do wrong because a boss told them to, and assume the responsibility is the boss's.
  • Hubris: success makes leaders overconfident, so they start believing the rules do not apply to them.

How an organisation guards against them. Each guard answers one pressure:

  • A clear code of conduct sets the standard, so peer habits are not the only guide.
  • Safe whistleblower channels let people refuse a wrong order and report it without fear.
  • An independent board or audit committee checks even the top leader, which limits hubris.

5.5 · Leadership for sustainability

Signature: embeds long-term environmental and social goals into the vision.

The key word is embeds. Sustainability is not a side project or a CSR report. It is part of what the organisation is trying to achieve, so it shapes everyday decisions.

Success is judged on the triple bottom line:

  • People: the effect on employees and the community.
  • Planet: the effect on the environment.
  • Profit: the financial result.

A leader who looks only at profit can win this year and harm the business later. Weighing all three forces a longer view. This lens joins the earlier ones: it needs the inspiration of transformational leadership and the wider accountability of responsible leadership.

Example

A managing director makes water reduction a target for every plant head, reports it next to profit in the board pack, and links part of the bonus to it. The goal sits inside the vision, not in a separate CSR file.

5.6Power: Meaning and Sources

Definition

Power is the capacity to influence. Politics is what people do to get and use it.

Notice the word capacity. Power is the ability to influence, whether or not it is used. A manager who could cut your bonus has power over you even on a day she does nothing. Leadership (5.1 to 5.5) is one way of using power. This section asks where that power comes from in the first place.

French and Raven identified five sources of power. The first three come from the position. The last two come from the person.

SourceWhere it comes fromHow it works
LegitimatePosition and titlePeople accept that the role gives the right to direct them. "Do it because I am the manager."
RewardAbility to give valued thingsPeople comply to get pay rises, good projects or praise.
CoerciveAbility to punish or threatenPeople comply out of fear of a penalty. It gets obedience, not commitment.
ExpertKnowledge and skillPeople follow because the person knows what they are talking about.
ReferentBeing liked and admiredPeople follow because they want to be like the person or keep their approval.
Exam tip · why expert power is the most tenuous

Expert power is fragile for three reasons. It is narrow: a tax expert has no special say on marketing. It is lost as others learn: once the team picks up the skill, the expert is no longer needed. It is gone if the knowledge stops mattering: a new system can make old know-how useless. Yet on a problem that turns on that expertise, it can outweigh a legitimate superior, because the boss has to defer to the person who actually knows.

5.7Power and Politics in Organisations

Definition

Organisational politics is the use of power to get outcomes not sanctioned by the organisation.

The key phrase is not sanctioned. Using power for the goals the organisation has set is just management. Politics is using it for outcomes the organisation never approved, often personal ones.

When politics rises. Three conditions feed it:

  • Scarce resources: when there is not enough budget or promotion to go round, people fight for their share.
  • Unclear goals: when nobody knows exactly what counts as success, people argue that their own work matters most.
  • Low trust: when people expect others to play games, they play games first to protect themselves.

Tactics to watch for:

  • Impression management: shaping how others see you, for example taking visible credit for a team's work.
  • Coalition building: lining up allies so a position looks like the majority view.
  • Controlling information: holding back or filtering what others know, because whoever controls the facts controls the decision.

Managing politics. Each step removes one of the causes above:

  • Set clear goals and rules, so success is defined and there is less room to argue.
  • Allocate scarce resources fairly and openly, so people do not need to fight in private.
  • Communicate with transparency, which takes away the advantage of controlling information.
  • Lead by example, because people copy what the leader does, not what the leader says.
  • Confront destructive politicking early, before it becomes accepted as how things work.

5.8Conflict: Process, Sources and Outcomes

Politics often ends in open conflict. Conflict does not appear out of nowhere. It builds through five stages, and a manager who knows the stages can step in early.

  • Stage 1, potential opposition. The conditions for conflict exist, but nobody has clashed yet. There are three sources:
    • Communication: misunderstanding, poor information or messages that get distorted.
    • Structure: how work is set up, such as unclear responsibilities or departments with competing goals. Role conflict is a common structural source: one person faces clashing expectations from two masters, such as customers and management.
    • Personal variables: differences in personality, values and attitudes.
  • Stage 2, cognition and personalisation. People become aware of the conflict and it is felt. They feel tense, frustrated or angry, so it becomes personal. Before this point the disagreement exists only on paper.
  • Stage 3, intentions. Each side decides how it will handle the conflict, which is the choice of handling style covered in 5.9.
  • Stage 4, behaviour. The conflict becomes visible in what people say and do: arguments, demands, refusals. This is the open clash.
  • Stage 5, outcomes. Conflict can end well or badly. Functional conflict lifts the quality of ideas, surfaces problems early and drives change. Dysfunctional conflict drains energy and trust, so the team spends its effort fighting instead of working.
Example · role conflict

A bank relationship manager is told by customers to waive charges and by management to collect them. The clash comes from the structure of the role, not from either person.

5.9Conflict Management

Stage 3 of the conflict process is the choice of a handling style. There are five handling styles, placed on two dimensions:

  • Assertiveness: how hard you try to satisfy your own concerns.
  • Cooperativeness: how hard you try to satisfy the other party's concerns.
  • Competing (high assertive, low cooperative): push to win your point. It fits when a quick, firm decision is needed, such as a safety rule.
  • Collaborating (high on both): dig into the real problem so both sides fully win. It takes the most time, so it fits issues too important to settle by giving in.
  • Compromising (middle on both): each side gives a little and meets in the middle. It fits when a quick, acceptable settlement matters more than a perfect one.
  • Avoiding (low on both): step back and let the disagreement cool. It fits trivial issues, or when tempers need time.
  • Accommodating (low assertive, high cooperative): give in to keep the relationship smooth. It fits when the issue matters far more to the other side than to you.
Exam tip

There is no best style, only the right one for the situation. Say this in any answer on conflict handling, then match one style to one situation.

5.10The Negotiation Process

When two sides in a conflict need each other's agreement, they negotiate. Negotiation is how two or more parties settle a conflict by agreeing terms. It runs in five steps, and each step sets up the next:

  1. Preparation: before meeting, each side works out its own goals, what the other side is likely to want, and the facts that support its case. Most weak deals are lost here.
  2. Ground rules: the two sides agree how they will negotiate: who will be at the table, where they will meet, by when they must finish, and how they will handle a deadlock.
  3. Clarification: each side explains and justifies its position. This is not yet bargaining. It helps each side understand what the other really needs.
  4. Bargaining: the give and take. Each side trades what matters less to it for what matters more, until an agreement emerges.
  5. Closure: the agreement is written down, formalised and put into action, with clear steps on who does what.
Example

A union and plant management prepare their wage numbers, agree to meet weekly for a month, explain their positions, trade wages against shift timings, and close with a signed settlement.

5.11Negotiation Strategies

In the bargaining step, a negotiator can take one of two approaches. The difference lies in how each side sees the thing being divided.

Distributive
Win-lose · fixed pie
  • The pie is treated as a fixed size
  • One side's gain is the other's loss
  • Each side tries to claim the biggest slice
Integrative
Win-win · grow the pie
  • The sides look for ways to make the pie bigger
  • Both can gain at the same time
  • Protects the long-term relationship

Integrative bargaining works by looking beyond the stated positions to what each side actually needs. Once those needs are clear, there is often a solution that gives both sides more. Distributive bargaining suits a one-off deal with a stranger. Integrative bargaining suits parties who will keep working together, such as a firm and its long-term supplier.

Example

Two managers fighting over one fixed budget are in a distributive negotiation. If they find that one needs the money this quarter and the other next quarter, they can both get what they need, which is integrative.

5.12Global Implications of Negotiation

The negotiation process and strategies above look the same everywhere on paper. In practice, the culture of each side changes how they play out. Culture shapes three things:

  • Pace: some cultures move quickly to a deal and see delay as wasted time. Others take time, and see rushing as a sign of pressure or bad faith.
  • Style: some cultures say things directly and openly, including a plain "no". Others are indirect and polite, so a refusal may sound like "we will consider it".
  • Deal or relationship first: some cultures want the contract settled first and treat the relationship as a result of the deal. Others want trust built first and see the deal as a result of the relationship.

The practical lesson: a negotiator who reads these signals through their own culture will misjudge the other side. Preparing for a cross-border negotiation means studying the other side's culture, not just their numbers.

Example

An Indian supplier meeting a Japanese buyer should expect several meetings to build the relationship before price is discussed, while an American buyer may want terms on the table in the first meeting.

5.13Organisational Culture: Definition and Functions

Course plan only

The topics from here to the end of the unit are in the course plan but not in the class notes. They are kept at surface level so you know what each one means.

Organisational culture is the system of shared values, beliefs and ways of doing things that makes one organisation different from another.

Culture is the unwritten "how we do things here". It tells people what matters, how to behave and what is rewarded, often more strongly than formal rules. Its functions are about giving members a common identity, holding the organisation together and guiding behaviour without constant supervision.

5.14Creating and Sustaining Culture

Creating and sustaining culture is about where an organisation's culture comes from and how it is kept alive over time.

A culture usually starts with the founders, whose values shape the early organisation. It is then kept going by the people the organisation hires, the example set by top management, and the way new employees learn the ropes. The topic matters because a culture that is not actively maintained drifts.

5.15Organisational Culture and Climate

Organisational climate is how employees feel about their work environment at a given time, while culture is the deeper set of shared values underneath.

The two are related but not the same. Culture is long-lasting and hard to change. Climate is the current mood, such as morale or trust, and can shift quickly. The topic is about telling them apart and seeing how culture shapes the climate people experience.

5.16Creating a Culture for Sustainability and Innovation

A culture for sustainability and innovation is one where caring for long-term social and environmental impact, and trying out new ideas, are part of everyday values.

Organisations cannot innovate or act sustainably through policy alone. People need to feel safe to suggest ideas, try things and sometimes fail. This topic is about building values and habits that make responsible, creative behaviour normal rather than exceptional.

5.17Green Organisational Culture

Green organisational culture is a culture in which environmental responsibility is a shared value that guides how people work.

In such a culture, saving energy, cutting waste and thinking about environmental impact are expected of everyone, not left to one department. The topic matters because green goals only stick when they become part of what employees believe and do every day.

5.18Spirituality and Organisational Culture

Workplace spirituality is the recognition that people have an inner life that is fed by meaningful work done within a community.

It is not about religion. It is about people wanting their work to have purpose and to feel connected to others. The topic looks at how an organisation's culture can give employees that sense of meaning, and why this affects commitment and well-being.

5.19Organisational Change: Meaning and Forces for Change

Organisational change means making things different in an organisation, such as its structure, technology, processes or people. Planned change is change that is intentional and goal-oriented.

Organisations change because pressures inside and outside them push them to. These forces include new technology, competition, economic shifts, social trends and a changing workforce. The topic is about understanding what change is and why no organisation can stand still.

5.20Resistance to Change and Overcoming It

Resistance to change is the tendency of people and organisations to hold on to existing ways and oppose new ones.

People resist because change brings uncertainty, threatens habits, security or status, and can feel imposed. Resistance can be open or hidden. The topic covers why resistance happens and how managers can reduce it, mainly by communicating, involving people and supporting them through the change.

5.21Approaches to Managing Organisational Change

Approaches to managing change are structured ways of planning and carrying out change so that it succeeds and lasts.

Change rarely works if it is simply announced. Well-known approaches, such as Lewin's model and Kotter's model, give managers a sequence to follow, from preparing people for change to making the new way stick. The topic is about having a plan for change rather than reacting to it.

5.22Behavioural Change for Sustainability

Behavioural change for sustainability means getting employees to adopt environmentally responsible habits at work and keep them.

Green targets depend on the small daily choices of many people, such as switching off equipment, reducing waste or travelling less. The topic is about how organisations encourage and support those choices so that sustainable behaviour becomes routine rather than a one-off campaign.

Case study practice · Section C style

Arjun, a new plant head, is asked to cut energy use by 20 per cent. He announces the target, puts up posters, and threatens to dock bonuses if numbers do not fall. The senior supervisors, who have run the plant for twenty years, agree in meetings but change nothing. Bonuses are still paid only on output. A young engineer suggests a cheaper motor upgrade, but the maintenance chief blocks it and keeps the cost data to himself. After six months energy use is down 3 per cent and two good engineers have resigned.

(a) Identify the leadership, power, politics and conflict problems in this case. (b) What should Arjun do now?

Answer outline
  • Power: Arjun relies on legitimate and coercive power only. He has no referent power with the supervisors, and the engineer's expert power is being ignored.
  • Politics: the maintenance chief is controlling information to protect his position. Unclear goals (energy target but output-only bonus) and low trust make politics more likely.
  • Conflict: the supervisors avoid open conflict by agreeing in meetings and changing nothing. The clash between the energy target and the output bonus is a structural source of conflict.
  • Managing politics: set one clear goal, link part of the bonus to energy savings, share cost data openly, and confront the blocking early.
  • Leadership: move from transactional threats to transformational leadership (inspirational motivation, individualised consideration) and shared leadership, letting the engineer lead on the technical fix.
