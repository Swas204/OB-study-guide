Q1 · 5 marks

CO4 · L4 · Four criteria of a group

At Kaveri Textiles, Coimbatore, two collections of people are both called "groups" by HR. The first is a 300-member WhatsApp community of former employees that shares job openings now and then. The second is a five-member audit task force that meets daily to close the year-end books by 31 March. Analyse whether each one is a group in the OB sense, using the four defining criteria.

In OB, a group is two or more people who interact, depend on each other, see themselves as members and come together for a shared objective. All four criteria must be present.

  • Interaction: the WhatsApp community has some interaction, since members post and reply. The task force interacts every day while working on the books. Both meet this criterion.
  • Interdependence: no one in the WhatsApp community needs anyone else to get their job done. In the task force, one member cannot close the books until the others finish their reconciliations. Only the task force meets this.
  • Psychological awareness: WhatsApp members know they are in the community, but 300 people rarely think of themselves as "we". Task force members clearly see themselves as one unit. The task force meets this strongly.
  • Shared objective: the community has no common goal beyond loose information sharing. The task force has one clear goal: close the books by 31 March.
  • Verdict: the WhatsApp community fails interdependence and shared objective, so it is a network, not a group. The audit task force meets all four criteria and is a formal task group.
Marking scheme

Definition of a group: 1 mark. Each criterion applied to both collections: 1 mark each for any three criteria (3 marks). Correct verdict for both with reason: 1 mark.

Q2 · 5 marks

CO4 · L3 · Formal and informal groups

At Sahyadri Hospital, Pune, you notice four collections of staff: (i) the head nurse of Ward 4 and her twelve nurses, (ii) a committee of doctors and administrators set up to plan NABH accreditation, (iii) nurses from Kerala who eat lunch together every day, and (iv) a set of junior doctors campaigning together for shorter night shifts. Classify each into the correct type of group and explain one way each type matters to hospital management.

Groups are either formal, created by the organisation to get work done, or informal, emerging naturally from people's own needs.

  • (i) Command group (formal): a manager and her direct reports, as on the organisation chart. It matters because it is the main channel for giving instructions and checking ward performance.
  • (ii) Task group (formal): people from different departments brought together for one job. It matters because it pools skills the accreditation needs, and it ends when the job is done.
  • (iii) Friendship group (informal): people who come together because they share an identity and like each other. It matters because it gives belonging and support, which helps nurses cope with stressful shifts.
  • (iv) Interest group (informal): people joined by a shared cause. It matters because it signals a real grievance that management should hear early, before it hardens.
  • Overall point: formal groups carry the hospital's work, while informal groups meet members' social needs and shape how they feel about the work. Management needs to understand both.
Marking scheme

Correct classification with reason: 1 mark each for the four groups (4 marks). Formal vs informal distinction or overall management point: 1 mark.

Q3 · 5 marks

CO4 · L3 · Why people join groups

During a restructuring at Meridian BPO, Noida, many agents who had kept to themselves suddenly joined an employee forum, a weekend cricket club and a skills circle. Identify and explain five reasons why these employees joined groups, using the situation.

People join groups because groups meet needs that individuals cannot meet alone. Five reasons fit this case:

  • Security: restructuring creates fear of job loss. Standing alone feels risky, so the employee forum makes agents feel stronger and less anxious.
  • Power: one agent asking for fair redeployment can be ignored, but a forum of two hundred carries weight. Numbers bring influence.
  • Affiliation: people need social contact, and the cricket club meets that need at a lonely and tense time.
  • Self-esteem: being accepted and valued by the club or circle helps agents feel worthwhile when the company's message seems to be that they are replaceable.
  • Goal achievement: the skills circle helps members learn new processes together, which is faster than learning alone and improves their chance of being retained.

Status is a sixth reason: if the skills circle is seen as the place for high performers, joining it brings recognition.

Marking scheme

Any five reasons explained and linked to the restructuring: 1 mark each.

Q4 · 5 marks

CO4 · L3 · Tuckman's stages

A new product-launch committee at Nimbus Foods was formed three weeks ago. The polite introductions are over. Now the marketing head and the supply-chain head argue in every meeting about who should lead, two members have stopped attending, and nobody agrees on how decisions will be made. Identify the stage of group development the committee is in and recommend four actions the manager should take.

The committee is in the storming stage of Tuckman's model. The politeness of forming has worn off and members now clash over roles, control and direction. It is the hardest stage, because the conflict can split the group.

  • Bring the disagreement into the open: hold a meeting where the two heads state their views directly, instead of fighting through side comments.
  • Settle roles clearly: decide who leads which part of the launch, for example marketing leads positioning and supply chain leads logistics, so the leadership fight has an answer.
  • Agree on how decisions will be made: fix a rule such as majority vote with the chair deciding ties. This starts the move toward norming.
  • Keep attention on the goal: restate the launch date and target so members see that the fight is costing the shared objective.

Reconnecting with the two absent members is also useful, since withdrawal is a sign that storming is splitting the group.

Marking scheme

Correct stage with reason from the case: 1 mark. Any four manager actions suited to storming: 1 mark each.

Q5 · 5 marks

CO4 · L3 · Adjourning: task force vs permanent department

At an IT services firm in Hyderabad, a six-month data-migration task force has just finished its work. In the same month, the payroll department's long-serving head retires and two members move to other roles. Differentiate how adjourning applies to these two groups and explain what the manager should do in each case.

Adjourning is the final Tuckman stage, when a group wraps up. It does not look the same for every group.

  • Differentiation: the task force was temporary and truly adjourns, because its job is complete and it disbands. The payroll department is permanent, so it does not disband. It loses members and continues.
  • Task force, recognise the work: the manager should publicly thank the team and acknowledge what the migration achieved, since members may feel proud or a sense of loss.
  • Task force, debrief and help members move on: hold a lessons-learned session so the knowledge is kept, then help members settle into their next projects.
  • Payroll, manage handover: make sure the retiring head and the two movers pass on their knowledge before leaving, so payroll runs without errors.
  • Payroll, manage renewal: bring in the new members, explain roles and norms, and expect the changed group to pass through some forming again.
Marking scheme

Differentiation of temporary vs permanent group: 1 mark. Manager actions for the task force: 2 marks. Manager actions for the permanent department: 2 marks.

Q6 · 5 marks

CO4 · L4 · Factors influencing group behaviour

Two branches of Coastal Cooperative Bank have staff with similar qualifications and experience. The Mangaluru branch staff help each other and clear loan files fast. The Udupi branch staff work in silos and pass blame. The Udupi branch pays only individual incentives, has a cramped layout with staff on two floors, and two very abrasive senior clerks. Analyse the difference using the factors that influence group behaviour.

Group behaviour is shaped by three sets of factors: external conditions, group member resources and group structure.

  • External conditions, reward system: Udupi pays only individual incentives, so helping a colleague does not pay. Behaviour follows what is rewarded.
  • External conditions, physical setting: staff split across two floors find it hard to meet and talk, so coordination and trust suffer.
  • Group member resources, personality: abilities are similar in both branches, so ability does not explain the gap. But the two abrasive clerks make it harder for members to get on.
  • Group structure: these conditions shape the group's norms. At Udupi the norm has become "protect yourself and pass blame", while Mangaluru's norm is to help each other.
  • Conclusion: the gap comes from the conditions around the people, not their ability. Changing the reward system and layout would do more than retraining the staff.
Marking scheme

Three factor sets named: 1 mark. Each factor correctly applied to the case: 1 mark each for any three applications (3 marks). Conclusion: 1 mark.

Q7 · 5 marks

CO4 · L3 · Role concepts

Identify the role concept shown in each situation and justify your answer briefly:

  1. Arjun, promoted from machine operator to shift supervisor, now defends management's overtime rules he once complained about.
  2. A new trainee believes her job is mainly to stay quiet and take notes in client meetings.
  3. Her manager, however, expects trainees to ask questions and suggest ideas.
  4. Staff at a startup worked long hours because they believed they would get stock options, but the options never came and effort collapsed.
  5. A relationship manager is told by his boss to sell a high-fee product and by his clients to give honest, low-cost advice.

A role is the set of behaviours expected of someone in a given position.

  1. Role identity: the attitudes and behaviours that go with a role. Arjun adopted management's view as soon as his role changed.
  2. Role perception: her own view of how she is supposed to act, built from what she has watched and assumed.
  3. Role expectations: how others believe she should act. The gap between (2) and (3) is a mismatch the manager should clear up.
  4. Psychological contract: the unwritten agreement on what each side expects. The firm broke its side (stock options), so trust and effort fell.
  5. Role conflict: meeting the boss's demand makes it hard to meet the clients' demand. He serves two masters with clashing expectations.
Marking scheme

Correct concept with justification: 1 mark each (5 marks). No marks for the label alone without a reason.

Q8 · 5 marks

CO4 · L3 · Task, maintenance and self-oriented roles

In a project meeting at Indus Logistics, five members say the following: Priya: "Here are last quarter's delivery figures." Karan: "Let me summarise what we have agreed so far." Megha: "Rohan hasn't spoken yet, let's hear him." Vikram: "That idea won't work, and neither will the next one." Sana: "Before we vote, can someone tell me if my proposal from last week got noticed by the director?" Classify each member's behaviour into a role family and advise the manager on handling the self-oriented roles.

Group roles fall into three families: task roles that help the group reach its goal, maintenance roles that keep relationships healthy, and self-oriented roles that serve the individual at the group's cost.

  • Priya, task role: giving information moves the work forward.
  • Karan, task role: summarising keeps the group on track toward a decision.
  • Megha, maintenance role: encouraging a quiet member keeps participation fair and relationships healthy.
  • Vikram and Sana, self-oriented roles: Vikram blocks ideas without offering alternatives. Sana seeks attention for her own work in the middle of a group decision.
  • Advice: the manager should keep both task and maintenance roles active and check self-oriented ones. Ask Vikram to suggest an alternative each time he objects, and handle Sana's concern privately after the meeting so it does not derail the group.
Marking scheme

Correct classification of the five members: 0.5 mark each (2.5 marks). Explanation of the three families: 1 mark. Advice on self-oriented roles: 1.5 marks.

Q9 · 5 marks

CO4 · L3 · Group size and social loafing

A professor puts 12 students into one group for a 40-mark project. At the end, four students did almost all the work. Explain this outcome using the concepts of group size and social loafing, and design three strategies to prevent it.

Social loafing is the tendency to put in less effort in a group than when working alone.

  • Why size matters: bigger groups are better for fact-finding, but smaller groups are better for action. A 40-mark project needs action, and 12 is too many to coordinate.
  • Why loafing happens: in a large group each person's effort is hard to see and responsibility feels shared, so each member eases off. This is the Ringelmann effect: as more people pulled a rope, total pull rose but each person pulled less.
  • Strategy 1, keep groups small: split the class into groups of four or five, so every contribution matters.
  • Strategy 2, make contributions visible: assign each member a named section of the report and the presentation.
  • Strategy 3, add individual marks: combine a group mark with peer ratings, so effort is rewarded and loafing is noticed.
Marking scheme

Social loafing and the size trade-off explained: 1 mark. Ringelmann effect: 1 mark. Three strategies: 1 mark each.

Q10 · 5 marks

CO4 · L3 · Choosing a decision technique

Recommend the most suitable group decision-making technique for each situation and justify it:

  1. A marketing team needs 50 possible names for a new snack brand by Friday.
  2. A hospital committee keeps choosing whatever the senior surgeon suggests, while junior doctors stay silent.
  3. A cement company wants demand forecasts from eight experts in different states who should not influence one another.
  4. A three-person team must agree on today's dispatch schedule in ten minutes.
  1. Brainstorming: the aim is quantity. Any idea is welcome and criticism is not allowed, so members are not held back by fear of being judged.
  2. Nominal Group Technique: members write ideas silently before anyone speaks and rank them privately. This curbs domination by the senior surgeon.
  3. Delphi technique: experts never meet. They answer questionnaires over several rounds and revise after seeing the summary, so they stay anonymous and remote.
  4. Interacting group: a normal face-to-face discussion is fastest. With three people and a routine decision, the risk of conformity is low.

Overall point: each technique solves a different problem. The choice depends on whether the group needs ideas, protection from domination, anonymity or speed.

Marking scheme

Correct technique with justification: 1 mark each (4 marks). Overall point on matching technique to problem: 1 mark.

Q11 · 5 marks

CO4 · L3 · Johari Window

Nisha, a newly promoted team lead, is confident in her Excel skills, which everyone knows about. She does not realise that she sighs loudly when people make mistakes. She privately worries she was promoted too early. Nobody, including Nisha, yet knows how she handles client negotiations. Place each of these in the correct pane of the Johari Window and explain how Nisha can enlarge her open pane.

The Johari Window (Luft and Ingham) crosses what you know about yourself with what others know about you, giving four panes.

  • Open, Excel skills: known to Nisha and to others.
  • Blind, sighing at mistakes: others see it, Nisha does not.
  • Hidden, worry about early promotion: Nisha knows it, others do not.
  • Unknown, negotiation ability: neither side knows yet. It may appear when she handles her first client.
  • Enlarging the open pane: asking for feedback would tell her about the sighing and shrink the blind pane. Some self-disclosure, such as telling the team she is still learning the role, shrinks the hidden pane. Both enlarge the open pane, where trust lives.
Marking scheme

Four panes correctly placed: 0.75 mark each (3 marks). Feedback and self-disclosure explained: 2 marks.

Q12 · 5 marks

CO4 · L4 · Work group vs work team

At Pratham Motors (a fictional two-wheeler maker in Pune), the eight regional sales managers meet every Monday to share market updates, but each is appraised only on their own region's sales. Separately, a five-member squad of a designer, an engineer, a buyer, a marketer and a finance analyst has been set up to launch an electric scooter, and all five share one launch target. Analyse whether each is a work group or a work team.

A team is a group with positive synergy and shared accountability. Every team is a group, but not every group is a team.

  • Main activity: the sales managers meet only to share information so each can run their own region. The launch squad works jointly on one collective output, the scooter launch.
  • Synergy: the managers' combined result is just the sum of eight regions, so synergy is neutral. The squad's mix of design, engineering, buying, marketing and finance can produce a launch no single member could, so synergy is positive.
  • Accountability: each sales manager is judged only on their region, so accountability is individual. Squad members answer for their own part and for the shared launch target, so accountability is individual and mutual.
  • Skills: the managers all have similar sales skills, brought together by job title rather than design, so their skills are random. The squad's skills were chosen to fit together, so they are complementary.
  • Verdict: the sales managers form a work group. The launch squad is a work team.
Marking scheme

Definition of a team: 1 mark. Any three bases of difference applied to both cases: 1 mark each (3 marks). Correct verdict for both: 1 mark.

Q13 · 5 marks

CO4 · L3 · Why organisations need green teams

The sustainability head of Vindhya Cement (a fictional firm in Madhya Pradesh) proposes a green team of plant workers, engineers and supervisors. The CFO objects: "We already have an environment policy. Why spend time on a team?" Justify the need for a green team with five reasons.

Green teams are cross-functional groups that drive the sustainability agenda day to day. A policy states intentions. A green team turns them into action.

  • Cut waste and energy cost: cement is energy-heavy. People on the plant floor spot leaks, idle running and waste that a policy document never will, and every saving lowers the cost line the CFO cares about.
  • Meet tightening ESG and regulatory demands: pollution and emission rules keep getting stricter and investors ask for ESG proof. A green team gives the firm evidence of real action, not just a policy.
  • Protect brand reputation: a cement firm is often seen as a polluter. Visible green action protects its name with customers, communities and lenders.
  • Engage employees around purpose: workers who help reduce dust and emissions in their own town feel their work means more, which raises commitment.
  • Turn a vague vision into shop-floor action: the policy says "reduce our footprint". The team converts that into specific changes, such as fixing kiln heat losses, because the people who run the plant decide the steps.

So the team is not an extra cost. It is how the policy pays for itself.

Marking scheme

Any five reasons, each applied to the cement plant: 1 mark each (5 marks).

Q14 · 5 marks

CO4 · L4 · Virtual teams, trust and clear goals

Kalpa Software (a fictional IT firm) runs a product team split across Bengaluru, Manila and Toronto. The members have never met in person. Deadlines slip because each location works to its own idea of what "done" means, and the Bengaluru members suspect the Toronto members are not pulling their weight. Analyse the team's problems using the features of a virtual team and suggest two remedies.

A virtual team is one whose members work across locations and time zones, held together by technology rather than a shared room. Because of that, trust and clear goals matter even more than in a co-located team.

  • Unclear goals: each location has its own idea of "done". In a shared office this would be fixed in a quick chat. Across three time zones it is not, so confusion turns into missed deadlines.
  • Low trust: Bengaluru members cannot see Toronto members working, so they assume the worst. In a virtual team, trust has to replace the reassurance of seeing colleagues at their desks.
  • No shared room: the team is held together only by technology, so if the tools are not used to make work visible, the team behaves like three separate groups.
  • Remedy 1, clear shared goals: agree one written definition of "done" and one set of deadlines for the whole team, visible to all locations.
  • Remedy 2, visible work to build trust: use a shared board where every member updates progress, so contribution is seen rather than assumed, and hold a short regular video call to build relationships.
Marking scheme

Definition of a virtual team: 1 mark. Two problems analysed (goals, trust): 2 marks. Two suitable remedies: 1 mark each (2 marks).

Q15 · 5 marks

CO4 · L4 · Collaboration versus accountability

For its festive campaign, Tarang Retail (a fictional chain) asked marketing, stores, logistics and finance to "collaborate closely". Twenty people joined the planning group and ideas flowed. Yet two weeks before Diwali, nobody had booked the delivery trucks, because each department thought another had done it. Analyse what went wrong and explain how the firm should organise the next campaign.

Broad collaboration spreads ideas and ownership, but it can blur who is responsible when everyone owns everything. Effective teams hold both wide collaboration and clear individual and mutual accountability.

  • The benefit Tarang got: twenty people from four departments produced many ideas and felt ownership of the campaign.
  • The gap: because the whole group "owned" the campaign, no single person owned booking the trucks. Each department assumed another would do it, so the task fell into the gap between many willing hands.
  • Missing individual accountability: no task had a named owner, so nobody could be asked "why is this not done?"
  • Fix 1, named role owners: keep the wide planning group for ideas, but form small teams inside it and give every task, such as truck booking, one named owner with a deadline.
  • Fix 2, mutual accountability: review the full task list together each week, so the whole group still answers for the campaign as a whole.
Marking scheme

Collaboration vs accountability explained: 1 mark. Diagnosis of the gap from the case: 2 marks. Two fixes using named owners and mutual accountability: 1 mark each (2 marks).

Q16 · 5 marks

CO4 · L3 · Task-technology fit: synchronous vs asynchronous

A team lead at a Hyderabad analytics firm manages members in India and Germany. For each activity, recommend a synchronous or asynchronous tool and justify it in one line: (i) brainstorming names for a new product, (ii) the weekly status update, (iii) telling a member that their work has slipped badly, (iv) maintaining the project documentation, (v) settling a heated disagreement between two members.

Task-technology fit means the tool must suit the task. Synchronous tools (video, calls) are rich and real-time. Asynchronous tools (docs, email, boards) are lean and time-shifted.

  1. Brainstorming, synchronous (video call): creative work needs quick back-and-forth, with ideas building on each other in real time.
  2. Weekly status update, asynchronous (shared board or email): it is routine, and members in two time zones can read it when they start work.
  3. Telling a member their work has slipped, synchronous (video or phone call): sensitive feedback needs tone and the chance to respond. In writing it is easily misread.
  4. Project documentation, asynchronous (shared docs): it must be written down, kept and updated by anyone at any time.
  5. Settling a heated disagreement, synchronous (video call): emotional work needs a rich, real-time channel. Email threads tend to make conflict worse.
Marking scheme

1 mark per activity: 0.5 for the correct choice and 0.5 for a justification based on rich vs lean or real-time vs time-shifted.

Q17 · 10 marks

CO4 · L5 · Groups vs individuals

The new CEO of a mid-sized pharmaceutical firm announces: "From now on, every major decision will be made by a committee, never by one manager." Evaluate this policy by weighing the strengths and weaknesses of group decision-making against individual decision-making.

Group decision-making means a decision is reached by several members together rather than by one person. It has clear strengths and weaknesses, so a blanket rule needs testing.

Strengths of the committee approach

  • More complete information: a decision on a new drug launch needs input from R&D, regulatory, marketing and finance. No single manager holds all that knowledge.
  • More viewpoints: members see different risks and options. A regulatory head may spot an approval problem that marketing would miss.
  • Higher acceptance: people who helped make a decision support it. A plant-closure decision made with production heads meets less resistance during implementation.
  • More legitimacy: a committee decision is seen as fairer than one person's call, which matters for sensitive choices such as promotions.

Weaknesses of the committee approach

  • Slower: meeting and agreeing takes time. A pricing response to a competitor's discount cannot wait two weeks for a committee.
  • Conformity pressure: members go along to fit in, which can lead to groupthink.
  • Domination by a few: a senior or loud member may steer the committee, so the "group" decision is really one person's.
  • Unclear responsibility: when no one person is accountable, follow-through suffers and blame is spread thin.

Evaluation

The policy is right for complex, high-stakes decisions where information is spread out and acceptance matters, such as entering a new therapy area. It is wrong for urgent or routine decisions, where speed and clear accountability matter more. A better rule is to match the decision type to the method, and to use techniques such as NGT to limit domination when committees are used.

Marking scheme

Definition: 1 mark. Four strengths with examples: 3 marks. Four weaknesses with examples: 3 marks. Balanced evaluation with conditions: 2 marks. Conclusion: 1 mark.

Q18 · 10 marks

CO4 · L4 · Groupthink diagnosis and remedies

The credit committee of a hypothetical private bank, Suvarna Bank, approves a large unsecured loan to a well-known real-estate group. The chairman calls the borrower "too big to fail". A risk officer's note about falling sales is dismissed as "pessimistic". Two members privately doubt the deal but stay quiet, and the vote is recorded as unanimous. A year later the loan turns bad. Identify the symptoms of groupthink in this case and outline the remedies management should introduce to prevent a repeat.

Groupthink occurs when the desire for agreement overrides honest evaluation. Members value unity more than testing the decision, so weak ideas pass unchallenged.

Symptoms in the case

  • Illusion of invulnerability: "too big to fail" shows the committee believed the loan could not go wrong.
  • Rationalising warnings: the risk officer's note was dismissed as pessimistic instead of being examined.
  • Pressure on dissenters: labelling the note "pessimistic" told others that raising problems was unwelcome.
  • Self-censorship: two members with doubts kept quiet.
  • Illusion of unanimity: the vote was recorded as unanimous, so silence was read as agreement.
  • Mindguards (possible): if anyone kept the full risk data away from the committee, that would be mindguarding.

Remedies

  • Devil's advocate: for every large loan, one member is formally assigned to argue against approval. Objections are then raised as part of the process, so nobody is seen as disloyal.
  • Outside expert review: loans above a set limit go to an independent credit expert before the vote. An outsider is not bound by the committee's wish to agree.
  • Make dissent safe and visible: the chairman speaks last, and the minutes record each member's view and reasons, so self-censorship and false unanimity become harder.

Why it matters for a bank: a credit committee exists to test risk. When its members value unity over testing, it approves loans that one careful officer would reject, and the cost falls on depositors and shareholders.

Conclusion: the committee did not fail for lack of information. The risk officer had it. It failed because the group's wish to agree silenced that information. The remedies work by making disagreement a normal part of the process.

Marking scheme

Definition: 1 mark. Any five symptoms identified from the case: 1 mark each (5 marks). Remedies explained with implementation: 3 marks. Conclusion: 1 mark.

Q19 · 10 marks

CO4 · L4 · Group shift and risky shift

Before the meeting, each of the six members of a mutual fund's investment committee privately favoured putting 10% of the fund into a volatile small-cap sector. After a two-hour discussion, the committee voted to put in 25%. Analyse this outcome using the concept of group shift, explain why it happened, distinguish it from groupthink, and suggest how the decision process could be changed.

Group shift (polarisation) means discussion pushes members toward a more extreme version of the view they started with.

  • Direction of the shift: members already leaned bold, preferring 10% in a risky sector. Discussion did not moderate them. It pushed them further in the same direction, to 25%.
  • This is a risky shift: the group backed a bolder choice than any single member would have made alone.
  • Why it happened, shared responsibility: if the bet fails, no single member carries the blame. Responsibility is spread across six people, so each feels freer to accept more risk.
  • Discussion reinforced the lean: as members heard others support the sector, the bold view seemed more normal and confident.
  • Distinction from groupthink: groupthink is about the urge for agreement suppressing honest evaluation. Group shift is about the final position becoming more extreme than the starting views. Here members may have evaluated honestly but still ended more extreme.

Changing the process

  • Record private positions first: each member writes their preferred allocation before discussion, as in the Nominal Group Technique, so the starting views stay visible.
  • Private ranking at the end: members rank options privately rather than voting in open discussion, which reduces the pull of the group mood.
  • Restore individual accountability: record who proposed and supported the final figure, so responsibility does not feel shared away.

Note: group shift can also run the other way. A committee whose members lean cautious can end up even more cautious after discussion, and miss good opportunities.

Conclusion: the committee became bolder because shared responsibility lowered each member's sense of risk. Structuring the process so individual views and accountability stay visible reduces the shift.

Marking scheme

Definition: 1 mark. Risky shift identified: 2 marks. Shared-responsibility reason: 2 marks. Distinction from groupthink: 2 marks. Process changes: 2 marks. Conclusion: 1 mark.

Q20 · 10 marks

CO4 · L5 · Cohesiveness and performance norms

An HR head at a Chennai manufacturing firm says: "Build a tightly bonded team and productivity will follow automatically." Assess the validity of this statement using the relationship between cohesiveness and performance norms.

Cohesiveness is how strongly members are attracted to the group and want to stay in it. Performance norms are the group's shared standards for how hard to work and how much to produce.

Why the statement is only partly true

Cohesion does not decide which way a group pushes. It makes members follow the group's norms more closely. So its effect on productivity depends on the performance norms.

High performance normsLow performance norms
High cohesionStrong rise in productivityDrop in productivity
Low cohesionSlight rise, at bestLittle effect
  • High cohesion, high norms: a close-knit assembly team committed to zero defects pushes each member to meet that standard. Here the HR head is right.
  • High cohesion, low norms: a close-knit team whose norm is "do the minimum" holds each other to that low standard. A bonded team can block management's targets. Here the statement fails badly.
  • Low cohesion, high norms: the standard is high, but members are not bound to the group, so some follow it and others do not. Productivity rises slightly at best.
  • Low cohesion, low norms: there is no strong pull either way, so little effect.

Verdict and advice

The statement is valid only when the group's performance norms are high. Team outings and festival celebrations build bonds, but they do not by themselves raise the standard the team works to. The manager should first set and model high performance norms, through clear targets, recognition of high output and visible standards, and only then build cohesion. Building bonds first risks creating a tight group that protects low output.

CO4 · L4 · Transactional Analysis

Read three exchanges at a bank branch in Lucknow:

  1. Clerk: "Sir, has the cash limit for today been updated?" Manager: "Yes, it is ₹40 lakh, check the system."
  2. Clerk: "Sir, has the cash limit for today been updated?" Manager: "How many times must I tell you? Can't you do anything yourself?"
  3. Manager, smiling: "Nice of you to join us at 10:20."

Analyse each exchange using Transactional Analysis. Identify the ego states and the type of transaction, and advise the manager on how to communicate better.

Transactional Analysis (Eric Berne) studies transactions, the basic exchanges between two people, by looking at the ego state each person speaks from. The three ego states are Parent (taught, judging or caring), Adult (rational, fact-based, here and now) and Child (emotional, spontaneous or rebellious).

  • Exchange 1, complementary transaction: the clerk asks from the Adult state and expects an Adult reply. The manager answers with facts from the Adult state. The reply comes from the expected ego state, so communication is smooth.
  • Exchange 2, crossed transaction: the same Adult question gets a scolding reply from the judging Parent, aimed at the clerk's Child. The reply comes from an unexpected ego state, so communication breaks down. The clerk may feel humiliated and stop asking questions.
  • Exchange 3, ulterior transaction: on the surface it is a polite greeting. The hidden message from the Parent is "you are late again". The real message is the hidden one, and it builds resentment.
  • Life position: exchanges 2 and 3 suggest the manager is acting from "I'm OK, You're not OK", which treats staff as less capable.

Advice to the manager

  • Speak from the Adult state: answer questions with facts, as in exchange 1.
  • Avoid crossed transactions: if irritated, pause and give the factual answer, then raise any repeated problem calmly and separately.
  • Make hidden messages direct: instead of sarcasm, say plainly, "Branch opens at 10. Please be here on time."
  • Work from "I'm OK, You're OK": respect both himself and the staff, which makes problems easier to solve together.

Conclusion: the same question can lead to smooth or broken communication depending on the ego state of the reply. Adult-to-Adult transactions keep a branch running smoothly.

CO4 · L4 · Johari Window for team trust

After a merger, two finance teams at a Mumbai conglomerate are combined. Members are guarded, rumours spread, and people only discover each other's strengths by accident. Analyse the team's problem using the Johari Window, and design practices that would build trust.

The Johari Window (Luft and Ingham) is a self-awareness tool that crosses what a person knows about themselves with what others know about them. It produces four panes: open, blind, hidden and unknown.

Analysing the merged team

  • Small open pane: members share little, so few things are known to both sides. Communication is hardest when the open pane is small.
  • Large hidden pane: being guarded means members keep worries, skills and opinions to themselves. Fear about the merger keeps it large.
  • Large blind pane: nobody gives honest feedback across the old team lines, so people do not know how they come across to the other side.
  • Large unknown pane: strengths come to light only by accident, so abilities neither side has seen are not being used.
  • Effect: rumours fill the gap left by missing information, and trust stays low.

Practices to build trust

  • Structured self-disclosure: each member shares their background, strengths and concerns about the merger in a facilitated session. This shrinks the hidden pane.
  • Regular two-way feedback: short monthly feedback conversations across old team lines tell members how they come across. This shrinks the blind pane.
  • Mixed project pairs: pair members from the two old teams on real tasks, so untested abilities come to light. This shrinks the unknown pane.
  • Leaders go first: when the team head openly admits what she is still learning, others feel safer disclosing.

Conclusion: feedback and self-disclosure both enlarge the open pane, and the open pane is where trust lives. A merged team with a larger open pane has fewer rumours and works with fewer misunderstandings.

CO4 · L3 · Tuckman's model in full

A Jaipur-based retail chain sets up an eight-member task force from IT, finance and store operations to roll out a new billing system across 60 stores within six months. Explain how this task force is likely to develop through Tuckman's five stages, and what the project manager should do at each stage.

Tuckman's model describes five stages most groups pass through. Each stage has its own mood, and the manager's role changes as the group moves on.

  • Forming: members from IT, finance and stores are polite, cautious and unsure who does what. They look to the manager for direction. Manager: set clear goals and the 60-store deadline, introduce members, and explain each person's role.
  • Storming: conflict starts. IT wants a fast rollout, store operations fears disruption during the festive season, and finance worries about cost. Members clash over control and direction. Manager: bring disagreements into the open, settle who decides what, and keep attention on the shared goal.
  • Norming: roles settle and members agree on working rules, such as a weekly review and a pilot in five stores first. Cohesion grows. Manager: reinforce useful norms, such as honest status updates, and start to step back.
  • Performing: energy moves to the task. The team rolls out store by store and solves problems quickly. Manager: delegate, provide resources and recognise results.
  • Adjourning: the rollout ends and the task force disbands. Some members feel proud, others feel a sense of loss. Manager: recognise the work, debrief lessons for future rollouts, and help members return to their departments.

Practical note: the stages are not evenly spaced. If storming is not handled, the task force can stay stuck there for months and miss the deadline. A skilled manager shortens storming by settling roles early, rather than hoping the conflict fades.

Conclusion: the model helps the manager expect conflict instead of being surprised by it, and adjust their style from directing in forming to delegating in performing.

CO4 · L5 · Groupthink, group shift and decision techniques

Vistara Electronics (a fictional consumer-electronics firm in Noida) set up a seven-member launch committee for its first smartwatch. The CEO chairs the meetings and opens each one by sharing his own view. The team has had three successful launches and members often say, "We never miss." When a market-research analyst reported that customers found the price too high, the committee called the data "a small sample" and moved on. Two engineers had concerns about battery life but did not raise them, saying later, "Everyone else seemed sure." Before the meeting, most members favoured a modest launch in three cities. After discussion, the committee approved a nationwide launch with a large ad budget. The launch failed and stock piled up.

(a) Critically evaluate the committee's decision-making using the concepts of groupthink and group shift. [7.5]

(b) As an OB consultant, formulate a five-step plan for the CEO to improve how the committee makes future launch decisions. [7.5]

(a) Evaluation

Groupthink occurs when the urge for agreement overrides honest evaluation. Group shift occurs when discussion pushes the group to a more extreme version of its starting view. Both are visible here.

  • Illusion of invulnerability: "We never miss" shows three past successes made the committee feel it could not fail.
  • Rationalising warnings: the price data was dismissed as "a small sample" instead of being examined.
  • Self-censorship: the two engineers kept their battery concerns to themselves.
  • Illusion of unanimity: "Everyone else seemed sure" shows silence was read as agreement.
  • Domination by a few: the CEO stating his view first set the direction, which is a known weakness of group decisions and adds to conformity pressure.
  • Risky shift: members privately preferred a three-city launch but the group approved a nationwide one. The group backed a bolder choice than members would have alone, because responsibility felt shared.
  • Pressure on dissenters (implied): calling the analyst's data "a small sample" in front of everyone also signals that bringing bad news is unwelcome, so the next person with a doubt is less likely to speak.

Evaluation: the committee had the information it needed, from the analyst and the engineers. A group should beat an individual because it brings more complete information and more viewpoints. Here it lost both, because conformity and shared responsibility silenced that information. In effect, a seven-member committee made a one-person decision, the CEO's, while spreading the blame across seven people. The failure came from the process, not from a lack of talent. The cost is visible in the unsold stock and the wasted national ad budget.

(b) Five-step plan

  1. CEO speaks last: the CEO stops opening meetings with his view and shares it only after others have spoken. This reduces domination and pressure to agree.
  2. Silent idea round (NGT): before discussion, each member writes down their view and concerns about the launch, then presents them one at a time. Hidden doubts, like the engineers', reach the table.
  3. Assign a devil's advocate: for every launch decision, one member is formally asked to argue against the plan, so objections are expected and nobody is seen as disloyal.
  4. Outside expert review: major launches go to an independent market expert before approval, since an outsider is not bound by the committee's wish to agree.
  5. Private ranking and named accountability: members rank options privately, and the minutes record who supported the final scale, so responsibility does not feel shared away and the risky shift is checked.

How the plan would have changed this launch: in the silent round the engineers would have written down the battery concern. The devil's advocate would have had to argue the analyst's pricing case. Private ranking would have shown that most members preferred the three-city launch.

Conclusion: together these steps keep the strengths of a group decision while limiting groupthink and the risky shift. The order matters: the CEO first removes his own pressure, then the process brings hidden views out, and only then does the group decide.

  3. Change the reward system: introduce a group bonus for output above 450, so the whole line gains from higher production and the norm "the pay is the same" no longer holds.
  4. Make contributions visible: split the 30-worker line into smaller cells of six with their own output boards, which reduces social loafing.
  5. Use cohesion to hold the new norm: recognise cells that meet the higher target in front of the whole line. Once the norm is high, the group's strong bonds work for management, moving the line to the high-cohesion, high-norm cell.

Why the plan keeps unity: it never punishes the group or singles out individuals. It changes the norm through the group itself, its informal leaders and a shared reward, so the bonds stay intact. Breaking up the group would lose the cohesion that makes the new norm stick.

Conclusion: the aim is to move the line from high cohesion with low norms to high cohesion with high norms. The same unity that held output at 450 can then hold it at 600.

Marking scheme

(a) Norms: 1.5 marks. Conformity with Asch: 1.5 marks. Cohesiveness matrix applied: 2 marks. Size or reward system: 1 mark. Verdict on the supervisor's statement: 1.5 marks. (b) Five sequenced, management-led steps: 1.5 marks each (7.5 marks).

Q33 · 15 marks

CO4 · L5 · Transactional Analysis and Johari Window

Meenakshi, branch head of a fictional cooperative bank in Madurai, is highly skilled and gets results, but her team avoids her. When a cashier asks, "Ma'am, should I process this cheque now?" she replies, "Do I have to think for all of you?" She praises staff in a tone that sounds like criticism, such as "Good of you to finally finish that report." Her staff say she never asks for their opinions. In a recent survey, staff rated communication in the branch very low, which surprised her, as she believes she is "clear and direct". Meenakshi privately feels she must appear strong because she is the youngest branch head in the region.

(a) Analyse Meenakshi's communication problems using Transactional Analysis and the Johari Window. [7.5]

(b) Formulate a five-step development plan the regional manager should implement to improve communication in the branch. [7.5]

(a) Analysis

Transactional Analysis (Berne) explains exchanges through the ego states people speak from: Parent, Adult and Child.

  • Crossed transaction: the cashier asks a factual Adult question and expects an Adult reply. Meenakshi replies from the judging Parent, aimed at the cashier's Child. The reply comes from an unexpected ego state, so communication breaks down and staff stop asking.
  • Ulterior transaction: "Good of you to finally finish that report" looks like praise on the surface but carries a hidden critical message. Staff respond to the hidden message, which builds resentment.
  • Life position: her replies suggest "I'm OK, You're not OK", treating staff as less capable. The healthiest position is "I'm OK, You're OK".

The Johari Window (Luft and Ingham) shows what is known to self and to others.

  • Blind pane: her harsh, sarcastic style is seen by staff but not by her. She thinks she is "clear and direct", which is why the survey surprised her.
  • Hidden pane: her worry about being the youngest branch head is known only to her. It drives the Parent behaviour, but staff cannot see the reason.
  • Small open pane: she does not ask for opinions or share her concerns, so little is known to both sides and trust stays low.

Linking the two models: the hidden worry explains the Parent behaviour. Because she feels she must look strong, she replies from the judging Parent to show control. Because that style sits in her blind pane, she cannot correct it. Her skill and results are real, but staff avoid her, so problems reach her late or not at all, which is a risk for a bank branch.

(b) Five-step development plan

  1. Structured feedback session: the regional manager shares the survey results and specific examples with Meenakshi privately. This feedback shrinks her blind pane.
  2. Coaching on TA: arrange coaching so she learns to recognise her Parent replies and practise answering from the Adult state, aiming for complementary transactions.
  3. Encourage self-disclosure: help her share with the team that she is new to the role and wants their help. This shrinks the hidden pane and makes her pressure understandable.
  4. Regular two-way meetings: introduce weekly branch meetings where she asks for staff opinions first, building an "I'm OK, You're OK" relationship.
  5. Review and repeat: repeat the communication survey after three months, discuss the change with her, and continue coaching where needed.

Example of the change: to the cashier's question, an Adult reply would be, "Yes, process it now, it is within your limit." Praise without the ulterior sting would be, "Thank you for finishing the report, the client section was clear."

Conclusion: the plan starts with feedback because she cannot change what she cannot see. Coaching and disclosure follow, and the survey checks the result. Keeping her skills while fixing her communication protects both performance and the team.

Marking scheme

(a) Crossed and ulterior transactions identified: 2.5 marks. Life position: 1 mark. Johari blind, hidden and open panes applied: 3 marks. Link between the two models: 1 mark. (b) Five sequenced steps led by the regional manager: 1.5 marks each (7.5 marks).

Q34 · 15 marks

CO4 · L5 · Green teams, group vs team, team effectiveness

Amrit Dairy (a fictional cooperative dairy in Gujarat) set up a "green committee" a year ago to cut water and energy use at its main plant. Its twelve members are all middle managers from the head office, chosen because they volunteered. The committee meets once a month, members report on what their own department has done, and the minutes end with "all to continue efforts". No targets have been set and no plant workers are members. Water use has not fallen. The committee's work is not counted in anyone's appraisal, so attendance has dropped to five. Meanwhile, a large retail buyer has asked for proof of water reduction before renewing its contract, and the state has announced stricter rules on effluent. The managing director now asks whether the committee should simply be shut down.

(a) Critically evaluate the green committee using the difference between a work group and a work team, the reasons organisations need green teams, and the factors that make a team effective. [7.5]

(b) Formulate a five-step plan for the MD to turn the committee into an effective green team. [7.5]

(a) Evaluation

A team is a group with positive synergy and shared accountability. Green teams are cross-functional groups that drive the sustainability agenda day to day. The green committee is neither.

  • It is a work group, not a team: members share information about their own departments and no one answers for a joint result. Accountability is individual, synergy is neutral, and the output is only the sum of separate reports.
  • Random, not complementary, skills: members were chosen because they volunteered, not for the skills a water-saving task needs. There are no plant workers or engineers who know where water is used.
  • Context failures: no resources or targets, no reward that recognises the team (the work is outside appraisals), and no visible leadership.
  • Process failures: no common purpose turned into specific goals. "All to continue efforts" is not a goal, and falling attendance shows low team confidence and free riding.
  • Why shutting it down is wrong: the reasons for green teams apply directly. The buyer's demand puts brand reputation and revenue at risk. Stricter effluent rules raise regulatory pressure. Cutting water and energy also cuts cost. What has failed is the design, not the need.

Evaluation: the committee was set up as a green team in name only. It cannot turn the green vision into shop-floor action because the shop floor is not in the room. Closing it now, just as a buyer and the regulator are asking for proof, would remove the one structure that could provide it.

(b) Five-step plan

  1. Reset purpose and goals: the MD restates the purpose (meet the buyer's and the regulator's demands while cutting cost) and sets specific targets, such as a 20 percent cut in water use per litre of milk within nine months.
  2. Rebuild composition: replace the volunteer head-office list with a smaller cross-functional team of about seven: plant operators, a maintenance engineer, a quality officer, a finance analyst and a production supervisor, so skills are complementary.
  3. Fix the context: give the team a budget for meters and repairs, a senior sponsor it trusts, and make its results part of members' appraisals and a team reward.
  4. Create joint accountability: give each improvement project a named owner, review the team's water and energy figures weekly on the plant floor, and hold the whole team answerable for the target.
  5. Show results and keep momentum: report savings to the buyer and the workforce, celebrate early wins to build team confidence, and use the evidence for the effluent rules.

Why this order: the team needs a clear goal before the right members can be chosen. It needs resources and rewards before joint accountability can be enforced. Results are shared only once there is something real to show.

Conclusion: the answer to the MD is to redesign the committee, not to close it. With complementary skills, clear goals and shared accountability, it becomes the green team the dairy needs.

Marking scheme

(a) Group vs team applied: 2 marks. Effectiveness factors (context, composition, process): 3 marks. Reasons green teams are needed, with a verdict on closing the committee: 2.5 marks. (b) Five sequenced, management-led steps: 1.5 marks each (7.5 marks).

Q35 · 15 marks

CO4 · L5 · Virtual cross-functional team, collaboration and task-technology fit

Sahyog Pharma (a fictional drug maker) must publish its first ESG report in six months. It forms a virtual team of fifteen people across Mumbai, Hyderabad and its London office, drawn from finance, operations, marketing and legal. Everyone is told to "collaborate on everything". All discussion happens in one large chat group, and data is shared as email attachments that keep getting overwritten. Two months in, nobody can say who is collecting the emissions data. The London members feel left out because calls are set at times that suit India. A sharp argument between finance and marketing over how to present the numbers has dragged on for weeks over email. Members have never met, and each assumes the others are doing less than they claim.

(a) Analyse the team's problems using the features of virtual and cross-functional sustainability teams, collaboration versus accountability, and task-technology fit. [7.5]

(b) Formulate a five-step plan for senior management to get the ESG report delivered on time. [7.5]

(a) Analysis

  • Virtual team without trust or clear goals: members across three locations are held together only by technology. In such teams trust and clear goals matter even more. Here members have never met and suspect each other, and "collaborate on everything" is not a goal.
  • Cross-functional team without joint ownership: the team rightly blends finance, operations, marketing and legal, as an ESG goal needs. But nobody owns specific parts of the report, so the blend produces confusion rather than a shared result.
  • Collaboration without accountability: "collaborate on everything" spread ownership so widely that no one is responsible for the emissions data. Everyone owns everything, so the task falls into the gap between many willing hands.
  • Poor task-technology fit, asynchronous side: data shared as email attachments gets overwritten. Records and data belong in shared documents, which are lean, time-shifted and lasting.
  • Poor task-technology fit, synchronous side: the finance and marketing conflict is being handled over email, a lean channel with no tone, so it drags on. Conflict needs a rich, real-time video call.
  • Time zones handled badly: calls set for India's convenience exclude London. Routine updates should be asynchronous so every location can take part.

Overall: the team has the right people and the right tools, but uses the tools the wrong way and has no clear roles, goals or trust. Technology carries a team, it does not build it.

(b) Five-step plan

  1. Set the goal and break it down: senior management confirms the deadline and splits the report into sections (emissions, energy, social, governance, financial disclosures) with dated milestones.
  2. Name role owners: each section gets one named owner, for example operations for emissions data, while the whole team stays accountable for the full report. This closes the gap that left the emissions data unowned.
  3. Agree a channel guide: one shared document workspace for all data and drafts, a shared board for weekly updates, and video calls only for decisions, brainstorming and disagreements. Rotate call times so London is not always at a disadvantage.
  4. Resolve the conflict and build trust: hold a video meeting between finance and marketing, with a senior sponsor, to agree how the numbers will be presented. Make each member's progress visible on the board, so contribution is seen rather than assumed.
  5. Review and adjust: run a short fortnightly video review of milestones against the plan, fix blockers quickly, and recognise the team's progress.

Why this order: roles cannot be assigned until the work is broken into parts. Tools can only be matched to tasks once roles are clear. Trust grows once work is visible, and reviews keep the plan on track.

Conclusion: the ESG report can still be delivered if the team keeps wide collaboration but adds named accountability, uses each tool for the task it fits, and builds the trust and clear goals a virtual team depends on.

Marking scheme

(a) Virtual team features: 1.5 marks. Cross-functional team: 1.5 marks. Collaboration vs accountability: 2 marks. Task-technology fit (sync and async): 2.5 marks. (b) Five sequenced, management-led steps: 1.5 marks each (7.5 marks).
