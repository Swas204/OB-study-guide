2.10Values and the Importance of Values

Definition

Values are basic convictions that a specific mode of conduct or end-state of existence is personally or socially preferable to an opposite or converse mode of conduct or end-state of existence.

Milton Rokeach

In plainer words: values are your settled beliefs about what is right, good and desirable. Note the word preferable. A value is not a neutral description of the world. It carries a judgement, so it always implies that the opposite is worse.

The two attributes of every value

Attribute 1
Content attribute
  • Says that the mode of conduct or end-state is important.
  • "Honesty matters."
Attribute 2
Intensity attribute
  • Says how important it is.
  • "Honesty matters more to me than getting promoted."

When you rank your values by intensity, you get your value system. Two people can hold exactly the same values and still clash badly, because they rank them differently. That point is worth making in an answer, because it explains most workplace value conflict.

Characteristics of values

  • Formed early. Largely established by adolescence, through parents, teachers, friends, community, religion and culture.
  • Relatively stable and enduring. They can change, but slowly and usually only after a significant life event.
  • Judgemental. They carry an idea of what ought to be, not just what is.
  • They influence perception. Values act as a filter, so two people looking at the same situation see different things.
  • Mostly not objectively verifiable. You cannot prove that loyalty is better than ambition.

Importance of values in OB

01
Foundation for attitudes and motivation

Values come first. Attitudes are built on them, and motivation flows from what a person values. You cannot understand either without values.

02
They cloud objectivity

Values mean people enter a job with preconceptions about how things ought to be. This distorts rationality, which matters for the decision-making biases in Unit 3.

03
They influence perception

An employee who values equity reads a fast promotion for someone else as unfairness. Someone who values achievement reads the same event as proof that merit works.

04
Person-organisation fit

Employees whose values match the organisation's are more satisfied, perform better and stay longer. Value mismatch produces exit even when pay is good.

05
They drive ethical conduct

Codes of conduct only work where they align with what employees already value. This is the basis of the whistle-blowing section below.

06
They are the foundation of culture

Organisational culture is shared values. So everything in Unit 5 on culture rests on this section.

Example · person-organisation fit in practice

A graduate who values security and stability joins a two-year-old start-up on a 40 per cent higher salary. Within eight months she is miserable, not because the work is bad but because targets change monthly and nothing is settled. A colleague who values excitement and variety finds the same environment energising. Neither the salary nor the job changed. The fit did.

Exam tip

You will not be asked to reproduce all thirty-six. Learn the definition of each category, the ends versus means logic, and about five examples from each column. Then add the finding below, which is the part that earns the extra marks.

The research finding to quote

Rokeach found that people in the same occupation or category tend to hold similar value systems, but different groups hold significantly different ones. Corporate managers, union members and activists each ranked the RVS differently, and those differences explain much of the conflict between them.

Example: corporate managers and union members both ranked honesty highly, but managers ranked a comfortable life and responsible far higher, while union members ranked equality and freedom far higher. Both sides are behaving with integrity according to their own value system, which is why the dispute feels moral to both and neither yields to evidence.

Example · a value conflict inside one person

A finance manager holds the terminal value family security and the instrumental value honest. Her CFO asks her to defer recognising a known expense into the next quarter, which she believes is wrong. Refusing puts her job, and therefore family security, at risk.

Nothing in her values has changed, and she is not a weak person. Two of her own values now point in opposite directions, and her value system, the ranking, decides what she does. This is exactly the situation the next section is about.

2.12Whistle-blowing as an Ethical Issue in OB

Definition

Whistle-blowing is the disclosure by an organisation member of illegal, immoral or illegitimate practices under the control of their employer, to persons or organisations who may be able to take action.

Near and Miceli

Internal and external whistle-blowing

First resort
Internal whistle-blowing
  • Reported within the organisation: to a supervisor, the audit committee, an ethics officer or a vigil mechanism.
  • Gives the organisation a chance to fix the problem itself.
  • Lower personal cost, and usually protected by policy.
  • Fails when the wrongdoing involves the very people who receive the complaint.
Last resort
External whistle-blowing
  • Reported outside: to a regulator such as SEBI, to the police, to the media, or to a court.
  • Usually happens only after internal channels have failed or are compromised.
  • Far higher personal cost, and near-certain retaliation.
  • Most effective at forcing action, and most damaging to the organisation.

Why whistle-blowing is an OB problem, not only a legal one

The legal question is whether disclosure is protected. The OB question is different and harder: why do most employees who see wrongdoing say nothing? Surveys consistently find that far more employees observe misconduct than report it. The reasons are behavioural, and every one of them appears elsewhere in this syllabus:

  • Fear of retaliation, which is low psychological safety (Unit 1).
  • Diffusion of responsibility: someone more senior must already know.
  • Loyalty and in-group norms: reporting feels like betraying the team (Unit 4).
  • Conformity and obedience to authority: the instruction came from a superior (Unit 4).
  • Rationalisation: "everyone in this industry does it", which is moral disengagement.
  • Learned helplessness: previous reports went nowhere.
  • Value ranking: family security outranks courage, as in the example above.

When is whistle-blowing ethically justified?

The standard test in business ethics, useful for a ten-mark answer, sets conditions that should all be met:

  1. The wrongdoing involves serious and considerable harm to the public or to identifiable people.
  2. The employee has reported it internally first, unless doing so is futile or dangerous.
  3. There is documented evidence that a reasonable observer would find convincing, not just suspicion.
  4. There is a reasonable expectation that disclosure will prevent or correct the harm.
  5. The motive is the public interest, not revenge or personal gain.

The competing duty on the other side is the employee's duty of loyalty and confidentiality to the employer. The ethical argument is that loyalty is owed to the organisation's legitimate purposes, not to its wrongdoing, so concealing harm is not loyalty but complicity.

The Indian legal and governance framework

ProvisionWhat it requires

Example · the cost side, in one Indian case

Satyendra Dubey, a project director at the National Highways Authority of India, wrote in 2002 about corruption in the Golden Quadrilateral project, asking that his identity be kept confidential. His letter was circulated within the bureaucracy with his name attached. He was murdered in November 2003.

The case is the standard Indian illustration of why the behavioural question matters more than the legal one. A policy that permits reporting is worthless if the process leaks the reporter's identity. Confidentiality is not a courtesy in a whistle-blower mechanism. It is the mechanism.

What makes a whistle-blower mechanism actually work

  • Genuine anonymity and a channel that bypasses line management, since the line manager is often the subject.
  • Independent receipt, usually by the audit committee or an external ombudsman, not by HR alone.
  • Non-retaliation enforced visibly. One publicised case of a reporter being protected teaches more than the entire policy document.
  • Feedback to the reporter, so the channel does not feel like a void.
  • Tone from the top. If senior management treats a report as disloyalty, no structure will compensate.
  • Psychological safety at team level, because most concerns are first voiced informally to a colleague or a supervisor, long before any formal mechanism is used.
The link to make in an answer

Whistle-blowing is where values (2.10), attitudes (2.15), employee voice and silence (Unit 1), conformity and group norms (Unit 4) and power (Unit 5) all meet in a single decision by one person. That is why the syllabus places it in the values section rather than treating it as a law topic.

2.13Values Across Cultures: Hofstede's Framework

Geert Hofstede surveyed more than 116,000 IBM employees across 40 countries, later extended to over 70, and found that national culture varies along a small number of dimensions. His book is on your prescribed list, so quote him by name.

Examples · one per dimension, in workplace terms
  • Power distance (India high): a junior analyst who spots an error in the partner's model raises it privately through a senior, or not at all. In Denmark, which scores low, they would say it in the meeting. Neither is rudeness or cowardice. It is the dimension.
  • Collectivism: an individual "Employee of the Month" award can embarrass the winner and annoy the team in a collectivist setting, where team recognition works better.
  • Masculinity: in a high-masculinity workplace, a manager who leaves at 6 pm for a child's function is judged. In a high-femininity one, that is normal and expected.
  • Uncertainty avoidance: a German subsidiary asks for the written SOP before starting. The Indian team starts and works it out, then documents later. This is the single most common source of friction in Indo-German joint ventures.
  • Long-term orientation: a family business willing to run a loss for five years to establish a brand, against a listed company managing to the quarter.
  • Indulgence vs restraint: how acceptable it is to take your full leave entitlement without apology.
Criticisms of Hofstede · needed for an "evaluate" question
  1. One company, one era. The original data came only from IBM employees, collected in the 1960s and 1970s. IBM employees are not a cross-section of any society, and the data is now decades old.
  2. Nation equals culture. The framework treats each country as culturally uniform. This is least defensible for India, where variation across states, languages, castes, religions and urban versus rural life is enormous. A single score of 77 for power distance conceals all of it.
  3. Ecological fallacy. A national average says nothing reliable about the individual in front of you. Using it to predict one colleague's behaviour is a statistical error, and in practice becomes a sophisticated form of stereotyping.
  4. Western-centred dimension design. The questions were framed within a Western management vocabulary, so what mattered locally may never have been asked. The fifth and sixth dimensions were added later precisely because the original four missed non-Western concerns.
  5. The "masculinity" label is dated and confuses a gender description with a values dimension.

2.14The GLOBE Framework

What it is

GLOBE stands for Global Leadership and Organizational Behavior Effectiveness. Begun in 1991 under Robert House, it is an ongoing cross-cultural investigation covering 62 societies, around 825 organisations and roughly 17,000 managers.

GLOBE was designed partly to update and extend Hofstede, and it is the more current framework. It confirmed that Hofstede's dimensions were broadly valid while adding to them.

The nine dimensions

DimensionMeaningRelation to Hofstede
Power DistanceDegree to which members expect power to be unequally sharedSame as Hofstede
Uncertainty AvoidanceReliance on social norms and procedures to reduce unpredictabilitySame as Hofstede
AssertivenessHow confrontational, assertive and aggressive people are in relationshipsSplits out one half of Hofstede's masculinity
Gender EgalitarianismExtent to which the society minimises gender role differencesSplits out the other half of masculinity
Institutional CollectivismExtent to which people are encouraged to integrate into organisations and societySplits Hofstede's collectivism into two
In-Group CollectivismPride, loyalty and cohesiveness within families and small groupsThe second half of that split
Future OrientationExtent to which the society rewards planning, investing and delaying gratificationClose to long-term orientation
Performance OrientationExtent to which the society rewards performance improvement and excellenceNew
Humane OrientationExtent to which the society rewards being fair, altruistic, generous and kindNew
GLOBE's most useful contribution: "as is" versus "should be"

GLOBE measured each dimension twice: cultural practices (how things actually are) and cultural values (how respondents believe things should be). The two often diverge sharply, and the gap is analytically valuable.

Example: in high power distance societies including India, respondents report high power distance as a practice but want considerably less of it as a value. So the hierarchy is a description of the present, not a preference to be preserved. A manager who flattens hierarchy is often working with what employees say they want, which is the opposite of what a Hofstede-only reading would suggest.

GLOBE also grouped societies into culture clusters, including Anglo, Latin Europe, Nordic Europe, Germanic Europe, Eastern Europe, Latin America, Sub-Saharan Africa, Middle East, Confucian Asia and Southern Asia, which is the cluster containing India, alongside Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines, Thailand and Iran.

The verdict to write

Hofstede remains the standard vocabulary and is the better teaching tool, because six dimensions can actually be held in mind. GLOBE is the better research instrument, and its practices-versus-values distinction is the more useful idea for a manager. The honest conclusion is that both commit the same underlying error: they describe averages, and managers deal with individuals. Use either to raise a hypothesis about likely friction, never to predict the behaviour of the person sitting opposite you.

2.15Components of Attitudes

Definition

Attitudes are evaluative statements, either favourable or unfavourable, about objects, people or events. They reflect how we feel about something.

Robbins and Judge

An attitude is narrower and more specific than a value. "Honesty matters" is a value. "This company's expense reporting is dishonest" is an attitude. Values are few and stable; attitudes are many, specific and more changeable.

The three components: A-B-C

Exam tip

This is a guaranteed five-mark question. Always answer it with a single worked example carried through all three components, as above, rather than defining the three separately. An examiner can see immediately whether you understand the model or have memorised three labels.

Cognitive dissonance

Definition

Cognitive dissonance is any incompatibility an individual might perceive between two or more attitudes, or between behaviour and an attitude.

Leon Festinger

Festinger's argument is that dissonance is uncomfortable, so people are motivated to reduce it. The important point is that they usually reduce it by changing the attitude to fit the behaviour, not by changing the behaviour.

The desire to reduce dissonance depends on three things:

  • Importance of the elements creating it. Trivial inconsistencies are simply tolerated.
  • Degree of influence the person believes they have over it. If the behaviour was required rather than chosen, dissonance is lower.
  • Rewards involved. High rewards reduce the felt tension, because the reward provides its own justification.
Example

A salesperson believes honesty matters, but her job requires her to oversell a product's warranty terms. That is dissonance. She can resolve it three ways: stop doing it, which risks her target; quit, which is costly; or change the attitude: "everyone in this market does it, customers should read the terms, and my job is to sell, not to advise."

The third route is the cheapest, which is why it is the one most people take. Note what has happened. Her behaviour did not adjust to her values. Her values adjusted to her behaviour. This is the mechanism behind most gradual ethical drift in organisations, and it is the honest answer to why ordinary people participate in misconduct.

Does an attitude actually predict behaviour?

Early research said barely. Later research found the link is strong, but only when certain moderating variables are present. Naming these is a high-mark point.

01
Importance

Attitudes that reflect fundamental values or self-interest predict behaviour strongly.

02
Correspondence to behaviour

Specific attitudes predict specific behaviour. General attitudes predict general behaviour. "I dislike my supervisor" predicts behaviour towards him better than "I dislike this company" does.

03
Accessibility

Attitudes we talk about often are easily recalled, and so more likely to guide action.

04
Social pressures

Strong pressure to conform can override the private attitude entirely, which explains employee silence.

05
Direct experience

An attitude formed from personal experience predicts behaviour far better than one absorbed second-hand.

The three components of organisational commitment

Meyer and Allen's model, which is very examinable because the three reasons for staying have completely different consequences.

Example

Three employees have each been at the firm nine years. The first stays because she believes in what the firm does. The second stays because his gratuity vests next year and no competitor will match his inflated salary. The third stays because the firm supported her through an illness and leaving now would feel like ingratitude.

On an HR dashboard all three appear as "tenure: 9 years, retained". Only one of them is doing her best work.

2.17Job Involvement

Definition

Job involvement is the degree to which a person identifies psychologically with their job and considers their perceived performance level important to their self-worth.

The second half of that definition is the part students drop, and it is the part that matters. A highly job-involved employee does not merely work hard. Their sense of who they are is tied to how well they do the job. That is what produces the effort, and also what produces the damage when the job goes badly.

The distinction that gets asked

Job satisfactionJob involvementOrganisational commitment
Attached toThe job's characteristicsThe job itself, as part of identityThe organisation
The statement"I like my job""My job is who I am""I belong to this company"
Can you have one without the other?Yes. A surgeon can be deeply involved in her work and dissatisfied with her hospital's conditionsYes. Someone can love the work and dislike the employer, and leave for a competitor
Example

Two teachers. The first describes herself as "a teacher" and takes a poor class result personally, sitting up to redesign the lesson. The second describes herself as "working at a school" and treats a poor result as information about the students. The first has high job involvement. She will out-work the second, and she is also the one at real risk of burnout, because a professional setback lands as a personal failure. High job involvement is an asset with a health cost attached, and Part C picks that up.

Outcomes of high job involvement

  • Higher OCB and job performance.
  • Lower absenteeism and lower resignation rates.
  • Greater persistence on difficult tasks.
  • The risk: stronger emotional reaction to failure, criticism and job loss, and greater vulnerability to burnout and workaholism.

Psychological empowerment, which is taught alongside

Closely related to job involvement, and it has four components worth naming:

01
Meaning

The work matters and fits the employee's own values.

02
Competence

Belief in one's own capability to do the work well, which is self-efficacy.

03
Self-determination

A sense of choice and autonomy over how the work is done.

04
Impact

Belief that one's work influences outcomes that matter.

A manager wanting higher involvement works on these four. Notice that none of them is pay. This is the practical bridge to the motivation theories in Part C.

2.18Job Satisfaction: Causes and Effects

Definition

Job satisfaction is a positive feeling about a job, resulting from an evaluation of its characteristics.

How it is measured

Method 1
Single global rating
  • One question: "All things considered, how satisfied are you with your job?" on a 1 to 5 or 1 to 7 scale.
  • Fast, cheap, and surprisingly valid.
  • Tells you there is a problem, but not where.
Method 2
Summation of job facets
  • Rates specific facets separately: the work itself, supervision, pay, promotion opportunities, relations with co-workers.
  • Slower and more expensive.
  • Diagnostic: tells you which facet to fix.

Causes of job satisfaction

The ranking here is the examinable part, because the common assumption is wrong.

The pay finding · state it carefully

Pay correlates with satisfaction only up to the level of a comfortable living standard. Beyond that, increases add very little. This is not an argument for underpaying anyone. Pay below a fair level is a powerful dissatisfier, which Herzberg explains in Part C. The correct conclusion is that money buys the removal of dissatisfaction, and does not buy satisfaction, so a manager whose only lever is a raise has almost no lever at all.

Effects of job satisfaction: the positive side

OutcomeThe relationship
Job performanceSatisfied workers are more productive, and the relationship is stronger for complex and professional jobs than for routine ones. It also runs both ways: good performance produces satisfaction, not only the reverse
Organisational Citizenship BehaviourSatisfied employees are more likely to help colleagues and go beyond the job description. Perceived fairness matters more here than satisfaction alone
Customer satisfactionSatisfied employees produce satisfied, more loyal customers. Dissatisfaction is transmitted directly to the customer in service roles
Life satisfactionThe two are strongly and reciprocally related. Work satisfaction spills into life, and life satisfaction spills into work

Effects of dissatisfaction: the EVLN framework

Employees respond to dissatisfaction in four ways, and the model is built on two axes: constructive against destructive, and active against passive.

The managerial insight from EVLN

Managers fear exit because it is visible and measurable. The genuinely costly response is neglect, because the employee stays on the payroll, the attrition number looks healthy, and the performance loss is spread invisibly across months.

Voice is the only response that gives the organisation information it can act on. So the practical goal is not to eliminate dissatisfaction, which is impossible, but to make voice the easiest and safest of the four routes. That is precisely the psychological safety argument from Unit 1, arriving from a different direction.

Other consequences of dissatisfaction

  • Absenteeism: a consistent negative relationship with satisfaction, though moderated by whether absence is easy to take.
  • Turnover: a stronger relationship than absence, moderated heavily by the availability of alternative jobs. Satisfaction predicts turnover best for high performers, because the organisation does less to retain low performers and low performers have fewer options.
  • Counterproductive Work Behaviour: theft, substance use at work, undue socialising, gossip, sabotage, lateness.
A point worth making about CWB

These responses substitute for one another. An employee blocked from quitting, because the market is weak or there is a bond, will express the same dissatisfaction some other way: lateness, reduced effort or gossip. So controlling the symptom, for example by tightening attendance rules, simply relocates the behaviour. Only addressing the dissatisfaction removes it. This is a strong closing line for a ten-mark answer on job satisfaction.

Case study practice · Section C style

Meghna, a senior associate at a listed mid-cap manufacturer, discovers that the plant has been recording certain repair expenses as capital work-in-progress, which overstates profit. She raises it with her reporting manager, who says it was "cleared at the top" and advises her to let it go. The company has a whistle-blower policy on its website, and complaints go to the HR head, who reports to the CFO. Meghna is thirty-one, the sole earner for her parents, and has two years left on an education loan.

Over the next four months she stops volunteering for extra work, starts leaving on time, declines a stretch project, and quietly registers on a job portal. Her appraisal rating slips from 4 to 3, and her manager notes "reduced initiative, attitude concerns".

(a) Analyse Meghna's situation using the concepts of values, attitudes and job satisfaction. (b) Why is the company's whistle-blower policy ineffective here? (c) What should the company change?

Answer skeleton for the case

(a) The analysis. A value conflict within one person: the instrumental value honest and courageous against the terminal value family security, with her value system ranking deciding the outcome. Her attitude maps cleanly onto the ABC model: cognitive ("the accounts are misstated and the firm knows"), affective (resentment, loss of trust), behavioural (disengagement and job search). Cognitive dissonance is present, and she is resolving it not by changing behaviour but by withdrawing, which protects her self-image without confronting the cost. On EVLN, she attempted voice once, it was shut down, and she has moved to neglect and the early stage of exit. Her affective commitment has collapsed while continuance commitment holds her in place, which is the loan and her parents, so she stays and underperforms. Falling job satisfaction is driven by organisational injustice rather than by pay. Note the managerial misreading: the appraisal has labelled a situational and ethical response as a personality defect, which is the fundamental attribution error from Unit 3.

(b) Why the policy fails. The reporting line is fatally designed: complaints reach the HR head, who reports to the CFO, and the CFO is implicated by "cleared at the top". There is no independent receipt by the audit committee, which Section 177(9) and (10) of the Companies Act 2013 and Regulation 22 of SEBI LODR both contemplate. No anonymity, no feedback to the complainant, and no visible instance of anyone being protected. The first attempt at voice was met with "let it go", which is the single most effective teaching event in the whole case, and not only for her.

(c) What to change. Route the mechanism to the audit committee chairperson directly, with genuine anonymity and an external ombudsman option. Give reporters feedback on outcomes. Publicise one case where a reporter was protected, because policy text changes nothing and a precedent changes everything. Separately, the accounting treatment itself needs referral to the audit committee and the statutory auditors, which is a governance obligation and not a personnel matter. On the people side, reopen her appraisal, since the rating reflects a suppressed ethical concern rather than a performance decline, and train managers to treat an attempt at voice as information rather than as an attitude problem.
