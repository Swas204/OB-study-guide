Q1 · 5 marks

CO5 · L4 · Shared leadership

A Pune hospital is moving its patient records to a new digital system. The project team has a doctor, a nurse supervisor, an IT engineer, a billing executive and a project manager. The hospital director asks the project manager to "take every decision yourself so there is one point of control". Analyse why shared leadership would suit this project better, and explain how the project manager's role should change.

Shared leadership means leadership is distributed across the team, not owned by one person. Each member leads where they are strongest.

  • No single person knows enough: the project needs clinical, nursing, technical and billing knowledge. One manager deciding everything will make poorly informed calls.
  • Leadership moves with the task: the doctor should lead on how clinical notes are recorded, the IT engineer on system set-up, the billing executive on payment screens. Each steps back when the work moves to someone else's area.
  • Better decisions: because whoever knows most leads each part, errors that a generalist would miss are caught early.
  • Ownership and commitment: members who lead a part of the work feel responsible for it, so they push it through instead of waiting for instructions.
  • The manager's new role: the project manager does not disappear. The role shifts from deciding everything to coordinating: setting timelines, linking the parts and resolving clashes between them.
Marking scheme

Meaning of shared leadership 1 mark. Any three reasons applied to the hospital 3 marks. Change in the manager's role 1 mark.

Q2 · 5 marks

CO5 · L4 · Transactional leadership

The operations head of a Gurugram call centre runs the floor on a strict system: daily call targets, a cash incentive for every agent who beats the target and a written warning for anyone who misses it three days in a row. Analyse two strengths and three limitations of this leadership approach.

This is transactional leadership, which leads through an exchange: clear tasks, rewards for hitting targets and correction when targets are missed.

Strengths

  • Clarity: every agent knows exactly what is expected and what follows, so there is little confusion on a high-volume floor.
  • Keeps the system running: routine, measurable work like call handling is well suited to targets and incentives, so output stays steady.

Limitations

  • People do only what the deal pays for: agents chase call counts and may rush customers, because quality is not part of the exchange.
  • No change or ideas: the approach maintains the system. Nobody is inspired to suggest a better script or process.
  • Commitment lasts only as long as the reward: if a rival call centre pays a bigger incentive, agents have no deeper reason to stay.
Marking scheme

Identifying transactional leadership with its meaning 1 mark. Two strengths 2 marks. Three limitations 2 marks (any relevant three, applied to the call centre).

Q3 · 5 marks

CO5 · L3 · Authentic leadership

A regional sales head at a consumer electronics company learns that her division will miss its annual target. Her instinct is to keep the numbers hidden until the year-end review "so that the team stays motivated". Applying the qualities of authentic leadership, explain how she should handle the situation and what she gains by doing so.

Authentic leadership means leading by being genuine. It rests on three qualities that lead to one outcome, trust.

  • Self-aware: she should first be honest with herself about why the target is slipping, including any part her own planning played.
  • Values-driven: if she values honesty, she should act on it even though hiding the numbers is more convenient in the short run.
  • Transparent: she should share the shortfall with the team now and explain what she thinks went wrong and what will change. There should be no gap between what she knows and what she says.
  • Outcome, trust: when her words match the facts, the team believes her. People will bring bad news to her early in future instead of hiding it.
  • Practical gain: with the numbers out in the open, the team has time to act on the gap now. Hiding it until year-end removes that chance.
Marking scheme

Three qualities applied to the case 3 marks (1 each). Trust as the outcome 1 mark. Practical benefit 1 mark.

Q4 · 5 marks

CO5 · L4 · Ethical vs responsible leadership

Two managing directors run cement plants in Rajasthan. Mr Sharma has never accepted a bribe, treats every supplier fairly and expects the same honesty from his staff. Ms Rao holds monthly meetings with nearby villages about dust and water use, and reports the plant's effect on workers and the environment alongside profit. Differentiate the leadership shown by each, and explain why a plant needs both.

  • Mr Sharma, ethical leadership: he leads by personal moral principle, with fairness, honesty and integrity. The focus is on his own conduct.
  • Role-modelling: by refusing bribes and treating suppliers fairly, he shows staff what right conduct looks like, so they copy it.
  • Ms Rao, responsible leadership: she widens accountability beyond shareholders to employees, society and the environment. The focus is on whom she answers to.
  • Owning the full impact: by discussing dust and water with villagers and reporting them next to profit, she owns the effects of her plant's decisions on everyone they touch.
  • Why both are needed: an honest leader can still ignore the village's water problem, and a leader who reports to the community can still cut corners privately. Ethical leadership keeps conduct clean, and responsible leadership makes sure the whole impact is counted.
Marking scheme

Ethical leadership identified and explained 2 marks. Responsible leadership identified and explained 2 marks. Why both are needed 1 mark.

Q5 · 5 marks

CO5 · L4 · Ethical decision-making: watch and guard

A junior accountant at a listed infrastructure firm is asked to backdate invoices before the quarter closes. Her colleagues say "everyone does it here", her manager says "just do it, I will take responsibility", and the CEO, celebrated in the press for ten straight quarters of growth, dismisses audit concerns as "paperwork". Identify the pressures pushing towards a bad decision and outline the guard that answers each one.

Good people make bad calls under three pressures. An organisation guards against each with a matching mechanism.

  • Peer pressure: "everyone does it here" makes backdating feel normal.
  • Guard, a clear code of conduct: a written standard that bans backdating gives her a rule to point to, so peer habit is not her only guide.
  • Obedience to authority: the manager's "I will take responsibility" invites her to do wrong and assume the blame is his.
  • Guard, safe whistleblower channels: a confidential, protected channel lets her refuse the order and report it without fear for her job.
  • Hubris: the CEO's run of success has made him believe the rules do not apply to him.
  • Guard, an independent board or audit committee: independent oversight checks even the top leader and limits hubris.
Marking scheme

Three pressures identified from the case 1.5 marks. Three matching guards 3 marks (1 each). Pairing each guard to its pressure 0.5 mark.

Q6 · 5 marks

CO5 · L4 · Leadership for sustainability

The managing director of a Tiruppur garment exporter must choose between two dyeing units. Unit A is cheaper but discharges untreated water and runs on long, unpaid overtime. Unit B costs 12 per cent more but treats its water and pays fair wages. Analyse the decision using the triple bottom line and explain what leadership for sustainability would require of her.

Leadership for sustainability embeds long-term environmental and social goals into the vision. Success is judged on the triple bottom line: people, planet and profit.

  • People: Unit A's unpaid overtime harms workers and exposes the exporter to labour complaints. Unit B treats workers fairly.
  • Planet: Unit A pollutes local water, which harms the community and the region the business depends on. Unit B protects it.
  • Profit: Unit A looks cheaper this year. Over time, pollution penalties, lost buyers and worker turnover can cost more than the 12 per cent saving.
  • Judgement: a profit-only view picks A and wins this year. Weighing all three points to B, which protects the business in the long run.
  • What it requires of her: she should make clean water and fair wages part of the firm's goals, judge suppliers on all three lines, and report them next to profit, so the choice is part of the vision and not a one-off favour.
Marking scheme

Meaning with triple bottom line 1 mark. People, planet and profit applied 3 marks. What the leader must do 1 mark.

Q7 · 5 marks

CO5 · L4 · Coercive and reward power

A shift supervisor at a Chennai auto-parts plant gets work done mainly by threatening to cut overtime and file warnings. Output is acceptable, but workers do the bare minimum and quality slips whenever he is away. Analyse why his approach produces this result and recommend other sources of power he should build.

Power is the capacity to influence. The supervisor relies on coercive power, the ability to punish or threaten.

  • Obedience, not commitment: coercive power gets people to comply out of fear. They do what avoids the penalty and nothing more, which explains the bare minimum.
  • It works only under watch: fear depends on the chance of being caught, so quality slips the moment he leaves the floor.
  • Reward power: he could recognise good shifts with praise, preferred rosters or recommending staff for incentives, so people comply to gain something.
  • Expert power: if he helps fix machine problems and teaches better methods, workers follow him because he knows the work.
  • Referent power: treating workers fairly and with respect makes them like and admire him, so they want his approval even when he is away.
Marking scheme

Identifying coercive power with meaning 1 mark. Two reasons for the result 2 marks. Any two alternative sources with application 2 marks.

Q8 · 5 marks

CO5 · L4 · Power vs politics

Anil, a marketing manager, uses his approved budget to fund the campaigns in the annual plan. His colleague Kavita spends weeks lobbying senior leaders in private to move money into her own pet project, which the plan never included. Differentiate power from politics using the two managers as examples.

  • Power: power is the capacity to influence. Both managers have it, through their positions and their access to budgets.
  • Politics: politics is what people do to get and use power. More precisely, organisational politics is the use of power to get outcomes not sanctioned by the organisation.
  • Anil, power used for sanctioned goals: he uses budget authority for campaigns the organisation approved. That is management, not politics.
  • Kavita, politics: she uses influence to win an outcome the plan never approved, through private lobbying. That is political behaviour.
  • The test: the difference lies in the outcome sought, not in having power. The same budget authority is ordinary management when used for approved goals and politics when used for unapproved ones.
Marking scheme

Meaning of power 1 mark. Meaning of politics with "not sanctioned" 1 mark. Each manager correctly classified with reason 2 marks. The distinguishing test 1 mark.

Q9 · 5 marks

CO5 · L4 · Causes of organisational politics

A public sector bank has 40 branch-manager vacancies and 300 eligible officers. The promotion criteria have not been published, and officers believe the previous round was decided by contacts. Politics in the bank has surged. Analyse the conditions driving this politics and suggest two management steps to reduce it.

Organisational politics is the use of power to get outcomes not sanctioned by the organisation. It rises under three conditions, all present here.

  • Scarce resources: 40 posts for 300 officers means most will lose, so officers fight for their share.
  • Unclear goals: with no published criteria, nobody knows what counts, so each officer argues that their own work matters most.
  • Low trust: officers believe the last round was decided by contacts, so they play games first to protect themselves.
  • Step 1, set clear goals and rules: publish the promotion criteria and weights so there is less room to argue.
  • Step 2, allocate openly and fairly: share the selection process and results with reasons, so officers do not need to fight in private.
Marking scheme

Three conditions applied to the bank 3 marks. Two management steps, each linked to a cause 2 marks.

Q10 · 5 marks

CO5 · L3 · Political tactics

In a consulting firm, one associate presents a team's analysis to the partner as his own. Another quietly gets three seniors to back her proposal before the meeting so it looks like everyone agrees. A third holds back client feedback from colleagues so only he can answer the partner's questions. Identify the political tactic in each case and suggest how the partner could counter it.

  • Impression management: the first associate shapes how the partner sees him by taking visible credit for the team's work.
  • Counter: ask teams to present together and name who did what, so credit is visible.
  • Coalition building: the second associate lines up allies so her position looks like the majority view.
  • Counter: judge proposals on evidence in an open discussion, and ask for dissenting views before deciding.
  • Controlling information: the third associate filters what others know, because whoever controls the facts controls the decision.
  • Counter: communicate with transparency by sharing client feedback with the whole team through a common channel.
Marking scheme

Three tactics correctly named and explained 3 marks. Counters 2 marks (any relevant two or three).

Q11 · 5 marks

CO5 · L3 · Sources of conflict (Stage 1)

A store manager at a supermarket chain faces these tensions: head office emails reach her a day late, the regional office wants lower staff costs while customers want more billing counters open, her deputy is impatient while she is cautious, two departments share one storeroom with no clear owner, and she and the deputy disagree on whether staff should get festival bonuses. Classify each tension under the sources of potential opposition.

Stage 1 of the conflict process, potential opposition, has three sources: communication, structure and personal variables.

  • Late head-office emails, communication: poor or delayed information causes misunderstanding.
  • Regional office vs customers, structure (role conflict): she faces clashing expectations from two masters. The clash comes from how her role is set up.
  • Shared storeroom with no owner, structure: unclear responsibilities are a structural source.
  • Impatient deputy vs cautious manager, personal variables: a difference in personality.
  • Disagreement on festival bonuses, personal variables: a difference in values and attitudes.

At this stage nobody has clashed yet. Spotting the source early tells the manager where to act: fix the email chain, clarify storeroom ownership and talk through the role conflict with the regional office before tension builds.

Marking scheme

Five tensions correctly classified with a reason, 1 mark each.

Q12 · 5 marks

CO5 · L5 · Functional vs dysfunctional conflict

At a Mumbai advertising agency, the copy team and the design team argue hard over every campaign. Last year the arguments produced the agency's two award-winning campaigns. This year the same arguments have turned into personal insults, missed deadlines and two resignations. Evaluate whether the conflict is functional or dysfunctional, and explain what changed.

Conflict can end well or badly. Stage 5 of the conflict process separates functional from dysfunctional outcomes.

  • Last year, functional: the arguments lifted the quality of ideas and produced award-winning work. This is what functional conflict does.
  • Surfacing problems early: the clash between copy and design exposed weak ideas before they reached the client.
  • This year, dysfunctional: insults, missed deadlines and resignations show the conflict now drains energy and trust. The teams spend their effort fighting instead of working.
  • What changed: the conflict has become personal. In the language of Stage 2 it has been personalised, so the disagreement is about people, not ideas.
  • Judgement: the same conflict can be either, depending on whether it stays on the work. The manager should keep debate on ideas and stop personal attacks early.
Marking scheme

Functional conflict identified with features 2 marks. Dysfunctional conflict identified with features 2 marks. Explanation of the shift 1 mark.

Q13 · 5 marks

CO5 · L3 · Conflict handling styles

Recommend the most suitable conflict handling style for each situation and justify it. (i) A worker refuses to wear a helmet on a construction site. (ii) Two colleagues argue about which brand of coffee the pantry should stock. (iii) Two department heads disagree on a new product launch that will shape the firm's next five years. (iv) A client wants a minor change in report format that matters a lot to them and little to you. (v) Two teams need a quick, acceptable split of meeting-room slots before tomorrow.

Handling styles sit on two dimensions, assertiveness and cooperativeness. There is no best style, only the right one for the situation.

  • (i) Competing: a safety rule needs a quick, firm decision. Push to win the point.
  • (ii) Avoiding: the issue is trivial, so step back and let it cool.
  • (iii) Collaborating: the issue is too important to settle by giving in, so dig into the real problem until both sides fully win, even if it takes time.
  • (iv) Accommodating: the issue matters far more to the client than to you, so give in and keep the relationship smooth.
  • (v) Compromising: a quick, acceptable settlement matters more than a perfect one, so each side gives a little.
Marking scheme

Each correct style with a justification 1 mark (5 × 1).

Q14 · 5 marks

CO5 · L4 · Functions of culture

A Kochi software services firm grew from 60 to 600 employees in three years. Long-time staff say the place "no longer feels like one company". New hires are unsure what is acceptable with clients, teams in different offices follow different rules, people leave for small pay rises, and every manager handles the same situation in a different way. Analyse which functions of organisational culture are weakening in this firm.

Organisational culture is the shared system of values and assumptions that makes one organisation different from another. A healthy culture does five jobs, and each one is failing here.

  • Setting boundaries: new hires do not know what is acceptable with clients. The culture no longer marks the lines between right and wrong behaviour.
  • Giving identity: long-time staff say it "no longer feels like one company". Members have lost the sense of belonging to something distinct.
  • Building commitment beyond self: people leave for small pay rises, which shows they care about their own pay but not about the firm's goals.
  • Adding stability: offices following different rules shows the social glue that holds the firm together has weakened as headcount grew.
  • Guiding behaviour: managers handle the same situation differently, so there is no shared sense of "how we do things here" to guide daily action.

Rapid hiring diluted the culture faster than it could be passed on.

Marking scheme

Any five functions, each named and linked to evidence from the case, 1 mark each. A function named without case evidence earns half a mark.

Q15 · 5 marks

CO5 · L4 · Culture vs climate

A Chennai insurance company has been known for decades for its "customer first" values. This year, bonuses were delayed by four months and the latest staff survey shows morale at a ten-year low. At the board meeting, the CEO says, "Our culture is broken. We need to rebuild it from scratch." Differentiate organisational culture from organisational climate and assess the CEO's conclusion.

Culture is the deep, shared values of an organisation, the why behind behaviour. Climate is the felt atmosphere right now, how it feels to work there today.

  • Depth: culture is the underlying layer of values. Climate is the surface mood that employees feel day to day.
  • Speed of change: culture is built over years and changes slowly. Climate can shift within weeks because of a single event, such as delayed pay.
  • What the survey measured: a morale survey measures climate. Falling morale after delayed bonuses is a climate problem, not proof that the customer-first values have gone.
  • Assessment: the CEO's conclusion is not valid. Nothing suggests staff have stopped valuing customers. Rebuilding the culture from scratch would throw away decades of strength to fix a temporary mood.
  • What to do instead: repair the climate by paying the bonuses, explaining the delay honestly and listening to staff. Protect the culture while doing so.
Marking scheme

Differentiation of culture and climate on two bases 2 marks. Linking the survey to climate 1 mark. Assessment of the CEO's statement 1 mark. Correct remedy 1 mark.

Q16 · 5 marks

CO5 · L3 · Socialisation

Sneha joined Kapoor & Rao, a mid-sized audit firm in Mumbai, after her CA final. She expected glamorous client meetings. In her first month she spent most days ticking vouchers in a client's storeroom and felt let down. By her sixth month she understood why careful groundwork mattered, was proud of the firm's reputation for accuracy, and was training the next batch of articles. Identify the stages of socialisation in Sneha's experience and suggest how the firm could make each stage smoother.

Socialisation is the process by which new employees learn the organisation's culture and adapt to it. It runs in three stages.

  • Prearrival: the expectations Sneha brought before joining, that audit work means glamorous client meetings.
  • Encounter: her first month, when expectations met reality (ticking vouchers in a storeroom) and she felt let down.
  • Metamorphosis: by the sixth month she had taken on the firm's value of accuracy, felt pride in it and was passing it on as an insider.
  • Smoother prearrival and encounter: give a realistic picture of first-year work during hiring, so the encounter is less of a shock.
  • Smoother metamorphosis: pair each new article with a senior who explains why the groundwork matters, so new hires take on the values sooner.
Marking scheme

Three stages correctly identified from the case 3 marks (1 each). Suggestions to smooth the stages 2 marks.

Q17 · 5 marks

CO5 · L4 · Workplace spirituality

At a leadership meeting of a Bengaluru e-commerce firm, the HR head proposes a programme on workplace spirituality. The finance head objects: "We are a business, not a temple. Spirituality means prayer rooms, and that is a private matter." Analyse the finance head's objection and explain how the HR head could raise spirituality at work.

Workplace spirituality means meaning, purpose and community at work. It is not religion.

  • The objection is based on a misunderstanding: the finance head confuses spirituality with religion. Workplace spirituality has nothing to do with faith or prayer rooms. An employee of any religion, or none, can share in it.
  • What it is really about: meaning (work that matters), purpose (serving something beyond pay) and community (feeling connected to colleagues).
  • Design jobs that connect people to real impact: show warehouse and support staff how their work reaches customers, for example a small seller whose business grew on the platform.
  • Give autonomy: let teams decide how to meet their targets, which builds ownership and respect.
  • Align work with shared values: if the firm claims to care about small sellers, its policies towards them should match, so work and purpose do not pull apart.
Marking scheme

Correcting the misunderstanding with the meaning of spirituality 2 marks. Three ways to raise it, applied to the firm, 3 marks.

CO5 · L3 · Forces for change

A Ludhiana bicycle manufacturer is under pressure. Cheaper e-bikes from new brands are taking its customers. Battery and motor technology is changing fast. A slowdown has cut demand in its rural markets. New safety standards for electric cycles are coming into force. Inside the company, the new CEO wants to enter the export market, and most of the factory workforce is now under 30 and expects digital tools. Classify these forces for change as external or internal and explain how each pushes the company to change.

Organisational change means altering how the organisation works: its structure, technology, people or processes. Forces for change come from outside and inside.

  • Economy (external): the slowdown in rural demand forces changes in costs and target markets.

Q19 · 5 marks

CO5 · L4 · Lewin's three steps

A Hyderabad hospital announced that all prescriptions would be digital from 1 April. Doctors and nurses were given a one-hour demo the day before. For two months most prescriptions went through the new system, but by June half the wards were back to handwritten slips, which the pharmacy still accepted. Using Lewin's three-step model, analyse why the change did not last and what management should have done.

Lewin's model says change must pass through three steps: unfreeze, change, refreeze. The hospital rushed the first and skipped the last.

  • Unfreeze was weak: staff were told about the change, not shown why the old way could not continue. Management should have shared data on errors from illegible handwriting, so staff felt the need to change.
  • Change was under-supported: a one-hour demo the day before is not enough. Staff needed proper training and floor support in the first weeks, when uncertainty is highest.
  • Refreeze was missing: the pharmacy still accepted handwritten slips, so the old way stayed available and people slid back.
  • What refreezing needed: stop accepting paper slips, build digital prescriptions into ward audits and routines, and recognise wards that comply.
  • Lesson: a change that is not locked in fades. The two good months proved the system could work.
Marking scheme

Lewin's three steps named 1 mark. Analysis of each step against the case 3 marks. Corrective action, especially for refreeze, 1 mark.

Q20 · 10 marks

CO5 · L4 · Transactional vs transformational leadership

A state road transport corporation has a new managing director. Buses run on time, but ridership is falling and staff morale is low. One board member wants "strict targets and incentives", another wants "a leader who can inspire a turnaround". Compare and contrast transactional and transformational leadership, and recommend how the new MD should combine them.

Leadership is the ability to influence a group toward the achievement of a vision or set of goals. Transactional and transformational leadership influence people in different ways.

BasisTransactionalTransformational
How it influencesThrough an exchange: work for rewardBy inspiring people beyond self-interest
ToolsClear tasks, rewards for targets, correction when missedThe four I's
Effect on the systemMaintains itChanges it
Effort from staffWhat the deal pays forMore than the deal asks
Best suited toRoutine, measurable workTurnarounds and change

Similarities: both aim to get results through people, both need clear goals, and both can sit in the same leader.

The four I's the MD would use:

  • Idealised influence: riding the buses and meeting conductors himself, so staff admire and copy his commitment.
  • Inspirational motivation: a vision such as "the first choice for every daily commuter in the state" that gives drivers and conductors meaning.
  • Intellectual stimulation: asking depot staff to rethink routes and timings that lose riders.
  • Individualised consideration: listening to individual staff about shift and safety problems and helping them grow.

Recommendation: keep transactional basics for what already works. On-time running depends on clear schedules, rewards and correction. Add transformational leadership for what is failing. Falling ridership and low morale need inspiration and new ideas, which an exchange cannot produce.

Conclusion: the two styles are not enemies. Transactional leadership maintains the system and transformational leadership changes it, so the MD needs both.

Marking scheme

Meaning of both styles 2 marks. Contrast on at least four bases 3 marks. Similarities 1 mark. Four I's applied to the MD 2 marks. Recommendation and conclusion 2 marks.

Q21 · 10 marks

CO5 · L5 · Leadership for sustainability

"Leadership for sustainability is just corporate social responsibility with a new name." Critically evaluate this statement with examples from Indian business.

Leadership for sustainability embeds long-term environmental and social goals into the organisation's vision, and judges success on the triple bottom line of people, planet and profit.

Where the statement has some truth

  • Shared concerns: both care about society and the environment, so the activities can look alike, such as tree planting or community health camps.
  • Reporting overlap: both appear in annual reports, so outsiders may see no difference.

Where the statement is wrong

  • Embedded vs separate: the key word is embeds. A CSR project can sit in a separate file while the core business carries on unchanged. Sustainability leadership changes the core business itself.
  • Everyday decisions: a leader who makes water reduction a target for every plant head shapes daily operations. A one-time donation to a village water project does not.
  • Triple bottom line: success is judged on people, planet and profit together, reported side by side. CSR is often judged only by the money spent.
  • Long view: weighing all three lines stops a leader from winning this year and harming the business later, for example by choosing a polluting supplier to save cost.
  • Links to other lenses: it needs the inspiration of transformational leadership to change how people work, and the wider accountability of responsible leadership to employees, society and the environment.

Example: a cement firm that funds a school but keeps a polluting kiln is doing CSR. A firm whose MD links plant heads' bonuses to emissions and water targets, and reports them next to profit, is showing leadership for sustainability.

Conclusion: the statement is only partly valid. The two overlap in concern, but sustainability leadership puts long-term goals inside the vision and daily decisions, while CSR can stay outside them.

Marking scheme

Meaning with triple bottom line 2 marks. Points supporting the statement 2 marks. Points against it, any four 4 marks. Example 1 mark. Balanced conclusion 1 mark.

Q22 · 10 marks

CO5 · L4 · Authentic, ethical and responsible leadership

A digital payments company discovers that a software error exposed the card details of two lakh customers. The CEO can either issue a short legal statement and fix the bug quietly, or face customers, staff and regulators openly. Analyse how authentic, ethical and responsible leadership would each shape the CEO's response.

Leadership is the ability to influence a group toward a vision or goals. In a crisis, three lenses ask what kind of person the leader is and whom the leader answers to.

Authentic leadership: leading by being genuine

  • Self-aware: the CEO accepts that the company's testing failed, instead of blaming a vendor.
  • Transparent: she explains openly what happened, what is known and what is not. No gap between the public statement and the internal facts.
  • Outcome: customers and staff trust her account, which slows panic and rumours.

Ethical leadership: her own conduct

  • Honesty and fairness: she tells affected customers promptly instead of waiting to see if anyone notices.
  • Role-modelling: when the CEO admits the error, engineers learn that reporting mistakes is expected, not punished.
  • Resisting pressure: the legal team may push for silence. Integrity means doing what is right even when silence is safer for the company.

Responsible leadership: whom she answers to

Q24 · 10 marks

CO5 · L5 · Organisational politics

"Office politics is a character flaw. Hire good people and it will disappear." Assess the validity of this statement, explaining what causes organisational politics, the tactics it uses and how management can control it.

Organisational politics is the use of power to get outcomes not sanctioned by the organisation.

Why the statement is weak: politics comes from conditions, not just people

  • Scarce resources: when budgets or promotions are too few to go round, even good people fight for their share.
  • Unclear goals: when nobody knows what counts as success, people argue that their own work matters most.
  • Low trust: when people expect others to play games, they play first to protect themselves.

These conditions are created by the organisation, so hiring "good people" alone will not remove them.

Tactics that appear under these conditions

  • Impression management: taking visible credit for shared work.
  • Coalition building: lining up allies so a position looks like the majority view.
  • Controlling information: filtering facts, because whoever controls the facts controls the decision.

How management controls it (each step removes a cause)

  • Set clear goals and rules, so success is defined.
  • Allocate scarce resources fairly and openly, so nobody needs to fight in private.
  • Communicate with transparency, which removes the advantage of controlling information.
  • Lead by example, because people copy what the leader does.
  • Confront destructive politicking early, before it becomes "how things work".

Where the statement has a point: individuals do choose whether to play, and a leader who rewards political players will attract more of them.

Conclusion: the statement is largely invalid. Politics grows from scarce resources, unclear goals and low trust. Management controls it by fixing those conditions, not by hiring alone.

Q26 · 10 marks

CO5 · L5 · Conflict handling styles

"Collaborating is always the best way to handle conflict." Critically evaluate this statement using the five conflict handling styles, with workplace examples.

Conflict handling styles are placed on two dimensions: assertiveness (how hard you try to satisfy your own concerns) and cooperativeness (how hard you try to satisfy the other party's concerns).

The case for collaborating

  • High on both dimensions: it digs into the real problem so both sides fully win.
  • Best for important issues: for example, two heads disagreeing on a five-year expansion plan should collaborate, because the issue is too important to settle by giving in.

Why "always" is wrong

  • It takes the most time: collaboration is slow, and many conflicts do not deserve that time.
  • Competing fits emergencies: a worker ignoring a safety rule needs a quick, firm decision, not a joint problem-solving session.
  • Compromising fits deadlines: when two teams need a quick, acceptable split of a shared budget by tomorrow, meeting in the middle beats a perfect solution next month.
  • Avoiding fits trivial issues: an argument about pantry snacks, or a heated meeting where tempers need time to cool.
  • Accommodating fits lopsided stakes: when a client cares far more about a report format than you do, giving in keeps the relationship smooth.

The real test: a manager should weigh how important the issue is, how much time there is and how much the relationship matters, then choose the style.

Conclusion: collaborating is the best style for important, complex issues, but not always. There is no best style, only the right one for the situation.

CO5 · L3 · Negotiation process

The workers' union at a two-wheeler plant in Hosur has demanded a 15 per cent wage rise. Management can afford about 8 per cent but wants a change in shift timings. As the HR head, design a negotiation plan through the five steps of the negotiation process, explaining what management should do at each step.

Negotiation is how two or more parties settle a conflict by agreeing terms. The process has five steps, and each sets up the next.

  1. Preparation: management fixes its goals (a settlement near 8 per cent, plus new shift timings), its limit, and what the union really wants. It gathers facts: wage rates at nearby plants, cost of living and the plant's financial position. Most weak deals are lost here.
  2. Ground rules: both sides agree who sits at the table (union leaders, HR head, plant head), where and how often they meet, a deadline before the festival season, and what happens in a deadlock, such as bringing in a mutually trusted mediator.
  3. Clarification: each side explains and justifies its position. The union may reveal that rising rents and school fees drive the 15 per cent demand. Management explains its margins and why shift changes matter for output. This is understanding, not yet bargaining.
  4. Bargaining: each side trades what matters less to it for what matters more. Management could offer a wage rise near 9 per cent plus a transport allowance in return for the new shift timings. The union could accept a smaller rise in exchange for a written review after one year.
  5. Closure: the agreement is written down and signed, shared with workers, and put into action with clear dates for the wage change and the new shifts.

Strategy note: because management and the union will keep working together for years, the plan should lean integrative. Trading wages for shift timings grows the pie, while a pure win-lose fight over the percentage would damage trust.

Conclusion: careful preparation and clarification turn a fight over one number into a trade that both sides can accept.

CO5 · L5 · Behavioural change for sustainability

  • It relied on force: fines and policing created resistance. People complied only when watched, which is the opposite of a habit.
  • Posters and emails did not create norms: people did not see colleagues switching off, so saving energy never became "what people here do".

  • No lasting change: the short-term drop faded because nothing in the daily routine was changed.

  • Clear, visible norms: display each floor's weekly power use on a screen by the lift, so people see what most colleagues do and want to match it.

  • Remove the fines: replace policing with recognition of floors that improve, so people act because it is normal, not because they are watched.

Conclusion: gentle nudges and clear norms change habits quietly and lastingly, while force produces only watched compliance. Over time this builds a green culture.

  1. Step 1, prepare thoroughly: management studies the carmaker's needs for the new model, its quality standards and its culture, and sets Sterling's own goals and limits.
  2. Step 2, build the relationship first: plan several visits, invite the buyer to the Pune plant and show long-term commitment before price is discussed.
  3. Step 3, agree ground rules: settle a meeting schedule, who attends, a realistic timeline and how disagreements will be handled.
  4. Step 4, clarify, then bargain integratively: listen to the buyer's priorities, then offer trades that grow the pie, such as a price commitment in return for volume, or joint engineering support in return for a longer contract. Confirm understanding in writing after each meeting to avoid misreading indirect replies.
  5. Step 5, close formally: write up the agreement in detail, get both sides' sign-off and set clear implementation steps for the first deliveries.

Conclusion: following all five steps, bargaining integratively and respecting the buyer's pace, style and relationship-first approach gives Sterling Gears its best chance.

Marking scheme

(a) Failures in the negotiation process 2.5 marks. Wrong strategy explained 2 marks. Pace, style, deal vs relationship applied 3 marks. (b) Five sequenced steps led by management 7.5 marks (1.5 each).

Q36 · 15 marks

CO5 · L5 · Transactional, transformational, shared leadership and sustainability

GreenVolt, a Coimbatore solar-installation company with 250 staff, was run for eight years by a founder who personally approved every decision. He paid installers per panel fitted, fined teams for delays and often said, "People only work for incentives." Output was steady, but safety shortcuts, waste of materials and complaints from rural customers about poor after-sales service were rising. Engineers stopped suggesting improvements because "the boss decides everything anyway". The founder has retired. The new CEO, Lakshmi, wants GreenVolt to grow into rural markets and be known as a company that cares for its workers, customers and the environment.

(a) Evaluate the founder's leadership style and assess the validity of his statement, "People only work for incentives." [7.5]

(b) Formulate a five-step leadership plan for Lakshmi, drawing on transformational leadership, shared leadership and leadership for sustainability. [7.5]

(a) The founder's leadership style

  • Transactional leadership: he led through an exchange: pay per panel, fines for delays, clear tasks. This kept output steady, which is what transactional leadership does well. It maintains the system.
  • People did only what the deal paid for: installers were paid for panels fitted, not for safety, materials or service, so those slipped.
  • No change or ideas: engineers stopped suggesting improvements because the system did not ask for them.
  • Leadership owned by one person: approving every decision himself was the opposite of shared leadership, so the knowledge of engineers and installers went unused.
  • Narrow bottom line: he judged success on output and profit. Safety shortcuts (people), material waste (planet) and service complaints show the other two lines were ignored.

Validity of "People only work for incentives"

  • Partly valid: incentives do drive the behaviour they pay for. Steady panel counts prove that.
  • Largely invalid: transformational leadership shows people give more than the deal asks when they believe in a purpose. GreenVolt's mission of bringing clean power to villages could inspire that.
  • Evidence in the case: engineers stopped offering ideas because the system ignored them, not because they lacked incentives.
  • Conclusion: incentives buy output. They do not buy ideas, care for safety or concern for the customer.

(b) Five-step plan for Lakshmi

  1. Step 1, set a sustainability vision: embed people, planet and profit into GreenVolt's goals, for example "clean, reliable power for every village we serve, installed safely". Report safety, waste and customer service next to profit.
  2. Step 2, inspire through the four I's: communicate the vision (inspirational motivation), model safe and honest work herself (idealised influence), invite teams to rethink installation methods (intellectual stimulation) and coach individual installers (individualised consideration).
  3. Step 3, share leadership: let engineers lead on design, senior installers on safety and the service team on rural after-sales. Lakshmi's role shifts to coordinating.
  4. Step 4, redesign rewards: keep transactional basics but pay on safe, quality installations and customer satisfaction, not panel count alone, so incentives support the vision.
  5. Step 5, review and recognise: hold monthly reviews of the triple bottom line, celebrate teams that improve safety or cut waste, and act on ideas from the floor so people see their input matters.

Conclusion: the founder's transactional system maintained output. Lakshmi needs transformational, shared and sustainability-led leadership to change GreenVolt and grow it responsibly.

Marking scheme

(a) Evaluation of the founder's style, including transactional features, lack of shared leadership and the triple bottom line 3.5 marks. Validity of the statement 4 marks. (b) Five sequenced steps covering transformational, shared and sustainability leadership 7.5 marks (1.5 each).

Q37 · 15 marks

CO5 · L5 · Organisational culture, climate and socialisation

Sarathi Textiles, a 60-year-old family-owned firm in Coimbatore, acquired Loom Lab, a Bengaluru design start-up of 80 people. Sarathi values loyalty, respect for seniority and careful, step-by-step approval of every decision. Loom Lab values speed, open debate with anyone, and trying ideas quickly. Six months after the merger, Loom Lab designers complain that every idea needs five signatures, while Sarathi managers call the designers "disrespectful". A staff survey shows morale at its lowest ever. New joiners from both sides receive a single welcome email and are sent straight to work, unsure whose rules apply. Eleven of Loom Lab's best designers have resigned. The managing director tells the board, "Culture is soft stuff. This merger is about numbers, and the numbers will fix themselves."

(a) Analyse the problems at Sarathi Textiles using the definition and functions of organisational culture, the difference between culture and climate, and socialisation. Assess the validity of the managing director's statement. [7.5]

(b) As an OB consultant, formulate a five-step plan for the leadership to manage the culture after the merger. [7.5]

(a) Analysis

  • Two cultures, not one: culture is the shared system of values and assumptions that makes one organisation different from another. Sarathi (loyalty, seniority, careful approval) and Loom Lab (speed, open debate, quick experiments) have opposite values, and the merger has not created shared ones.
  • Functions are failing: there is no common identity (each side sees the other as outsiders), no clear boundaries (new joiners do not know whose rules apply), weak commitment beyond self (eleven designers left) and no single culture to guide behaviour or add stability.
  • Climate has collapsed: the survey measures climate, the felt atmosphere right now. Record low morale shows how it feels to work there today. The deeper cause is the clash of cultures underneath.
  • Socialisation has broken down: a single welcome email gives no real encounter stage. New joiners never learn the values, so they cannot reach metamorphosis and become insiders.

Validity of "Culture is soft stuff, the numbers will fix themselves"

  • Invalid in the short term too: the resignation of eleven top designers is already a hard cost in lost talent and delayed products.
  • Invalid in the long term: Loom Lab was acquired for its speed and ideas. If its culture is crushed, the value the numbers depend on disappears.
  • Climate affects output: low morale lowers effort and raises exits, which shows up in the numbers.
  • Conclusion: culture is what produces the numbers. Ignoring it puts the merger itself at risk.

(b) Five-step plan

  1. Step 1, leadership acknowledges the clash: the MD and both leadership teams openly accept that the cultures differ, and stop the public name-calling. This is the top management example the culture will follow.
  2. Step 2, agree shared values: joint workshops decide which values to keep from each side, for example Sarathi's respect and reliability with Loom Lab's speed and open debate, written down as the merged firm's values.
  3. Step 3, align structure with those values: cut the approval chain for design decisions to one or two signatures, while keeping careful checks for financial decisions.
  4. Step 4, rebuild socialisation: replace the welcome email with a proper induction covering realistic expectations, mixed-team projects and a buddy from the other side, so new joiners move through encounter to metamorphosis.
  5. Step 5, select for fit and track the climate: hire people who fit the merged values, and run quarterly pulse surveys to check whether morale and retention recover, adjusting the plan if they do not.

Conclusion: the merger will deliver its numbers only once the two groups share one culture and the climate recovers.

Marking scheme

(a) Definition and two clashing cultures 1.5 marks. Failing functions 1.5 marks. Culture vs climate 1 mark. Socialisation breakdown 1 mark. Validity of the MD's statement 2.5 marks. (b) Five sequenced steps led by management 7.5 marks (1.5 each). Steps out of sequence or not led by management lose marks.

Q38 · 15 marks

CO5 · L5 · Forces for change, resistance, Lewin, behavioural change

A state electricity distribution company in Madhya Pradesh must install smart meters and move to digital billing within two years. The regulator has set a deadline, rival private distributors offer app-based billing, and the state wants line losses and paper use cut as part of its sustainability goals. Linemen fear the meters will end their meter-reading jobs. Billing clerks, whose knowledge of manual ledgers made them essential, say the software "will never work here". Section officers fear losing control over local billing disputes. Three years ago a similar project failed after the then MD issued a circular and transferred staff who objected. The new MD has proposed the same approach: "Issue the order, transfer anyone who refuses, and the change will happen."

(a) Identify the forces for change and the individual and organisational sources of resistance in this case. Evaluate the new MD's proposed approach. [7.5]

(b) Formulate a five-step change plan for the company using Lewin's three-step model and suitable ways of overcoming resistance, including how to build lasting sustainable behaviour among staff. [7.5]

(a) Forces and resistance

  • External forces: regulation (the regulator's deadline), competition (private distributors with app billing), technology (smart meters and digital billing).
  • Internal forces: strategy (the company's own goal of cutting line losses) and the sustainability target on paper use.
  • Individual resistance: economic fear and security (linemen fear losing their jobs), fear of the unknown (nobody knows how the new roles will work) and habit (years of manual routines).
  • Organisational resistance: threat to expertise (clerks' ledger knowledge loses value), threat to power (section officers lose control of disputes) and structural inertia (procedures built around manual billing).

Evaluation of the MD's approach

  • It relies only on coercion: coercion is the last of the six tactics, fast but damaging to trust.
  • It has already failed here: the same approach collapsed three years ago, so staff will expect a repeat and resist harder.
  • It ignores the sources: force does nothing about job fear, lost expertise or lost power. It drives resistance underground.
  • It skips unfreeze and refreeze: an order does not create a felt need for change or lock the new way in.
  • Verdict: the approach is likely to fail again. Coercion should be kept only for staff who still refuse after support has been offered.

(b) Five-step change plan

  1. Step 1, unfreeze through education: share data on line losses, billing errors and the regulator's deadline in meetings at every division, so staff see why the old way cannot continue.
  2. Step 2, negotiate on jobs: commit that no lineman will be retrenched and offer retraining as meter technicians or field service staff, which removes the economic fear.
  3. Step 3, involve and co-opt key groups: make experienced clerks part of the software testing team (participation) and give section officers a formal role in the new digital dispute process (cooptation).
  4. Step 4, change with facilitation: roll out division by division with hands-on training and a help desk, supporting staff when uncertainty is highest.
  5. Step 5, refreeze and build green habits: stop paper bills and ledgers, build digital billing into performance reviews, make paperless work the default in offices (a nudge) and share each division's paper and loss savings monthly, so the new behaviour becomes the norm.

Conclusion: matching tactics to the sources of resistance, and following Lewin's three steps in order, gives the change a far better chance than an order and a transfer list.

Marking scheme

(a) Forces for change, external and internal 1.5 marks. Individual sources 1.5 marks. Organisational sources 1.5 marks. Evaluation of the MD's approach 3 marks. (b) Five sequenced steps led by management, covering Lewin's steps, matched tactics and sustainable behaviour, 7.5 marks (1.5 each).
